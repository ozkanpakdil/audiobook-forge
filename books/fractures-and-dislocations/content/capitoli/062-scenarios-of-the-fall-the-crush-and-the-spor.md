# Chapter 62 — Scenarios of the Fall, the Crush and the Sporting Injury

This chapter is built from four constructed illustrations, and none of them is the record of a real patient. A fall from a roof, a crush in an industrial accident, a twisted knee on a field and a dislocated shoulder in a match are situations a listener may meet. Each turns on a decision rather than a diagnosis, and the same decision made early or late produces two very different patients. Every chapter of this course follows one sequence, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. What follows is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The fall from a roof begins before anyone touches the patient, with the scene itself. The height of the fall and the surface that ended it tell the listener how much energy the body absorbed. A fall of 4 meters onto a hard surface is taught as high energy, and that figure is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The lay first aider secures the scene and moves the patient only for an immediate hazard such as fire or traffic, while the trained responder works in personal protective equipment within the procedures the service authorizes.

The mechanism is followed by the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. The principle that orders everything is that life comes before limb, because a limb can wait for minutes that a brain and a heart cannot. The spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, and no lone manual handling technique is safe. The lay first aider keeps the head still and waits, while the trained responder may apply a rigid collar within the procedures the service authorizes.

Only when the patient is stable does the assessment turn to the limb, which is assessed by looking, by feeling and by asking, never by forcing. The responder looks for a wound, a deformity and a change in color, and compares the limb with the other side. A thigh that is swollen, shortened and rotated outward is a fracture of the thigh bone until proved otherwise, and it may bleed enough to threaten the circulation. Feeling is done gently over the bones rather than over the wound, and asking the patient to move the fingers and toes is a request, not a command.

Stabilization in the field is a short list of acts whose most important member is restraint. A deformed limb is splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, because vessels and nerves lie against the bone. Pulling a limb straight can tear what remained intact, can convert a closed fracture into an open one, and can release the pressure holding a torn vessel closed. The lay first aider supports the limb with the hands, with a pillow or with whatever is at hand, keeps the patient warm and waits. The trained responder may apply a splint and control the bleeding that can be controlled, within the procedures the service authorizes.

The neurovascular examination is never skipped and never assumed, and it is performed before the limb is splinted and again afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and warmth of the skin, feels the pulse beyond the injury and asks the patient to move the fingers and toes. Sensation is tested on both sides, and numbness or tingling is recorded in the words of the patient. The result is written before the splint and after it, because a finding not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. The lay first aider reports color, warmth and movement, while the formal examination and its interpretation belong to the clinician.

Immobilization prevents movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, because a bone splinted alone can still move at either end. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, applied over the position found, and secured firmly enough to hold without stopping the circulation. A device that pulls gently on a fractured thigh is used by trained responders, but its technique belongs to supervised training. The trained responder applies splints within the equipment the service authorizes, while the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually first, taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, and it includes the joint above and the joint below. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break around a joint, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues. Either study is requested when the answer will change what is done, and that decision belongs to the clinician responsible for the patient. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, the trained responder does not choose the study, and an image is a picture of one moment.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the splint is checked for tightness. Pain that rises instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling and a limb that becomes tense and swollen stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for confirmation and acts within the local protocol, which may allow a constricting splint to be released by someone authorized to do it. The limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the referral is urgent, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and some injuries cannot be managed where the patient lies. A patient with an open fracture, a suspected compartment syndrome, a dislocation, a damaged nerve or vessel, a fracture entering a joint, or a fracture in a child that crosses the growing part of the bone is referred to a facility with imaging and an operating room. The lay first aider calls for help and stays with the patient, and the decision about definitive treatment belongs to the physician. Definitive treatment includes reduction, which restores the position of the bone ends, traction, which holds them in line with a gentle pull, and casting or surgical fixation, which holds the position while the bone heals. Every one of those acts is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.

The same fall, told too late, is a story about hands that were too helpful. A responder took the shortened, rotated thigh in both hands and pulled it straight because the leg looked wrong. The artery that had been compressed against the broken bone rather than torn began to bleed inside a thigh that swelled hard and fast, and the closed fracture had become an open one through the skin. The neurovascular examination had not been recorded before the pull, so the numbness that followed could not be attributed to the injury or to the hand. A compartment syndrome declared itself during the night, and by the time the operating room was ready the muscle had died.

The crush injury of an industrial accident is the scenario in which the environment itself is the first patient. A worker is pinned by a fallen load or a machine part, a limb is compressed, and the space is loud, dusty and unsafe. Power is isolated and locked out by the people authorized to do it, and no responder enters a space that has not been declared safe. The lay first aider stays outside, keeps the patient talking and sends for help, while the trained responder works in personal protective equipment. The release of a compressing object is a planned act involving the rescuers and a physician, and it is not the work of a single responder.

Crush injury and crush syndrome are not the same thing, and that difference is the reason the release is planned rather than improvised. Crush injury is the local damage that pressure does to muscle, nerve and skin at the point of compression. Crush syndrome is what happens to the whole patient when the damaged muscle is reperfused, because substances held inside the injured cells enter the circulation and can damage the heart and the kidneys. A patient pinned for hours may therefore look surprisingly well while still trapped and then deteriorate within minutes of release. The duration of compression and the bulk of muscle involved drive the plan, and any interval named in it is an illustration to be confirmed against the local protocol.

For the trapped patient the responder's work is monitoring, warmth and the honest passing of information. The patient is kept warm and insulated from the ground, the load and the machine, and bleeding that can be controlled is controlled without disturbing the compressing weight. The neurovascular status of the limb beyond the compression is recorded and repeated, and the findings are spoken to the physician directing the plan. Anything given to that patient is decided by a physician under the local protocol, and the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber. A lay first aider gives nothing unless a clinician has asked for it, and a trained responder gives nothing beyond what the service authorizes.

The same crush, told too late, begins with an unplanned release. A team lifted the load before the receiving hospital had been told that a patient with a crushed limb was coming. The pressure came off, the substances entered the blood, and within minutes the patient became pale, confused and then pulseless. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was started by trained responders, and the hospital later found an injured heart and failing kidneys. The correct version of that story is telephone calls before lifting, a physician directing the preparation, warmth, continuous monitoring and transport to a facility with an intensive care unit.

A crush injury is also a wound, and the rules that govern any wound govern this one. The wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled, the neurovascular status is recorded, the limb is immobilized and the patient is referred urgently. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. Nobody explores such a wound with a gloved finger or an instrument in the field, and definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else. Protection against infection and against tetanus is a decision for the physician and the local protocol.

The twisted knee of sport is the quietest of the four scenarios and the one most often got wrong. A player plants a foot, the knee buckles inward without contact, and the player goes down with a joint that swells within minutes and will not bear weight. Rapid swelling after a twisting injury usually means blood inside the joint, which is called a hemarthrosis, and it says that something within the joint has torn. The decision is that nobody tests the stability of this knee on the field, since pulling on a joint is a clinical skill learned under supervision and can convert a partial tear into a complete one.

Assessment of the swollen knee follows the same order as everywhere in this course, and it begins with the primary survey and the mechanism rather than with the joint. The responder looks at the position of the limb, the swelling, the wound and the color of the skin, feels the bones and the pulse beyond the injury, and asks about the pain rather than pushing on the joint. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging are requested when the soft tissues need to be seen and the answer will change what is done. The lay first aider may support the limb and reassure, while the formal assessment belongs to the trained responder, the nurse or the physician.

Stabilization of the knee means holding it in the position found, keeping the patient warm and keeping weight off that limb until a clinician allows it. A trained responder may apply a splint within the equipment the service authorizes, padding the bony points and checking that the device does not stop the circulation. Cooling is used by many services for a swollen joint, and how it is applied, for how long and by whom is set by the local protocol rather than by this book. Nothing in this step straightens a joint, and nothing in it forces a locked knee to bend.

The neurovascular examination of the injured knee is written before the splint and after it, and it is brief enough to be done on a sideline. A knee that has gone out of place and slipped back by itself is more dangerous than one that stayed out, because the deformity has gone and with it the visible warning. Tingling, numbness, a cold foot, a weak pulse or pain far greater than the injury suggests are the findings that stop the responder and turn a sporting injury into an emergency. The lay first aider notices and reports these findings, and their interpretation belongs to a clinician.

The same twisted knee, told too late, is the story of a player who was sent back on. The knee had become much less painful after a few minutes, which was read as recovery but was the sign of a joint that had reduced itself. The player returned to the game, the knee was never examined neurovascularly and the pulse behind the knee was never recorded. By the evening the foot was pale and cold, and the artery damaged at the time of the injury had clotted, so the limb could not be saved. The right version of that story ends on the sideline with a splint, a warm patient, a written examination and a referral.

The dislocated shoulder of sport is the scenario in which the right decision is the hardest to watch. A player falls on an outstretched arm or is pulled by the arm, the shoulder becomes square instead of round, and the upper arm cannot be brought to the side. The instinct of everyone present is to pull the arm and put the joint back, and the decision that saves the limb is to refuse. The lay first aider supports the arm in the position found and sends for help, while the trained responder records the neurovascular status and applies a support. Reduction is performed by a physician or an orthopedic surgeon trained and authorized to do it, where imaging is available.

Reduction restores the position of the joint surfaces, and it is not a first aid maneuver, however simple the movement may look. It must not be attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment of bone is caught within the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks the training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. Each situation carries its own reason, because a shattered surface cannot be held by pulling on it, a trapped fragment blocks the way back, and a failing vessel needs a vascular surgeon. In each case the attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique.

The correct handling of a dislocated shoulder in the field is therefore almost entirely about what the responder does not do. The arm is supported in the position found, with a sling, a folded garment or the opposite hand of the patient, and the joints above and below are included in the support. The neurovascular examination is repeated after the support is applied and recorded again, with attention to sensation over the outer shoulder and the ability to lift the wrist, because the nerve closest to the joint is the one most often injured. Pain is reported honestly and treated by the physician under the local protocol, and the substance, the amount and the way it is given belong to the prescriber. 

The same shoulder, told too late, is the story of a well-meant pull on a touchline. A bystander took the wrist, braced a foot against the chest of the patient and pulled, and the joint did not return. The pull fractured the upper arm and stretched the nerve that supplies the muscles around the shoulder, and because no examination had been recorded before it, nobody could say whether the weakness came with the injury. In another version the shoulder was reduced successfully in a dressing room and the player was sent home without examination. Both versions share one root, because an act belonging to an authorized clinician was performed by someone untrained.

Across all four scenarios the same habits decide the outcome, and they are habits rather than talents. The first is that the neurovascular examination is done before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is written down. The next is that reassessment follows every intervention, including the application of a splint, the release of a bandage, the movement of a patient and the journey in the vehicle. The third is that the responder stops when there is resistance, because resistance means that the tissues are telling the hand something theory cannot. The last is that the record travels with the patient, since a finding spoken into a radio and never written is a finding lost.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality after it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, and any change in the patient are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story. The lay first aider reports what was seen and when, the trained responder and the paramedic report the observations of the service, and the nurse or the physician adds the findings and the decisions of clinical care. The last sentence of a handover should say what the responder is worried about, because that sentence is often the most useful one.

Cold runs through all four scenarios and is treated in this course as a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort. A patient lying on a roof in the rain, a worker pinned to a concrete floor and a player on a wet bench all lose heat, and a cold patient bleeds more easily. The ground is insulated, wet clothing is replaced with dry covering, and the patient is covered from the head down, with the injured limb included. The lay first aider can do all of this without equipment. A responder who keeps one hand on the patient is doing more than one who looks for something clever to do.

Pain is the finding that ties the four scenarios together, and it is managed by a physician rather than by a book. Pain that rises instead of settling, or that demands more attention than the injury appears to justify, is a finding to report urgently, because it may be the first sign of a compartment syndrome. The responder's part is to ask, to believe the answer, to record it in the words of the patient and to pass it on. Any substance used for pain, any amount of it and any way of giving it are decided by the prescriber under the local protocol, and this course deliberately names none of them.

Open injuries in these scenarios follow rules that do not change with the setting, and they are the rules most often broken by people who want to help. The wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled with pressure around the wound, the neurovascular status is recorded, and the transfer is urgent. An embedded object is left where it lies, exposed bone is never pushed back, and the wound is never explored with a finger or an instrument, because all three acts can start bleeding that cannot be stopped outside an operating room. Cleaning, exploration and definitive closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else. The lay first aider covers and supports, and the trained responder does the same within the procedures the service authorizes.

Injury of the spine appears in two of these scenarios and is treated in all of them as unstable until it has been cleared by someone qualified to clear it. The fall from a roof and the tackle that dislocated a shoulder can both involve the neck without any sign the responder can detect. No lone manual handling technique is safe, and a patient who must be moved is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. The lay first aider keeps the head still and waits, and the trained responder may apply a collar within the procedures the service authorizes. The decision to clear the spine belongs to a clinician with the imaging and the examination to support it, and it is never made in the field by elimination.

Every number in this chapter is an illustration rather than a rule. A height, a duration, an interval, a distance or a threshold is given only to make a mechanism understandable, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is used. Where practice differs, the chapter says that it differs rather than choosing one version as universal. A duration that one service treats as urgent may be ordinary in another, so the local protocol is the only reliable source.

Everything described in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and no chapter of this course teaches a listener to perform them from a book, however clearly the words describe them. A listener who wants to perform them should seek the supervised training that leads to certification, and should treat this chapter as preparation for understanding what that training is for. These acts are judged by feel as much as by sight.

The four scenarios end with a decision about escalation, and that decision is always safer when it is made out loud. A patient who is deteriorating, a limb that is changing, a wound that is open, a joint out of place and a compression lasting hours are each reasons to call for a more capable provider. The lay first aider calls the emergency number and follows the instructions given, and the nurse or the physician calls the specialist service that will receive the patient. The call is not an admission of failure, and it is often the single most useful intervention available, because it turns a lonely scene into a prepared receiving team.

What separates the early version of each story from the late version is not courage and not equipment. It is a refusal to do the dramatic thing, an examination performed and written before any intervention, and a message sent ahead of the patient. The responder who supports a limb in the position found, who records what the hand and the foot can feel and do, and who telephones before acting will deliver the better patient. Every one of these scenarios is a constructed illustration and not the care of a real patient, and the protocol of the place where the listener works, together with the law of that country, decides what may be done there.

To summarize, the fall from a roof, the crush of an industrial accident, the twisted knee of sport and the dislocated shoulder all turn on one sequence, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and on the same restraint. A deformed limb is splinted in the position found and is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, and an open wound is never explored blindly, because definitive closure belongs to the operating room. The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one, and its result is written down. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, or when an earlier attempt has failed. A trapped patient is released once, deliberately, with a physician directing the plan and a receiving team ready. Cold is treated as a complication, the spine is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear it, pain and any substance given for it belong to the prescriber and the local protocol. Stop when there is resistance, reassess after every intervention, and call for a more capable provider early, because the call is treatment. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
