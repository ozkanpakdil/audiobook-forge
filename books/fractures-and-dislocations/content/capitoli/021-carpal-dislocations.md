# Chapter 21 — Carpal Dislocations

The wrist is a place where many small bones meet, and that is the reason an injury there can hide from the person who examines it. A carpal dislocation means that one or more of the small bones of the wrist have moved out of the line they keep with each other and with the forearm, so the hand may lose not only its comfort but its feeling and its grip. It is one of the injuries this course calls subtle, because the wrist may look puffy rather than deformed, because the patient can still move the fingers, and because a radiograph taken from one direction alone may appear almost normal. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and this chapter keeps that order exactly. What follows is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The carpus is the group of small bones between the forearm and the bones of the hand, and there are eight of them, arranged in two rows. The row nearer the forearm holds the scaphoid, the lunate, the triquetrum and the pisiform, while the row nearer the fingers holds the trapezium, the trapezoid, the capitate and the hamate. The lunate sits in the middle of the near row and takes its name from its shape, which is that of a half moon. It is the keystone of the wrist, because the forearm bone called the radius rests on it and the capitate, the largest bone of the far row, sits in a hollow on its far surface, so that the two form a ball resting in a cup. The scaphoid bridges the two rows on the thumb side and swings like a link whenever the wrist bends, and short, strong ligaments with a fibrous capsule hold the whole arrangement together, so it is the ligaments rather than the bones that decide what happens when the wrist is injured.

On the palm side of the wrist the carpal bones form an arch, and a broad band of fibrous tissue stretches across the arch to make a passage. Through that passage run the tendons that bend the fingers and, with them, the median nerve, which gives feeling to the thumb, the index finger, the middle finger and half of the ring finger, and which works the short muscles at the base of the thumb. The passage has no room to spare even in health, so anything that narrows it presses on the nerve. This is why the median nerve appears again and again in injuries of the wrist, and why it is the nerve an examiner asks about first. The ulnar nerve, which gives feeling to the little finger, passes outside the passage on the other side of the wrist, and the radial nerve supplies feeling over the back of the hand, so a carpal dislocation can injure any of the three, although the median nerve is the one most often caught.

The usual mechanism is a fall onto an outstretched hand, with the wrist bent back and the weight of the body arriving through the palm. The force travels along the radius into the lunate and then into the capitate, and the lunate is levered against the bones around it until the ligaments that hold it give way. A low energy fall may stretch or partly tear those ligaments, or break the scaphoid, while a fall from a height or from a moving vehicle may tear them completely and carry the far row of carpal bones off the back of the lunate. The injury is best understood as one spectrum rather than several diseases, because at one end the ligaments are stretched, in the middle the link between the scaphoid and the lunate fails, and at the far end the far row of carpal bones moves out of place, which is called a perilunate dislocation, or the lunate itself is driven forward out of its socket, which is called a lunate dislocation. When a break of the scaphoid travels with the dislocation, the injury is called a trans-scaphoid perilunate fracture dislocation, and it is the most unstable of the group. The energy of the injury predicts the damage, so a violent mechanism deserves more suspicion than a simple fall, although thin bone may give way in a fall from standing height.

Carpal dislocations are uncommon compared with fractures of the radius, and that rarity is part of the danger, because an uncommon injury is not the first one a busy clinician considers. A large share of perilunate injuries are reported as missed at the first visit, and the size of that share differs between published series, so any figure offered here is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than a fixed truth. The reasons for the delay are easy to understand, because the wrist is swollen, the patient can still move the fingers, and the dramatic deformity that turns the wrist into the shape of a fork when the radius is broken is absent. A dislocation of the carpus may leave the wrist looking only puffy and slightly out of shape, and the tender point that would give it away lies deep beneath the swelling. In teaching, this injury is often described as a sprain that will not settle, and that is exactly the story a listener should distrust, because the mechanism, the swelling and any change in the feeling of the hand are the three findings that should keep the question open. An older adult with thin bone may instead break the radius, or break it and dislocate the carpus at the same time.

Assessment begins long before the wrist is touched, and it begins with the scene and with the patient as a whole. The responder asks how the injury happened, how far the patient fell, whether the patient landed on the hand, how much time has passed, and whether the same wrist has been injured before. The patient is asked about numbness, tingling and weakness of the grip, because those symptoms may be the only clue that the median nerve is involved, and the age of the patient and any illness or medicine that weakens bone are part of the history as well. If the patient is intoxicated, confused or unable to answer, the history is taken from a witness, and the examination is described as incomplete rather than reassuring. The primary survey comes before everything else, with its steps remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, because life comes before limb. Only when the patient is stable enough does attention turn to the wrist, and even then the wrist is examined without being forced.

The limb is examined by looking, by feeling and by asking the patient to move it, and never by forcing it. The examiner compares the injured wrist with the other one, because the difference between the two sides is more reliable than any single finding taken alone. Swelling may be the only visible change, and it can arrive quickly, which is one reason the first examination is the most informative one ever performed on that patient. Tenderness is often greatest over the hollow at the base of the thumb between the tendons, where the scaphoid lies, and it may also be found at the back of the wrist over the lunate. The contour of the wrist may be lost rather than obviously deformed, and a wrist that looks merely puffy is not thereby cleared of serious injury, so a finding that is absent proves nothing and a wrist that is swollen and painful out of proportion to the story told is treated as a serious injury until someone qualified decides otherwise.

Swelling is the great masker of this injury, and it deserves its own warning. A wrist that fills with fluid swiftly hides the loss of contour, hides the tender point over the lunate and hides the loss of the normal hollow at the back of the wrist. A swollen wrist is also harder to examine, so a clinician who arrives later will find fewer signs than the responder who arrived first, which is one reason the first record matters so much. The amount of swelling does not measure the severity of the injury, because a modest sprain can swell dramatically while a complete dislocation can swell surprisingly little at the beginning. A sign that depends on the examiner's fingertips, such as the exact shape of the wrist, is unreliable in a swollen limb and unreliable in inexperienced hands, so the response to that uncertainty is not to guess but to immobilize, to record what was found, and to refer the patient for proper assessment.

Stabilization is the second step of the sequence, and in an injury of the wrist it is a small set of deliberate acts. The wrist is supported in the position in which it was found and is never straightened, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bones, and the bones of the wrist are small enough that a pull on the fingers moves them. A deformed wrist is not pulled back into line by anyone in the field, for the same reason that governs every other limb in this course, which is that force applied to a joint whose ligaments are already torn can tear what remained intact. The lay first aider supports the wrist with the hands, a folded cloth or a pillow, keeps the patient warm, and waits for help, while the trained first responder and the paramedic may apply a splint within the procedures their service authorizes. Rings, watches and bracelets are removed early, before the swelling arrives, and a ring that is already tight is not pulled over a swollen finger but is cut away by someone with the proper instrument and the authority to use it. The patient is protected from cold, because cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, and a wet sleeve is replaced with dry covering. Pain is treated within the local protocol, but the choice of substance and the amount belong to the prescriber, and this course teaches no dose, no route and no regimen.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the hand and compares the two sides, then feels the pulse at the wrist on the thumb side, where the artery lies close to the surface, and compares it with the other hand. A nail is pressed briefly to watch the color return, and the examiner notes how quickly it does so rather than stating a threshold as a rule, because the figures that describe it differ between services and editions. Sensation is tested on both hands, and the examiner asks specifically about the thumb, the index finger, the middle finger and the inner half of the ring finger. The patient is asked to spread the fingers, to bend them, and to bring the thumb away from the palm against gentle resistance, which is the movement produced by the muscles at the base of the thumb. The formal examination of nerve function belongs to the clinician, while the lay first aider observes color, warmth and the ability of the patient to move and to feel.

Among all the findings, a change in the median nerve is the one that alters the plan. When the lunate is driven forward it presses on that nerve inside the passage, and the patient reports tingling in the thumb and the first two fingers, or cannot feel a light touch there, or finds the thumb weak. In some patients this comes on slowly over hours and in others it is present on arrival, and either pattern carries the same meaning, because a nerve that stays compressed for a short time can recover while a nerve compressed for longer may not. The absence of these symptoms does not exclude a dislocation, and their presence does not prove one, so the finding is weighed with the mechanism and the rest of the examination. A patient who cannot report sensation, because of intoxication, confusion, a head injury or a language barrier, is treated as a patient whose nerve status is unknown and therefore as one who needs urgent assessment. That judgment belongs to the clinician who takes over, and the duty of the responder is to hand over the uncertainty clearly.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it. In the wrist that means supporting the wrist together with the joint at the base of the fingers and, on the other side, the joint at the elbow, because a splint that holds the wrist alone can still be moved by the joints at either end. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, it is applied over the position found, and it is secured firmly enough to hold without becoming a band that stops the circulation. The material matters less than the principle, so a padded board, a folded magazine or a commercial splint can each serve, and an improvised device is checked more often because it shifts as swelling changes. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and they check the fingers and the pulse after the splint is in place. A rigid plaster cast is not the answer during the acute phase, because swelling inside a closed ring of plaster can turn the cast into a tourniquet, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what definitive immobilization is required, while a circumferential bandage applied by someone without the training to judge it is a hazard whose fingers are checked and rechecked.

There is one act that a listener must never take from this chapter and attempt, and that act is reduction. Reduction means restoring the small bones of the wrist to their normal relationship, and in a carpal dislocation it needs sedation or anesthesia, imaging during the attempt, and the strength to overcome the pull of the forearm muscles, which is far greater than a pair of hands can manage. It is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique itself belongs to supervised clinical training rather than to a book. A failed attempt may injure the median nerve further, may push a bone through the skin and convert a closed injury into an open one, or may drive the lunate farther out of place. Traction, which means a steady pull on the hand to draw the bones apart, is not a field technique for the same reason, and neither is casting, because neither can be done safely without knowing what lies beneath the skin. A reduction is not attempted when the diagnosis is unclear, when the injury is open, when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the equipment or the training is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed, because each of those situations can turn an injury that would have healed into one that cannot, and force is not a technique.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do it. A plain radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions, because a displacement seen from the front may be invisible from the side and the reverse is equally true. For the wrist, the view from the side shows whether the lunate still sits in line with the capitate, and a conclusion drawn from a single image taken from the front is not enough, so the clinician asks for further views when the story and the examination do not fit the first picture. Computed tomography shows more detail of the small bones and of the joints between them, and it is often needed, because the plain images of a carpal injury can look deceptively ordinary. Magnetic resonance imaging shows the ligaments, the cartilage and the bone that has lost its blood supply, and a normal report never clears a patient whose history and examination point to a carpal dislocation, because an image is a picture of one moment in a changing injury and the patient is examined and reassessed after it has been taken.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, and the comparison is what has value rather than any single measurement. The hand is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the splint or bandage is checked for tightness and for points of pressure. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness, and a hand that becomes tense, shiny and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. If a compartment syndrome is suspected, the constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the limits of local protocol, the wrist is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. A patient whose fingers were pink and sensitive before the splint and are pale and numb after it is a patient whose splint is the problem until someone proves otherwise. The responder says aloud what has changed, and says it to the person who will make the next decision.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and for a suspected carpal dislocation the answer is referral rather than a splint and a quiet journey home. The reason is that the small bones of the wrist are held in place by ligaments, so a dislocation means those ligaments have failed, and a splint supports the wrist without restoring the alignment of bones that are already out of line. A wrist that feels deceptively comfortable after splinting may still be dislocated, and the swelling that follows will make it harder, not easier, to put right. Delay carries specific penalties, because the median nerve is compressed by the displaced lunate, the cartilage is worn by bones that rub in the wrong place, and the lunate may lose its blood supply and die, a change that cannot be undone afterwards. The patient is therefore referred without delay to a facility where the wrist can be imaged properly, where a reduction can be performed by someone qualified, and where operative treatment is available, and a patient with numbness or weakness in the territory of the median nerve is referred as an emergency, with the receiving team told about it explicitly.

Definitive treatment of a carpal dislocation belongs to the hand surgeon and to the operating room, and the part of the listener is to understand its purpose rather than its steps. The surgeon first restores the relationship of the bones, which may be done as a closed procedure guided by imaging or as an open operation in which the joint is inspected directly. Because the ligaments that held the carpus are torn, the surgeon repairs or reconstructs them and holds the bones with metal pins, screws or a plate while the tissues heal, and the general name for the open operation is open reduction and internal fixation. A cast or a brace may be worn after the operation, and the timing of its removal and the start of movement belong to the surgeon and to the therapist rather than to the patient or the responder. Rehabilitation is slow, because a wrist that stiffens takes the function of the whole hand with it, and it is guided by a therapist working to the instructions of the surgeon. Published accounts of how long the wrist is protected differ between centers and between surgeons, so any period named in this course is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

What can go wrong is worth stating plainly, because it explains the urgency. The lunate has a fragile blood supply, and a lunate that is left out of place may die, a change called avascular necrosis, after which the bone collapses and destroys the joints above and below it. The scaphoid carries the same risk, particularly when it is broken near the end closest to the forearm, and a scaphoid that fails to unite causes pain and arthritis that may appear long after the fall. The cartilage of the wrist can be worn away by a joint that is not aligned, and the result is arthritis that limits grip and rotation years later. The median nerve can lose function permanently if it is compressed for too long, leaving a hand that cannot feel or grip properly, and the wrist itself can become stiff. Every one of these outcomes is more likely when the diagnosis is delayed, which is the argument for examining carefully, imaging properly and referring early rather than waiting to see whether the wrist improves.

A constructed illustration may help to fix the pattern in mind. A young adult falls from a ladder onto one outstretched hand and is brought to an urgent care center with a wrist that is swollen but not obviously deformed. The skin is intact, the fingers move and the pulse at the wrist is present, but the patient says that the thumb and the first two fingers feel asleep and that the thumb feels weak. A radiograph taken from the front appears nearly normal, and the clinician asks for a view from the side, which shows that the small bone in the middle of the wrist is no longer in line with the bone of the forearm. Nothing is straightened, the hand is splinted in the position in which it was found, the neurovascular findings are written down again, and the patient is transferred to a hand surgery service, where the joint is put back into place and the torn ligaments are repaired. That patient is invented for teaching and is not a real person, and the lesson of the illustration is that the numb thumb and the single direction of the first radiograph were the two findings that mattered.

Scope of practice runs through every step of this injury, and it is worth saying plainly who may do what. The lay first aider supports the limb, keeps the patient warm and still, removes a watch or a loose ring when that can be done without force, and calls for help. The trained first responder and the paramedic may splint, may record the neurovascular findings, may give what the local protocol authorizes for pain, and may transport the patient, but they do not reduce a dislocation and they do not interpret an image. The nurse observes, records, reassesses, prepares the patient and reports any change, working within the rules of the institution and the instructions of the treating clinician. The physician examines the patient, orders and interprets the imaging, decides about analgesia and sedation, and performs or arranges a reduction within the local protocol and the training that person holds. The orthopedic surgeon or the hand surgeon provides operative treatment and defines the plan for rehabilitation, and the therapist carries it out. When a responder is unsure whether an act lies within their scope, the safe answer is to do the things that need no permission, such as support, warmth, recording and calling for help, and to leave the rest to the person who is authorized to do it. The record follows the patient, because the position in which the wrist was found and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are what let the receiving team continue a story instead of starting a new one.

To summarize, a carpal dislocation is a tearing of the ligaments that hold the small bones of the wrist in line, and it may look like a sprain, because swelling hides the deformity while the fingers still move. The mechanism of a fall onto an outstretched hand, the swelling that arrives quickly, and any numbness or weakness in the thumb and the first two fingers are the findings that keep the question open. The sequence is followed in order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and the neurovascular findings are recorded before every intervention and repeated after it. A wrist is splinted in the position found and is never straightened or pulled in the field, a ring that is already tight is not pulled over a swollen finger, and an open wound is never explored blindly. A radiograph taken from one direction alone may look normal, so a normal report never clears a patient whose history and examination suggest this injury, and computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging may be needed. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and no listener should attempt them from a book, because a failed attempt can damage the median nerve or open a closed injury. A suspected carpal dislocation is referred urgently rather than splinted and sent home, and it is referred as an emergency when the median nerve has changed, because time is muscle and a wrist put right early works best.
