# Chapter 34 — Malleolar Fractures

The ankle carries the whole weight of the body through a surface no wider than the palm of a child, thousands of times a day and without complaint. Two bony prominences frame that surface, the medial malleolus on the inner side, which is the lower end of the shin bone, and the lateral malleolus on the outer side, which is the lower end of the slender calf bone. With the back edge of the lower shin bone they form a socket that grips the ankle bone, and that grip is what makes the joint steady enough to walk on. This chapter follows the order used throughout this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. What follows is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The medial malleolus is the inner corner of the lower tibia, pointing downward just beneath the skin with almost nothing between it and the air. The lateral malleolus belongs to the fibula, sits a little lower and farther back, and can be felt by anyone as the bump on the outside of the ankle. Behind the lower tibia lies a third prominence, the posterior malleolus, which cannot be seen from outside and is found by an examiner who feels the back of the joint or by the radiograph. The upper surface of the lower tibia, the plafond, is a slightly curved ceiling of smooth articular cartilage, and the ankle bone rolls beneath it inside the socket. That socket is a ring in which the tibia, the fibula and the strong ligaments binding them all take a share, so a break of one malleolus sends the examiner to look at the other one.

The inner side of the joint is held by a fan of ligament called the deltoid ligament, which runs from the medial malleolus to the bones of the foot and is strong enough that a violent force often breaks the bone instead of tearing it. The outer side is held by three ligaments, one from the front of the lateral malleolus to the ankle bone, one below it to the heel bone and one behind, and they are injured far more often because the foot rolls inward so much more easily than outward. A torn ligament lets the ankle bone tilt within the socket, so a patient whose bones are intact may still have an unstable joint full of blood. Between the two bones of the lower leg lies a firm fibrous joint that allows the fibula to move only a very little and holds the socket at a constant width, and when that binding tears the socket widens. The examiner therefore thinks of that binding whenever an ankle is swollen and the patient cannot bear weight.

The position of the foot at the moment of injury is the most useful piece of history a responder can obtain, because the direction of the force tells the examiner where to look. When the foot rolls outward while the leg twists inward on a planted foot, the ankle bone turns within the socket and presses the lateral malleolus outward, so the bone may break at the level of the joint or well above it. The same force stretches the inner side, so the medial malleolus may be pulled off as a fragment or the deltoid ligament may tear instead. When the foot rolls inward, the ankle bone tilts, the outer ligaments are stretched and torn, and a piece may be pulled from the tip of the lateral malleolus or a break may run across the bone just above the joint. A fall from a height drives the ankle bone upward into the ceiling of the socket and may break the front or the back edge of the lower tibia as well. These patterns are named and sorted in the textbooks and the names differ between them, so what matters at the bedside is the story of the force and the two prominences that hurt when touched.

Swelling is the finding that hides the injury, and that is why an ankle fracture is missed in the first hour more often than almost any other fracture in the body. The joint fills with blood from the torn bone ends and ligaments, and the soft tissues around the malleoli are loose enough to accept a great deal of it, so within minutes the ankle can double in size and the prominences can no longer be felt. A displaced ankle then looks like a broad, tense and shining swelling rather than an obvious step in the bone, and the deformity may show only as the direction in which the foot points. Comparing the injured ankle with the other one is therefore part of the examination, because the healthy side shows what this patient is normally like. A patient who cannot bear weight and whose tenderness lies over the bony prominences rather than the ligaments has a fracture until a clinician with images decides otherwise.

Looking at the injured ankle is the first act of the examination, and it is done with the limb supported and the other ankle in view for comparison. The responder notes the shape of the joint, the direction in which the foot points and the position of the heel, because that direction is the visible record of the force that broke the bone. The skin is inspected from below the knee to the tips of the toes, including the back of the heel, since a small wound can be the only sign that the injury communicates with the air. A wound over a malleolus makes the fracture an open one even when it is only a puncture, and the management of the whole patient changes at that moment. The skin over the medial malleolus is thin and lies directly upon the bone, so it is the skin most likely to be torn from within by a fragment or to break down under the pressure of swelling, and a pale or dusky patch there must be reported at once.

Assessment begins with the scene and with the story, and the story is taken while the limb is supported rather than examined. The responder asks how the injury happened, in which direction the foot was forced and at what time, and whether the ankle took weight afterward. The medical history matters as much as the accident, because a bone thinned by age, by disease or by medicine may break under a force that would leave a healthy ankle unhurt. Before the ankle is examined at all, the responder decides whether the patient is stable, because the ankle can wait for minutes that the airway, the breathing and the circulation cannot. The steps of that first survey are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and a broken ankle found during that survey does not change the order of it. A patient who fell from a height or was thrown from a vehicle may have injuries of the spine, the chest, the pelvis and the abdomen as well, so a falling level of consciousness or skin that has turned cold and pale sends the responder back to the beginning. The scene is checked for anything that can still injure the patient or the responder, personal protective equipment is used according to the procedures of the service, and only when the patient is stable enough does the examination turn to the limb, which is examined in the position in which it was found.

Feeling the ankle follows looking, and it is done gently, with the limb supported and the two sides compared. The examiner feels the medial malleolus, the lateral malleolus and the back edge of the lower tibia, presses along the whole length of the fibula up to the head beside the knee, and feels the base of the bone on the outer edge of the foot, because a twisting injury can break more than one bone. Tenderness greatest over a bony prominence suggests a fracture, while tenderness greatest over the soft tissue below and in front of the malleolus suggests a ligament injury. Crepitus, the crackling made when broken ends rub together, is never deliberately sought, because seeking it moves the fracture, and the tests that pull the ankle sideways belong to supervised clinical training rather than to the field. Movement is asked for rather than imposed, so the patient is invited to move the toes and to turn the foot gently if that is comfortable, and a refused movement is information too. A patient who cannot bear weight is treated as having a fracture until images say otherwise, the limb is never straightened and never used as a lever, and a patient who cannot cooperate has a limited examination recorded rather than a normal one. The trained responder, the nurse and the physician each examine within the limits that their training, their equipment and the local protocol allow.

The neurovascular examination of the foot is never skipped and never assumed, and it is performed before any intervention and repeated after every one. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the foot, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and feels the pulse on the top of the foot and the pulse behind the medial malleolus. Sensation is tested on both feet and compared over the top of the foot, over the sole and along the outer border of the foot, because different nerves supply each of those areas. The patient is asked to move the toes, to spread them and to lift the foot upward against a gentle hand, and any movement that was possible a moment earlier and is now lost is written down at once. The findings are recorded in the words of the patient before a splint is applied and again after it is applied, because a finding not recorded before an intervention cannot be attributed afterwards. A formal examination of this kind belongs to the trained responder, the nurse or the physician, while the lay first aider reports what the patient says about feeling and movement and keeps the foot warm. Some of these signs are less reliable than they look, because a patient whose nerves are already damaged by diabetes or by alcohol may not notice numbness that is new, and swelling may be modest in an ankle that is quietly bleeding. The answer to that uncertainty is a lower threshold for immobilization, for repeated reassessment and for urgent referral.

A pulse that can still be felt does not exclude serious injury, and that lesson belongs to the ankle as much as to any other part of the body. Pressure inside a closed space can stop the flow through the smallest vessels long before it stops the flow through the main artery, so a foot with a strong pulse may still be losing its muscle and its nerve. The lower leg is divided into four compartments and the ankle sits below them, so bleeding from a fractured malleolus can raise the pressure in the leg as well as fill the joint. Pain out of proportion to the injury, pain produced by stretching the toes gently upward, a foot that is tense and swollen, numbness and a foot that cannot be lifted are the findings that stop the responder. The diagnosis is clinical, and time is muscle, so a responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for a confirmation that will arrive too late.

Stabilization means keeping the patient still, keeping the ankle in the position in which it was found, controlling the bleeding that can be controlled, and keeping the patient warm. A deformed ankle is supported with the hands, with a pillow, with a rolled blanket or with padding on either side of the joint, and it is never straightened in the field, however alarming its shape appears. The reason is not tidiness but anatomy, because the vessels and nerves of the foot pass close to the bone, and a displaced fragment may be pressing on a torn vessel in the position that looks wrong to the eye. Pulling the joint straight can tear what remained intact, can release the pressure that was holding a torn vessel closed and can cut the skin from within, turning a closed fracture into an open one. An open wound over the ankle is covered with a sterile dressing and never explored blindly, exposed bone is never pushed back into it, and an embedded object is left exactly where it is, since definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else. Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, so the patient is insulated from the ground and wet clothing is replaced with covering.

A splint for the ankle is built around one principle, which is that it must prevent movement of the joint without pressing on the two bony prominences that are already injured. The support is placed along the position in which the joint was found, it is padded where it touches the malleoli and the heel, and it is secured firmly enough to hold the foot steady without becoming a band that stops the circulation. It is long enough to control the foot and the lower leg together, so that the ankle cannot be twisted by the weight of the foot hanging from it, and the toes are left uncovered so that color, warmth, movement and sensation can be checked and rechecked. The material matters far less than the principle, since a padded board, a folded blanket or a pillow bound in place each serve, and an improvised device is checked more often because it shifts as the patient is moved. The ankle is not forced into the square resting position of a healthy joint, because forcing it is another way of straightening a deformed limb. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, the nurse works within the same limits inside a facility, and the lay first aider supports the limb by hand and waits for help.

Reduction, traction and casting are the treatments that restore and hold the position of the bones, and this course describes what they are meant to achieve, what can go wrong and who is qualified to perform them, never how they are done. A reduction brings the displaced malleoli and the ankle bone back into their proper relation, a process sometimes held with traction applied as a gentle continuous pull, and a cast or a surgical fixation holds the position while the bone heals. Each of those acts is learned with a teacher and performed according to local protocol, and none of them can be learned from a book. A reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered, when a fragment is caught within the joint, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the responder lacks training or equipment, or when an earlier attempt has failed. In each of those situations the attempt can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. Force is not a technique, resistance and a new finding are signals to stop rather than to push harder, and the whole of the field role is to support the limb, keep the patient warm, record the neurovascular findings and speak plainly to the patient.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician trained to do it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and it is taken in at least two directions so that a displacement in the third direction is not hidden, with the joint above and the joint below included so that a second injury is not missed. The ankle is also pictured through a view that looks straight into the socket, since a fracture of one malleolus can let the ankle bone tilt in a way that a single straight picture does not show. The whole length of the fibula is examined when a lateral malleolus is broken, because the force that breaks the bone at the ankle can break it again beside the knee. Computed tomography shows the detail of a complex break, of the back edge of the lower tibia and of the socket when an operation is being planned, and magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for the ligaments, the cartilage and the growing part of the bone in a child. Either study is requested when the answer it gives will change what is done, and the lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging while the trained responder does not decide which study is needed. An image is a picture of one moment, and the patient must still be examined and reassessed after it has been taken.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with what was written before, and it is the comparison that carries the value rather than any single measurement. The foot is checked for color, warmth, the two pulses, sensation, movement and pain, the splint is checked for tightness and for pressure over the malleoli, and any dressing over a wound is checked for bleeding that has soaked through. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, pain when the toes are stretched gently upward, numbness between the first two toes, a foot that cannot be lifted and a joint that becomes tense and shiny are the findings that stop the responder. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome does not wait for the diagnosis to be confirmed, releases a constricting bandage or splint only within the limits of the local protocol, keeps the limb at the level of the heart rather than above it, keeps the patient warm and refers urgently. The position in which the limb is kept while the patient waits differs between services and editions and is confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Referral or definitive treatment is the final step of the sequence, and it begins with the recognition that some ankles cannot be managed where the patient lies. A patient with a break of both malleoli, a break of the back edge of the lower tibia, a fracture that has displaced the ankle bone within its socket, an open injury, a suspected compartment syndrome, a foot whose pulses or sensation have changed, or a limb with poor circulation is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. The decision about how urgently a patient must travel is made by the clinician or by the service protocol, and a responder who is unsure treats the injury as urgent and says so in the handover. Waiting to see whether the ankle settles on its own helps nobody, and the swelling that hides the deformity also hides the moment at which the skin becomes unsalvageable. Two groups deserve a lower threshold for immobilization and referral, and the first is children, whose lower tibia and fibula carry the growth regions that lengthen the leg, so a twisting injury that looks like a sprain may run through that region rather than through the joint. A child who will not use the ankle or who limps for days after a fall is therefore examined rather than watched, and an injury across the growing part of the bone can disturb the growth of the leg for years. The second group is older adults, whose bone has lost density and whose skin has grown thin, so a fall from standing height can displace an ankle, the pain may be far less than the severity of the injury, and the skin over the malleolus may break down within hours. A patient who lives alone or who cannot keep weight off the limb needs a plan for care rather than an appointment, and the receiving team is told the time of the injury, the mechanism and every neurovascular finding with the time at which it was recorded.

Definitive treatment takes several forms, and which is used depends on the bones that are broken, the state of the socket and the needs of the patient. A stable fracture that holds its position may be treated with support while the bone heals, with weight bearing allowed at a stage the treating clinician sets. A displaced fracture is usually held with plates and screws placed on the bone, an operation called open reduction and internal fixation, performed by an orthopedic surgeon in an operating room. When the socket has been torn open between the two bones of the lower leg, a screw or a tight binding may be placed across them for a period that teaching gives as several weeks, an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. When the skin and the soft tissues are too damaged to tolerate an operation through them, the surgeon may place a frame of pins and bars outside the limb and wait for the swelling to settle. After any of those treatments the ankle is stiff and swollen for weeks, and rehabilitation belongs to a physiotherapist working within a plan the treating clinician sets, since the goal is a joint that carries weight and not merely a bone that has joined.

This chapter describes no dose, no route and no drug regimen, because the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol. Pain is treated by the clinician who can assess it, and the finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, increasing, out of proportion to the injury, or accompanied by a foot that is tense, cold or numb. A responder at any level may make the patient more comfortable by supporting the ankle, keeping the patient warm and explaining what is happening, and a trained responder may give an analgesic only under the standing orders and the authorization of the service. Antibiotics for an open injury and protection against tetanus are decisions for the clinician, and the part of the responder is to report the wound, the time of the injury and the state of the patient's immunization. Sedation for any procedure belongs to a clinician working in a monitored setting with the equipment and the staff the protocol requires, and a listener must not be able to take a prescription from this book.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the ankle was found, the state of the skin and the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention are written or spoken in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified to clear it has done so, and no lone manual handling technique is safe, so a patient who fell from a height is moved by a trained team using the precautions the local protocol requires. Where this chapter names a time, a distance or a period of healing, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions. Everything described here is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision, and a responder who is unsure about any part of this chapter treats the ankle as urgent, keeps the patient warm, records what is seen, and calls for help rather than for reassurance.

To summarize, the ankle is a small joint that carries a great load, and its two malleoli are the lower ends of the tibia and the fibula, held into a socket by ligaments that decide whether it stays steady. The direction in which the foot was forced at the moment of injury tells the examiner which side to expect to be broken, and the swelling that follows within minutes hides the deformity and most often leads a broken ankle to be dismissed as a sprain. Assess the patient first, remember that life comes before limb, and record the neurovascular status of the foot, including color, warmth, the two pulses, sensation and movement, before any intervention and again after every one. Stabilize by supporting the ankle in the position found, never straightening it in the field, covering any wound with a sterile dressing, leaving an embedded object in place and keeping the patient warm. Immobilize the joint and the joints above and below it, pad the bony prominences, leave the toes uncovered for the checks that follow, and let only a trained responder or paramedic apply a splint within the equipment and the procedures the service authorizes. Image only when a clinician orders it, reassess after everything you do, suspect a compartment syndrome and act on it, and refer a displaced, open or unstable ankle, a foot whose nerves or vessels have changed, a child with an injury across the growing part of the bone, or an older adult with thin skin over a displaced joint. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and reduction is not attempted when the joint is shattered, a fragment is trapped, the vessels or the nerves are failing, the injury is open, the diagnosis is unclear, the responder lacks training and equipment, or an earlier attempt has failed. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
