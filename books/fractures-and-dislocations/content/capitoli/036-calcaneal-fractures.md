# Chapter 36 — Calcaneal Fractures

The heel bone, the calcaneus, is what a person lands on when the feet arrive first, and it is what gives way when a body falls from a height onto a hard surface. It is the largest bone of the foot, it forms the whole of the heel and the back of the arch, and it carries the weight of the body at every step. A break of the calcaneus is therefore rarely a small injury, because the force that broke it traveled through the whole skeleton first, and the same force may have broken the spine, the pelvis or the other heel without the patient noticing. This chapter follows the order used throughout this course, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. What follows is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The calcaneus lies beneath the ankle bone, the talus, and the two form the joint that lets the foot turn on uneven ground. Its upper surface carries smooth facets that meet the matching surfaces of the talus, and behind them the bone narrows into the heel that strikes the ground. At the back the tendon of the calf, the Achilles tendon, attaches to the bone, and along the sole a tough band called the plantar fascia runs from the heel to the toes, so the bone is loaded from above and pulled from below. On the inner side a shelf of bone, the sustentaculum tali, projects beneath the talus and supports it, and the tendons that turn the foot inward glide over that shelf. On the outer side the bone is thin, and the tendons that turn the foot outward pass close against it.

The force that breaks a heel bone is usually an axial load, which means a load directed straight up the line of the leg while the foot is planted. A fall from a height onto the feet does this, and so does a jump from a wall, a fall from a ladder, a fall down stairs, and a heavy object falling onto a seated patient. The talus is driven downward into the calcaneus like a wedge and, being harder than the bone beneath it, splits the heel along the lines of least resistance. Where that split runs decides much of what follows, because a break that stays outside the joint surface behaves differently from one that crosses it and steps the cartilage out of line. Whether the joint surface is involved is the question every classification asks, and the names of the categories differ between systems, so the listener should hold the question and let the surgeon hold the labels. The mechanism is the most useful history a responder can obtain.

Because the energy arrives from below, the examiner must look above the heel, and the spine is the structure most often missed. A patient who lands on both feet transmits the force through the legs, the pelvis and the spinal column, and a compression injury of the thoracic or lumbar spine can exist beside a broken heel in a patient who reports only the foot. The pelvis, the hips, the knees, the other heel and the head are examined in the same pass, and the patient is turned only once, by a team using the precautions the local protocol requires, because a spine that has not been cleared is treated as unstable until someone qualified clears it. The history begins with the height of the fall, the surface landed on and whether the patient could stand at once, and it ends with a search for every place the same fall could have injured. A responder who records only the foot has recorded half of the injury.

Both heels are broken in a substantial proportion of these patients, and the second heel is often the quieter one, so both are examined and compared rather than merely inspected. A patient may say that one foot hurts more, may refuse to put either down, or may be unable to say which foot struck first, and a swollen heel on the less painful side is easily dismissed when the other side is deformed. Comparing the two sides reveals the widening, the flattening of the arch and the loss of the normal inward tilt of the heel. This is a clinical comparison and not a diagnosis, and the definitive answer comes from imaging ordered by a clinician. In a patient who is intoxicated, confused or in shock from other injuries, even the comparison may be unreliable, so the threshold to image both heels is low.

Assessment begins before the foot is touched, with the scene and with the patient. A fall from a height can injure several body regions at once, so the primary survey is completed first, in the order remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. A patient with a broken heel may also be bleeding inside from a torn organ, may have a head injury, or may have an injured spine, and the limb is examined only when the patient is stable enough to allow it. The lay first aider keeps the patient still and warm and calls for help, and the trained responder or the paramedic continues the survey within the procedures the service authorizes. Where the fall happened and how long the patient has been lying there are part of the assessment, and when two correct actions compete for the same hands, life comes before limb.

The heel itself is examined by looking first. It is swollen and broadened, the skin over it is tight and shiny, and the normal curve of the outer edge of the foot is lost, so the foot sits flatter and lower than the other one. Bruising appears on the sole early, and bruising that reaches the sole after a fall points to the bone rather than to a sprain of the ankle, though it may take hours to appear and its absence proves nothing. The skin is inspected with particular care along the inner side of the heel, where the bone lies just beneath the surface and a sharp fragment can split the skin from within, turning a closed injury into an open one without any outside wound. Blisters filled with blood may form over the heel within a day, and they signal badly contused soft tissues rather than an intact bone.

Palpation is done gently and with a purpose, which is to find where the tenderness lies and whether the bone behaves as one piece. The examiner presses the two sides of the heel together between the fingers and the thumb, and a sharp increase in pain is the classic bedside sign of a broken heel, though it is absent in some patients and unreliable in one who is intoxicated, sedated or distracted by another injury. Pressing upward against the sole also causes pain, and neither maneuver establishes the diagnosis by itself. What the responder looks for is not one sign but the pattern of a swollen and widened heel, tenderness over the bone, pain on squeezing, and an inability to bear weight. Bone tenderness in a patient who cannot walk is enough to immobilize the foot and to ask for an image, and the choice of image belongs to the clinician.

Every step from this point onward is preceded and followed by the neurovascular examination, recorded in a fixed order so that the receiving team can compare it with its own findings. The examiner feels the pulse behind the inner ankle and the pulse on the top of the foot, notes the color and temperature of the skin, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and compares each finding with the uninjured side. Sensation is tested on the sole and on the outer border of the foot, where nerves run close to the bone and near the heel. The patient is asked to curl and spread the toes and to turn the foot against resistance, and any movement that was possible a moment earlier and is now weak is recorded as a change. Numbness or lost sensation on the sole, an absent or diminished pulse, and pale, cool skin make the injury urgent. The lay first aider reports what the patient says rather than testing it, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or the clinician.

Swelling in the foot can become a compartment syndrome, and the foot holds more than one closed space in which pressure can rise. The deep space of the foot and the space around the heel bone are the ones most often involved after this fracture, and the pressure rises because the broken bone bleeds inside a space that cannot expand. The findings are pain that increases instead of settling, pain when the toes are stretched upward, a foot that is tense and tight to the touch, and numbness or tingling in the sole, and the pulse may remain present while the tissue is starved, so a normal pulse never excludes the diagnosis. The diagnosis is clinical and belongs to a clinician, and time is muscle, so a responder who suspects it does not wait for confirmation. Where local protocol permits, an authorized responder releases the constricting bandage or splint, the foot is not elevated above the level of the heart, the patient is kept warm, and transfer is arranged as an emergency. Only the operating room can open those spaces, and that decision belongs to the surgeon.

Stabilization of a broken heel is a small set of acts, and the first of them is restraint. The foot is left in the position in which it was found and is never straightened, pulled into line or manipulated in the field, because the fracture lies inside a tight envelope where nerves and vessels lie against the bone, and because moving the fragments may push a sharp edge through intact skin. The patient is kept still, and the heel is supported from below with the hands, a pillow or a folded blanket so that its weight is not hanging on the injured tissues. Bleeding from an open wound is controlled by pressure around the wound and not inside it, an embedded object is left where it is, and exposed bone is never pushed back into the wound. Cold is a complication of trauma rather than a detail of comfort, so the patient is insulated from the ground and wet clothing is replaced with covering.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, and for the foot that means holding the ankle as well as the heel. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a padded splint that runs behind the calf and under the sole, with the foot supported at the angle in which it was found and the heel free of hard pressure, using the equipment and procedures their service authorizes. A pillow tied around the foot and leg, a rigid board padded with clothing, or a commercial splint can each serve, and an improvised device is checked more often. Nothing is bound tightly around the heel itself, because the swelling can double the size of the foot within hours and a band that was comfortable at the roadside can behave like a tourniquet on arrival. A cast is not applied during the acute phase of a swollen heel, and casting is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol rather than from a book.

Elevation is the next act, and it is done with one exception that must not be forgotten. Raising the injured foot above the level of the heart lets the swelling drain toward the body, and for a swollen heel without any sign of a compartment syndrome that is what the patient needs. The foot is raised on a pillow or a folded blanket while the knee is supported, so that the limb is not left hanging, and the position is held during transport and in the waiting area. The exception is the foot in which the pressure inside a compartment is already too high, because elevation then lowers the pressure driving blood into the tissues and can make the starvation worse. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome therefore keeps the foot level, refers the patient urgently, and asks rather than guesses. How high and for how long belongs to the clinician responsible for the patient.

Imaging confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered and interpreted by a clinician. A plain radiograph of the foot is usually the first study and is taken in more than one direction, and the heel often needs a view taken along the line of the bone through the sole, because the ordinary side view alone can hide a break that runs across the joint surface. The whole foot and the other heel are often included, and a patient who fell from a height also needs images of the spine and the pelvis, requested on the basis of the mechanism rather than the reported pain. Computed tomography shows the detail around the joint surface when the surgeon needs to know how many pieces there are and where they have moved, while magnetic resonance imaging is reserved for soft tissues and is rarely needed here. Only the clinician decides which study is required, and the responder neither orders nor reads images, while the patient is examined again after every study and every intervention.

One measurement appears repeatedly in the descriptions of this fracture, an angle drawn on the lateral radiograph between two lines, one along the upper surface of the heel bone and one along its under surface. The normal value lies within a range that differs slightly between texts, and a heel flattened by the fracture reduces that angle toward zero and sometimes reverses it, which describes in numbers the flattening an examiner can feel with a hand. The number is not for the responder to estimate, and any figure attached to it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Around that angle the fracture is sorted into those that spare the joint surface and those that cross it, those with two large pieces and those ground into many, and those that split the bone lengthwise. The names differ between countries and editions, and the practical questions are whether the joint surface is broken, whether the skin is open, how badly the soft tissues are injured, and what else the fall has broken.

Definitive treatment is chosen by the orthopedic surgeon together with the patient, and it depends on the pattern of the break, the state of the soft tissues, the other injuries and the needs of the person who must walk on that foot again. A fracture that does not enter the joint surface, that is not displaced, and that is found in a patient who cannot safely undergo an operation may be treated without surgery, which means a period of immobilization, no weight through the foot, elevation and a gradual return to walking under supervision. That program is prescribed by the treating clinician, and the responder's part is to keep the foot still and elevated and to ensure the patient takes no weight until someone qualified says so. Weight bearing before the bone has held is one way a good result becomes a painful flat heel, and no listener should decide this for themselves. The plan, its duration and its exceptions belong to the treating team and the local protocol.

An operation is often offered when the joint surface is broken and the pieces are out of line, because a heel that heals flat and wide wears out the joint above it and turns the foot outward. The operation is called open reduction and internal fixation, which means the surgeon opens the area, restores the position of the pieces and holds them with metal devices. A heel is difficult to operate because the skin and the fat beneath it are thin and easily damaged, and the swelling can be severe, so the surgeon may wait several days for the soft tissues to settle before operating. The timing, the approach and the choice of implant are surgical decisions, and the responder has no part in them. Reduction, closed or open, is learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, it is never taught from a book, and the same applies to traction and casting. Force is not a technique, and a reduction is not attempted when the joint surfaces are shattered beyond reconstruction, when a fragment is caught in the joint, when the vessels or nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, or when an earlier attempt has failed.

The complications of a broken heel arrive slowly, and most are caused by the shape the bone settles into rather than by the break itself. A heel that heals wide and flat changes the way the foot meets the ground, the outer edge of the ankle presses against the bone beneath it, and the tendons that turn the foot outward begin to rub and snap, so the patient reports pain on the outside of the ankle months later. The joint beneath the ankle bone develops arthritis when its smooth surfaces were stepped out of line, and that pain is felt deep in the foot and is worse on uneven ground. The pad of fat under the heel can be damaged or displaced, leaving pain on the sole with every step, and a small nerve can be caught in the scar, producing burning or numbness along the outer border of the foot. Wound problems, infection, stiffness and a foot that swells for months are also common, and a patient who smokes or has diabetes is at greater risk.

An open injury of the heel is treated as a surgical emergency, and the responder's part in it is narrow and exact. The wound is covered with a sterile dressing, the bleeding that can be controlled is controlled by pressure around the wound and not by probing inside it, the neurovascular status is recorded, the foot is immobilized in the position found, and the patient is kept warm and transferred to a facility with an operating room. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back, because the object may be plugging a torn vessel and because bone that has been outside the body is contaminated. Protection against tetanus and the use of antibiotics are medical decisions, and a listener must not be able to take a prescription from this book, so the substance, the amount, the route and the timing belong to the prescriber and the local protocol. Any break in the skin over the heel makes that decision urgent, and the clinician who examines the wound decides. Definitive cleaning and closure belong to the operating room and to nobody else.

Not every broken heel comes from a fall from a height, and the examiner who waits for a dramatic story will miss the quiet ones. A person whose bone has lost density may break the heel in a fall from standing height or in a misstep, and a patient who takes a medicine that thins the bone, or a steroid over a long period, breaks it more easily still. In such a patient the swelling may be modest, tenderness may be the only finding, and the ability to take a few steps does not exclude the fracture, so the threshold for immobilization and imaging is lower rather than higher. Age also changes treatment, since the risks of an operation rise with the illnesses that come with age, and those judgments belong to the treating team rather than the responder.

Reassessment is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment, and it is performed after every intervention and at every handover. The examiner repeats the same observations in the same order, compares them with what was recorded before, and treats any change as meaningful. The foot is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, and the splint for tightness, pressure over the bony points, and the position of the foot within it. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness or tingling in the sole, weakness of the toes, a foot that becomes tense and shiny, a wound that begins to smell, and a patient whose pulse and breathing are changing are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. A responder who suspects a compartment syndrome acts within the local protocol, where an authorized person may release the constricting bandage or splint, and does not elevate the limb above the level of the heart. The record of the change, the time it was noticed and the action taken travels with the patient and is part of the treatment.

Referral is the final step of the sequence, and for the bones of the heel it is the usual one, because the definitive decisions need an orthopedic surgeon and an operating room. A patient with a fracture that enters the joint, a badly comminuted break, an open wound, a suspected compartment syndrome, damaged nerves or vessels, a break of both heels, or any injury of the spine or pelvis is referred to a facility with imaging, an operating room and an intensive care unit. The transfer is arranged early rather than after the swelling has worsened, and the receiving team is told the mechanism, the time of the injury, the state of the skin, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, and what has already been done. During the transfer the foot stays splinted and elevated unless a compartment syndrome is suspected, the patient is kept warm, and the spine is treated as unstable until someone qualified clears it. The lay first aider does not decide the destination, and the nurse or physician who arranges the transfer works within the local protocol for the region.

An illustration, constructed rather than drawn from any real patient, shows how these pieces fit together. A man in middle age falls a single story from a ladder onto a concrete floor, lands on both feet, and tells the responders that his left foot hurts. His left heel is swollen, broad and tender when squeezed, his right heel is tender as well although far less swollen, and he has a tender area over the lower back that he attributes to lying on the ground. The responders record that both feet have a pulse and normal sensation, splint both feet in the position found with the heels supported and free of hard pressure, keep him warm, and take him to a facility with imaging rather than to the nearest clinic. The images show a break of the left heel that crosses the joint surface, a smaller break of the right heel that does not, and a compression injury of the spine, and the orthopedic surgeon plans the treatment of all three. The point is that the second heel and the spine were found because the mechanism guided the examination rather than the pain.

Everything in this chapter is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on one another, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision, and the provider level is named for every act because a course that ignores scope of practice can harm the patient who follows it. The lay first aider supports the foot, keeps the patient warm and still, and calls for help. The trained first responder and the paramedic splint the foot in the position found, record the neurovascular findings, and work within the equipment and procedures their service authorizes. The nurse and the physician examine, record, request imaging, reassess and refer within the local protocol, and the orthopedic surgeon decides about reduction, external support, operation and weight bearing. No listener should attempt a reduction, apply traction or apply a cast on the strength of a spoken lesson, and no listener should straighten a deformed foot in the field for any reason. Where this chapter names a time, a distance or a threshold, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions.

To summarize, the calcaneus is the heel bone, it breaks when a load is driven up through a planted foot, and the same fall may have broken the spine, the pelvis or the other heel, so the examination always looks above the foot as well as at it. The findings to look for are a swollen, widened and painful heel, tenderness over the bone, pain when the heel is squeezed, bruising that reaches the sole, blood blisters, a wound on the inner side, and an inability to bear weight. Both feet are compared, because the second break is often quieter. The order remains to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. The foot is never straightened in the field, an embedded object is never removed, exposed bone is never pushed back into a wound, an open wound is never explored, and definitive closure belongs to the operating room. Elevation is used for swelling but not when a compartment syndrome is suspected, and reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol.
