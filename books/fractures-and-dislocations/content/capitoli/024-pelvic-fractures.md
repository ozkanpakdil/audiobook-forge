# Chapter 24 — Pelvic Fractures

The pelvis is a ring of bone that carries the weight of the upper body into the legs. When it breaks, the injury is a bleeding injury before it is a bone injury, because blood collects behind the abdominal cavity, where nothing is seen from outside and nothing can be pressed by a hand. The patient may be pale, restless and thirsty while the skin over the pelvis shows only a graze. The pelvis therefore belongs to the first minutes of care and not to the later examination of the limb. This chapter keeps the order that every chapter of this course follows, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

To see why the bleeding is so heavy, the pelvis has to be understood as a container as well as a ring. The two hip bones meet in front at the pubic symphysis and behind at the sacroiliac joints, and strong ligaments close the ring. Inside it lie the bladder, the urethra, the rectum, the great vessels of the legs and the roots of the nerves of the lower limbs. The bone itself is rich in blood, and the veins of the pelvis form a wide, thin-walled network that lies against the bone. A ring rarely opens at one point alone, because the force that breaks it usually disrupts a second place as well.

Several sources bleed at once in this injury, which is why it is so hard to control. Torn bone surfaces ooze, torn veins bleed steadily into the loose space behind the peritoneum, and a torn artery may bleed under pressure. Teaching offers a figure of about 3 liters for what some patients lose, an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between services, countries and editions. A fracture that opens the ring increases the room available, and a break near the sacroiliac joint lies close to the great vessels. None of this bleeding is visible, and none of it can be stopped by pressing on the skin.

The mechanism tells the responder how much energy passed through the patient. A side impact in a vehicle crash, a motorcycle collision, a pedestrian struck by a vehicle, a fall from a height, a crush between heavy objects and a blast most often break the pelvis. In a young person the bone is strong, so a pelvic fracture means great energy and raises the chance that other organs are injured too. In an older person whose bone has lost density, the same ring may break in a fall from standing height. A sudden pull of a muscle can avulse a small piece of bone in a young athlete. The energy, rather than the appearance, is the first question.

A patient who cannot stand or bear weight is treated as having a broken pelvis until a qualified clinician decides otherwise. Pain may be felt in the groin, the hip, the lower back, the buttock or the perineum, and it may be reported as coming from the hip. A leg may lie turned outward or shortened, and the patient may be unable to lift a straight leg. Bruising may appear over the pubis, the groin, the scrotum, the labia or the flank, and it may take hours to show. A gap where the two pubic bones meet, or a wound near the perineum, is a serious finding. The responder records what is seen, felt and reported, and does not test the ring.

The signs are not always present, and a chapter that teaches them as certain will be believed and will then be wrong. Some pelvic fractures cause surprisingly little pain, and an older adult may walk a few steps with a break of a pubic ramus. There may be no bruising in the first hour, no deformity and no complaint beyond a reluctance to move. Intoxication, a head injury, a spinal injury and the pain of another wound can all hide the pelvis. A patient who is confused or unconscious cannot describe pain, so the responder falls back on the mechanism, the vital signs and the whole picture.

The pelvis belongs to the primary survey because it is one of the places where a patient can bleed to death while the examination of the limb is still being arranged. The survey follows the order remembered as ABCDE, which stands for airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure, and the control of massive bleeding sits inside the step for circulation. A pulse that seems normal may rise as the loss continues, and the change over minutes is more useful than any single measurement. Exposure is what reveals the wound at the perineum, the swelling of the thigh and the blood at the opening of the urethra. When two correct actions compete for the same pair of hands, life comes before limb.

No responder springs the pelvis, and that prohibition is the most important sentence in this chapter. Springing means pressing inward on the two iliac crests, rocking the ring, pulling on the legs to compare their length, or pressing again and again on the pubic symphysis to see whether it moves. Each of those movements can dislodge the clot that holds a torn vein closed, restart bleeding behind the peritoneum, drive bone into a vessel or a nerve, and cause pain that itself worsens the bleeding. The judgment about the stability of the ring belongs to a clinician who will act on the answer, and it is made once, gently, with the plan already decided. The hands of the responder support and cover, and do not test.

The neurovascular examination is done before any intervention and repeated after every one, and its result is written down, because a finding not recorded before a binder was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of both feet and feels the pulse at the top of the foot and behind the inner ankle on each side. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and the words the patient uses for numbness or tingling are written as far as possible. The pelvis shares its nerves with the legs, so a weak foot or an area of numbness may be the first sign that the ring has pressed on a nerve root. A pulse that is present does not exclude damage to a vessel, and a pulse that is absent is an emergency.

Some findings in this region are examined only by a clinician, and the task of the responder is to notice them and to report them. Blood at the opening of the urethra, blood in the urine, an inability to pass urine, a swollen scrotum or labia and a laceration of the perineum all change the plan. A urinary catheter is not passed when the urethra may be torn, and that decision belongs to the physician, or to the nurse acting under a protocol that names who may decide. A gloved examination of the rectum or the vagina is a clinical examination and not a field test. The lay first aider reports what is visible, and the trained responder does the same unless the local protocol says otherwise.

Stabilization of a suspected pelvic fracture is a short list of acts, and restraint is the most important of them. Bleeding that can be seen is controlled with direct pressure and a dressing, and an embedded object is left in place and stabilized. The patient is kept flat, moved as little as possible and protected from cold with blankets, insulation from the ground and the removal of wet clothing, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort. A deformed limb is splinted in the position in which it was found and is never straightened in the field, since the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone. The lay first aider acts without equipment, the trained responder and the paramedic act within the procedures their service authorizes, and the physician directs the care.

A pelvic binder is a device that wraps the ring at the level of the hips, and it is used to reduce the volume of the pelvis and to limit movement so that the clotting of the patient can work. It is a measure for the control of bleeding during transfer, and not a treatment that makes the fracture safe or removes the need for urgent referral. It is applied by a trained responder, a paramedic or a physician according to the training and the local protocol that govern it, while a folded sheet may serve where the service authorizes that practice. Applied wrongly, it damages skin, sits too high or too low and does nothing, or gives false reassurance while bleeding continues behind it. The reassessment after it is applied matters as much as the act of applying it.

Certain acts are forbidden in this injury, and each prohibition has a reason attached to it. The responder never asks a patient with a suspected pelvic fracture to stand, to walk or to lift a leg in order to test weight-bearing, because the test can complete a bone that was only cracked and start bleeding that was not happening. The responder never straightens a deformed limb, never pulls on a leg to compare lengths, never pushes exposed bone back into a wound, never removes an embedded object and never explores an open wound blindly. The patient is not sat up, and is not rolled from side to side without a team and a plan. Force is not a technique.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the ring, of the spine that rests on it and of the joints above and below the injury until someone qualified has cleared them. An injury of the spine is treated as unstable until it has been cleared by a person qualified to clear it, and no lone manual handling technique is safe. A patient with a suspected pelvic fracture is therefore moved by a trained team, with the precautions and the equipment that the local protocol requires. A scoop stretcher, a vacuum mattress or a firm transfer surface may be used by those trained in it, and the legs are left where they lie. A traction splint is not a device for the pelvis, and every device is checked after it is applied.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph of the pelvis is not a screening test for every patient who has fallen, and that decision belongs to the clinician who has examined the patient and weighed the mechanism. Computed tomography shows the ring, the sacrum and the joint surfaces in detail, and it is used when the patient is stable enough to travel to the scanner. Magnetic resonance imaging is used mainly for soft tissues and can show an injury that a radiograph misses in an older adult. A normal image does not exclude a fracture, and the patient is examined again after the picture has been taken.

Reassessment is performed after every intervention, and it is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison is what has value rather than any single number. The responder checks the level of consciousness, the breathing, the pulse, the color and the warmth of the skin, the pain, the abdomen for swelling, the wound for fresh bleeding, the device for tightness and the legs for movement and sensation. A patient who becomes more restless, more pale or more confused while the pulse rises is still bleeding, even if the pelvis looks unchanged. A deterioration that does not respond stops the process and sends the patient onward at once.

A pelvic fracture is dangerous because its bleeding is hidden, so the responder reads the whole patient rather than the injured part. Blood that collects behind the peritoneum does not swell the abdomen in the way that a bleeding spleen does, and it does not bruise the skin over the pelvis. A rising pulse, a falling blood pressure measured by those authorized to measure it, skin that is pale, cool and damp, thirst, restlessness and a reduced production of urine all suggest that the loss is continuing. In an older adult, a medicine that thins the blood may make the loss faster and the signs appear sooner. No single measurement is decisive, and the trend over minutes is what the receiving team will want to hear.

Definitive treatment of an unstable pelvic fracture belongs to the trauma team, the orthopedic surgeon and the interventional radiologist working in a facility with an operating room and an intensive care unit. Bleeding may be controlled by packing the pelvis in the operating room, by a procedure that blocks the bleeding vessel from inside under imaging, or by a device that holds the ring from the outside. The choice depends on the patient, the injury and the resources of the hospital, and definitive fixation of the ring is a later decision. None of these treatments is described here as a technique to be learned from a book, and none of them belongs to the responder.

Reduction, traction and casting are named in this course because a listener must know what they are, why they are done, what they are meant to achieve and what can go wrong, while the technique itself belongs to supervised clinical training and to the local protocol. Reduction restores the position of the bone ends, traction holds them in line with a gentle continuous pull, and casting or surgical fixation holds the position while the bone heals. In the pelvis these acts are performed in an operating room by a surgeon trained in them, and they are not attempted in the field or by anyone without the training and the equipment. A reduction is not attempted when the ring is unstable and the patient is bleeding, when the injury is open, when a vessel or a nerve is failing, when the diagnosis is unclear, when training or equipment is lacking, or when an earlier attempt has failed.

An open pelvic fracture is one in which the bone or the deep tissues have communicated with the outside, through a wound in the buttock, the perineum, the vagina or the rectum. The wound may be small and easy to overlook under clothing or blood, and it may communicate with the bowel, which contaminates the deep tissues at the moment of injury. The responder covers the wound with a sterile dressing, controls the bleeding that can be controlled, records the neurovascular status, immobilizes, keeps the patient warm and arranges urgent transfer. An embedded object is never removed and exposed bone is never pushed back, because the object may be holding pressure on a torn vessel. Closure and the decisions about infection and tetanus belong to the operating room and to the prescriber.

The pelvis rarely travels alone, and the responder should expect more than one injury. The bladder and the urethra may be torn, the rectum and the vagina may be opened, the vessels and the nerve roots that leave the pelvis may be damaged, and the spine, the thigh bone, the chest, the abdomen and the head may be injured in the same event. A fracture of the thigh bone on the same side increases the loss of blood and changes the way the limb lies. The responder notices, records and reports each finding in the order in which the patient was examined. Treating the obvious wound while the hidden bleeding continues is the commonest way to lose a patient who arrived talking.

Some groups of patients need a lower threshold for suspicion and for referral. An older adult whose bone has lost density may break the pelvis in a fall from standing height, and that break may be stable enough to be treated without an operation, so an event that looks minor to a young responder may be serious for that patient. A child has a more elastic pelvis, so the ring bends before it breaks, and a pelvic fracture in a child is rare and usually means very great energy and other injuries as well. A pregnant patient carries two lives, and the uterus, the placenta and the position of the patient belong to the clinician and to the local protocol, while the responder protects, warms and transports. The sequence of care does not change, but the degree of concern does.

Pain in a pelvic fracture is a finding, a burden and a risk, because pain drives the pulse, the breathing and the movement that the injury cannot afford. Relieving pain is part of the care of the patient, and the decision about how to relieve it belongs to the clinician or to the paramedic authorized by the local protocol. The responder who may not give a medicine still helps, by supporting the limb, by reducing movement, by keeping the patient warm and by watching for a change. The name of the substance, the amount and the way it is given are set by the local protocol and by the prescriber, and no listener should be able to take a prescription from this course.

Communication is part of the treatment, and it begins before the patient reaches the hospital. The emergency medical technician or the paramedic sends a pre-alert according to the local protocol, naming the mechanism, the time of the injury, the suspected pelvic fracture, the vital signs, the findings before and after each intervention and the treatment given. The receiving team prepares the room, the blood, the imaging and the operating room while the vehicle is still moving, and that preparation depends on a clear message. The nurse who receives the patient repeats the story back and records it, and the physician decides the next steps. Handover is given in a fixed order, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one.

The scope of practice for every act has to be said plainly, because the law and the training differ between countries and services. The lay first aider supports the limb, covers a wound, keeps the patient warm and calls for help, and does nothing that needs equipment or authorization. The trained first responder and the paramedic immobilize, apply the devices their service authorizes, monitor and report, and they act within the procedures written for them. The nurse observes, records, reassesses and gives the care that the nursing protocol allows. The physician examines, orders imaging and decides about transfer, while the orthopedic surgeon and the interventional radiologist perform the definitive procedures.

Every number in this chapter is an illustration and not a rule. The volume of blood that can be lost into the pelvis, the time within which a patient must reach an operating room, the pressure at which a device is applied and the interval between observations differ between services, countries and editions, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is used. A figure remembered from a book and applied in a different service can lead to a device that is too tight, a transfer that is too late or an observation that is never made. What does not differ is the order of the work, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively.

Stability of the pelvic ring is a judgment about which parts have moved and whether the ring can still carry the weight of the body. A break in one part with the rest of the ring intact behaves differently from a break that opens the ring at two points and lets the halves move independently. The clinician makes that judgment from the examination and the images, and it decides whether the patient needs an operation, how soon, and where. The responder cannot make that judgment and does not need to, because the safe course is the same in every case. The pelvis is treated as unstable, protected from movement, kept warm and moved toward a team that can decide.

A constructed illustration may make the order of the work easier to hold in mind, and the patient in it is imagined rather than drawn from any real case. A person is struck by a vehicle and lies on the road, awake but restless and pale, with pain in the groin and a leg that will not move. The responder checks the scene, protects against blood and moves through the primary survey, and in the step for circulation notices the pelvis. The pelvis is not sprung, the wound at the perineum is covered, the patient is kept flat and warm, and the neurovascular status of both feet is recorded before anything is applied. The ring is supported with the device the service authorizes, the findings before and after are written down, and the patient is transferred to a trauma team with the message that a pelvic fracture is suspected. Nothing required the responder to decide whether the ring was stable, and nothing was done twice.

When a complication is suspected, the response is to stop and to escalate rather than to press on. Force is not a technique, and a maneuver that meets resistance has already answered the question it was asked. A constricting bandage, splint or cast is released within the limits of the local protocol by a person authorized to release it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is kept warm and referred urgently, because the diagnosis of a compartment syndrome is clinical and time is muscle. The same principle applies to the pelvis, where a patient bleeding behind the peritoneum cannot be helped by more pressure from outside but only by a team that can stop the source.

Pelvic fractures teach a lesson that runs through the whole of musculoskeletal care, which is that the bone is often the least dangerous part of the injury. A broken ring may hold a clot that a single careless movement destroys, a wound that looks small may communicate with the bowel, and a patient who is talking may be losing blood into a space that no one can see. The value of the responder lies in recognizing the pattern early, in protecting the patient from movement and from cold, in recording what was found before and after every act, and in handing the patient to people who can stop the bleeding. Recognition, protection, record and referral are four words that carry most of the work.

To summarize, a pelvic fracture is a bleeding injury before it is a bone injury, because the pelvis is a ring with great vessels, wide veins and a large hidden space behind the peritoneum, and the loss can continue without a mark on the skin. The pelvis belongs to the primary survey, and a patient with a mechanism of high energy, pain in the groin, the hip or the perineum, or an inability to bear weight is treated as having a broken pelvis until a qualified clinician has decided otherwise. The responder never springs the pelvis, never asks the patient to stand or to lift a leg, never straightens a deformed limb, never pushes bone back into a wound and never removes an embedded object, because each of those acts can restart bleeding or damage a vessel or a nerve. The order of the work is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, with the neurovascular findings recorded before and after every intervention. Warmth, restraint, documentation and a clear handover to a trauma team are the contribution of the responder, and every act is named for the level of practice that is allowed to perform it. Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and no medicine and no dose is given in this course, because the substance and the amount belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.
