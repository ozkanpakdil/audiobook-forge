# Chapter 26 — Hip Fractures and Hip Dislocation

A fracture of the hip is one of the injuries most likely to end the independence of an older adult, and it is one of the few fractures whose outcome is decided as much by how the patient is handled in the first hours as by the operation that follows. The hip is a deep ball and socket joint, and the bone that breaks most often in this region is the femur, the long bone of the thigh, at the point where its head meets its shaft. A hip dislocation, in which the head of the femur leaves the socket altogether, is far less common and far more urgent, because the blood supply of the head runs along the bone and can be torn by the displacement itself. Every chapter of this course follows one order, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, and this chapter follows that order for both injuries. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

The hip joint is formed by the rounded head of the femur sitting in a cup-shaped hollow of the pelvis called the acetabulum, and the fit is deep and stable because the socket surrounds more than half of the ball. Strong ligaments spiral around the joint and the capsule that encloses it is thick, which is why the hip dislocates only under great force while the shoulder, with its shallow socket, dislocates easily. The neck of the femur angles from the head to the shaft, and the blood that keeps the head alive travels along that neck in small vessels that run beneath the capsule. Those vessels are already stretched and compressed in a displaced fracture of the neck, and they are stretched again when the head leaves the socket. The head of the bone has almost no blood supply of its own, so when the vessels along the neck are torn the bone dies, a process called avascular necrosis, and that is why both injuries in this chapter are treated as urgent.

Hip fractures are grouped by where the break lies in relation to the capsule, and the grouping matters because the blood supply of the head is at risk in one group and not in the other. A break of the neck of the femur, just below the head, is called intracapsular because it lies inside the capsule, and it interrupts the vessels that run along the neck, so the head may lose its supply. A break between the neck and the shaft, through the prominences called the trochanters, is called intertrochanteric, and it lies outside the capsule, where the bone is rich in blood and heals more readily. A break just below the trochanters is called subtrochanteric, and it usually follows greater force and is more likely to bleed. The examiner in the field does not make this distinction, because it belongs to imaging and to the orthopedic surgeon, but the distinction explains why one phrase, a broken hip, describes two injuries with different futures.

The patient who most often breaks a hip is an older adult whose bone has lost density, and the injury usually follows a fall from standing height or less. The bone of an older person is thinner and more brittle, and the muscles that would once have absorbed the fall are weaker, so a force that would leave a younger person bruised snaps the femoral neck or the trochanteric region. In a younger patient the same fracture follows a road crash, a fall from height or a bicycle injury, and it may be accompanied by other injuries that are more immediately dangerous, so a break of the hip in a young patient raises the suspicion of greater force and of injury elsewhere. A constructed illustration may help: an older woman who cannot rise after a fall in her kitchen and a young cyclist thrown over the handlebars may both arrive with a broken hip, and both are handled in the same order.

The findings that suggest a hip fracture are pain in the groin, the thigh or the knee, an inability to bear weight, and a limb that lies shortened and rotated outward, with the knee and the foot turned away from the other side. Pain on any movement of the hip is common, and the patient often holds the leg still and resists any attempt to move it. The knee may hurt more than the hip, because the nerve that supplies the hip also supplies the knee, so a patient who complains only of knee pain after a fall still needs the hip examined by a clinician. These signs are helpful, and none of them is reliable enough to build a decision on by itself. A fracture that is impacted, with one broken surface driven into the other, may allow the patient to walk, and a patient with dementia may not report pain at all. The consequence for the listener is a low threshold, so that any older adult who falls and cannot walk as before is treated as having a hip injury until someone qualified has excluded it.

Assessment begins before the limb, with the scene, the mechanism of injury and the primary survey, whose steps are remembered as ABCDE, meaning airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. An older patient lying on a hard floor after a fall may be cold, frightened, dehydrated and in pain, and the fall may have been caused by an illness rather than by an accident, so the responder asks what happened before the patient reached the ground. Life comes before limb, and when one pair of hands must choose, the airway, the breathing and the circulation are dealt with first while the hip is supported in the position found. The lay first aider recognizes the injury, calls for help, keeps the patient warm and does not move the patient unless the scene is unsafe. The trained responder and the paramedic examine within the procedures their service authorizes, and the physician or the orthopedic surgeon decides what is done next. A patient who is drowsy, whose pulse is weak and rapid or whose skin is cold and pale sends the responder back to the beginning of the survey.

The history that matters for a hip fracture is short and practical. The responder tries to learn the time of the fall, the height of the fall, what the patient was doing, whether the patient could stand afterwards and how long the patient has been lying there, because each of those answers changes the urgency. The medicines the patient takes, the illnesses the patient lives with, whether the patient walks with a frame or a stick, and where the patient lives and with whom all belong to the story, because they decide where the patient should be taken and how much support will be needed afterwards. Pain is assessed by asking, by looking at the face and the posture and by noticing which movement the patient avoids, and it is recorded rather than estimated silently. Constructed examples in this course are illustrations only, and no real patient is described.

Stabilization of a suspected hip fracture in the field is a small set of acts, and restraint is the most important of them. The limb is supported in the position in which it was found and is never straightened, because the vessels and the nerves lie against the bone and because pulling the limb may turn a closed fracture into an open one. The leg is not pulled, not rotated, not measured against the other leg again and again, and not lifted by the ankle, and nobody tests how far the hip will move. The lay first aider supports the leg with the hands, with a pillow or with a rolled blanket, keeps the patient warm and waits for help. The trained responder and the paramedic may apply a splint or a binding within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes, and the physician decides what immobilization is needed for the journey. Cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort, so the patient is insulated from the ground and wet clothing is replaced, since an older patient loses heat quickly and cools further while lying still.

The neurovascular examination is performed before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is recorded, because a finding that was not written before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The examiner looks at the color and the temperature of the foot, feels the pulses at the ankle and the foot, presses briefly on a nail to watch the color return, and asks the patient to move the ankle and the toes. Sensation is tested on both sides and compared, and numbness, tingling or a movement that has been lost is recorded in the words of the patient as far as possible. The lay first aider observes color, warmth and the ability of the patient to move and to feel, and reports any change, while the formal examination belongs to the trained responder or the clinician. One limit must be said plainly: this examination describes the limb below the injury, and no bedside test shows whether the blood supply of the femoral head has survived, which is why a displaced break of the neck and every hip dislocation are threats to the head until imaging and the surgeon say otherwise.

Immobilization means preventing movement of the injured part and of the joints above and below it, so far as that can be done without force. In a suspected hip fracture the limb is supported from the hip to the foot, a pad is placed between the knees, and the leg is held in the position found until a stretcher is ready. A splint is padded where it presses on bone, is applied over the position found, and is secured firmly enough to hold without stopping the circulation, and an improvised device must be checked more often because it shifts. The trained responder and the paramedic apply splints and bindings within the equipment and the procedures their service authorizes. Traction, which is a gentle continuous pull applied along the limb to hold the broken ends in line and to reduce spasm, is sometimes used while a patient waits for surgery, and it is applied only by a clinician acting under local protocol, because the pull, the counter-pull, the padding and the skin all need supervision. Traction is not a technique to be learned from a book, and this course does not teach it, nor does it teach casting, nor does it teach reduction.

Imaging is the step that confirms what the examination has already suggested, and it is ordered by a clinician trained to interpret it. A radiograph is usually the first study, and for a suspected hip fracture it is taken in at least two directions so that a break in one plane is not hidden, with the joint above and below included so that a second injury is not missed. Computed tomography gives more detail of a fracture around the socket or of a break that is hard to see, and it is also used to plan an operation. Magnetic resonance imaging shows bone and soft tissues together and can reveal a fracture of the neck that a radiograph did not show, which matters in a patient who cannot bear weight and whose first picture looked normal. The lay first aider never orders or interprets imaging, and the trained responder does not decide which study is needed, because that decision belongs to the clinician responsible for the patient. A normal radiograph does not exclude a hip fracture, so a patient who cannot walk as before is kept still and referred even when the picture is reported as normal.

Reassessment happens after every intervention and is the step that catches the complication caused by the treatment. The same observations are repeated in the same order and compared with the record, because the comparison matters more than any single measurement. The limb is checked for color, warmth, pulse, sensation, movement and pain, the splint is checked for tightness, and the skin over the heel and the sacrum is checked for damage, since an older patient who lies still can be injured by the surface itself. Pain that increases instead of settling, pain out of proportion to the injury, numbness, tingling, weakness and a limb that becomes tense and swollen are the findings that stop the responder and prompt escalation. Compartment syndrome is uncommon in the thigh but it happens, and a responder who suspects it does not wait for confirmation: within the limits of local protocol a constricting bandage or splint is released by someone authorized to do it, the limb is not elevated above the level of the heart, and the patient is kept warm and referred urgently, because the diagnosis is clinical and time is muscle. A change in the level of consciousness, in the breathing or in the pulse is also a reason to stop and escalate, since a hip fracture in an older patient may be only one part of a larger illness.

Referral or definitive treatment is the last step of the sequence, and for a hip fracture it almost always begins with a call to a hospital that has an orthopedic surgeon. A patient with a displaced break of the femoral neck, a fracture broken into several pieces, a suspected dislocation, an open wound, or signs of damage to a nerve or a vessel is referred urgently, and the reason is that the period in which surgery gives the best result is short, with the first 24 to 48 hours offered in teaching as an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since practice differs between countries and editions. Definitive treatment is decided by the orthopedic surgeon and may be a surgical fixation, in which metal devices hold the bone while it heals, or a replacement of the joint, in which the damaged head and sometimes the socket are exchanged for an artificial one. Some patients are treated without an operation because surgery is too dangerous for them, and that decision belongs to the clinical team. Reduction, traction, casting and every operation are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and none of them can be learned from a book.

A hip dislocation is a different injury, and it is rare because the socket is deep. The head of the femur is driven out of the acetabulum by great force, most often in a road crash when the knee strikes the dashboard or in a fall from a height, and the head usually comes to rest behind the socket, which is called a posterior dislocation. Less often it comes to rest in front of the socket, which is called an anterior dislocation. In the common posterior dislocation the limb is shortened, the knee is bent and the leg is turned inward toward the other leg, while in the anterior dislocation the limb is held out to the side and turned outward. The findings are taught as typical and not as certain, because a fracture of the socket or of the head can change them, and an examiner who expects one attitude may not see the other. The sciatic nerve, which passes behind the hip, is the nerve most often injured, and its damage shows as numbness or weakness below the knee, so the neurovascular examination before and after any intervention is the record that matters. A dislocation of the hip is often accompanied by a fracture of the acetabulum or of the femoral head, by a knee injury or by a head injury that takes priority.

A dislocated hip is an emergency for the blood supply of the femoral head, and the reason is anatomical. The vessels that feed the head run along the neck, and when the head leaves the socket those vessels are stretched and compressed, so the bone is starved of blood from the moment of the injury until the joint is put back. If the head is starved long enough it dies, and the dead bone collapses months or years later, which is the outcome called avascular necrosis, and the risk rises with every hour of delay. The responder's part is precise and limited: recognize the injury, keep the limb supported in the position found, record the neurovascular status, keep the patient warm and arrange the fastest safe transfer to a facility with an orthopedic surgeon. The responder does not attempt to put the joint back, does not pull on the limb, does not straighten it and does not test the joint. Reduction of a dislocated hip is performed by a clinician, usually under sedation or anesthesia in a controlled setting, and the substance, the amount and the route belong to the prescriber and to the local protocol, so no listener can take a regimen from this course.

There are situations in which a reduction must not be attempted at all, and the reasons are worth carrying because force applied in the wrong place can convert an injury that would have healed into one that cannot. An attempt is not made when a complex fracture and dislocation are suspected together, when a fragment of bone is caught between the joint surfaces, when the vessels or the nerves are failing and need a specialist, when the injury is open, when the diagnosis is unclear, when the person present lacks the training or the equipment, or when an earlier attempt has already failed. In each of those situations the correct act is to stop, to immobilize the limb in the position found, to record what was found before and after, and to move the patient to someone qualified to decide. Resistance is a signal to stop and not a problem to be overcome, because force is not a technique, and the rule protects the patient from an act that only felt helpful.

Some patients need a lower threshold than others. A child with hip pain who refuses to walk needs urgent assessment by a clinician, because several conditions in a child's hip present in the same way, and the examination and the imaging belong to the clinician. A patient whose hip has already been replaced can dislocate it with an ordinary movement such as bending to pick something from the floor, and the findings may be milder, so a replaced hip that becomes painful and loses its range of movement is treated as a dislocation until someone qualified has excluded it. A patient with dementia may be unable to report pain, and the responder then relies on what is seen, on the position of the limb and on the account of whoever found the patient. A patient who has fallen and cannot get up may have lain there for hours, and cold, dehydration and pressure damage may be more urgent than the fracture itself. A patient with a hip injury can also have an injury of the spine or of the pelvis, and the pelvis and the spine are each treated as unstable until they are cleared by someone qualified to clear them.

Handling and transport decide whether the work done before is preserved. The patient with a suspected hip fracture is moved by a trained team using the equipment and the precautions the local protocol requires, with the limb supported and the body kept in line, and no lone manual handling technique is safe however strong the responder is. A scoop stretcher, a vacuum mattress or a long board may be used, and which one is chosen belongs to the service, since a suspected injury of the spine or of the pelvis changes the plan. The lay first aider does not lift the patient unless the scene is unsafe, and waits with the patient for the team. The limb is supported through every transfer so that the broken ends do not move, and the leg is never used as a handle. The patient is covered and insulated from the ground, because cold is a complication of trauma and pain increases with it. The handover then names the time of the injury, the mechanism, the position in which the limb was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention and any change in the condition of the patient, so that the receiving team continues a story instead of starting a new one.

The complications of a hip fracture begin before the operation and continue long after it, which is why the injury is treated as an emergency rather than as a nuisance. Avascular necrosis of the femoral head can follow a displaced break of the neck or a dislocation and may not show itself for months, and nonunion, in which the broken ends fail to join, is more likely when the blood supply was damaged. Blood collects around the fracture and inside the thigh, and a hip fracture can lose more blood than a listener expects, with teaching figures offered as illustrations to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because estimates differ between services and editions. An older patient who lies still after the injury is at risk of pressure sores, of a clot in the deep veins of the leg and of its passage to the lungs, of chest infection and of confusion, and those complications are prevented by early assessment, careful handling, warmth, pain relief decided by a clinician and above all by early surgery and early movement. The risk of death in the year after a hip fracture is high enough that it must be said aloud, and it is one more reason why the injury is never dismissed as a simple break in an old person.

Pain relief in a suspected hip fracture is decided by a clinician, and the responder's contribution is to handle the limb gently, to immobilize it and to describe the pain clearly enough for someone else to act. The finding that makes the decision urgent is pain that is severe, that rises instead of settling or that comes with numbness, tingling, weakness or a limb that becomes tense, because those suggest a complication rather than ordinary injury pain. Analgesia, antibiotics, tetanus protection and sedation are prescribed by those whom the law and the local protocol authorize to prescribe them, and the substance, the amount, the route and the frequency are theirs to choose; this course gives no dose, no route and no regimen, and a listener must not be able to take a prescription from it. The lay first aider gives nothing unless the local protocol and a competent authority have trained and authorized that act. What the responder can always give is warmth, reassurance, a supported limb and a calm voice, and those are not small things to a patient who is frightened, in pain and unable to move.

The record and the handover are part of the treatment rather than a formality that follows it. The time of the injury, the mechanism, the position of the limb when it was found, the neurovascular findings before and after every intervention, the level of consciousness and any change in the condition are written or spoken in a fixed order. Nothing that was not recorded before a splint was applied can be attributed afterwards, and nothing that was not reported can be acted on by the team that receives the patient. Where this chapter names a time, a volume, a pressure or a distance, it names an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between services, countries and editions. Every skill described here is learned with a teacher, practiced on models and on each other, and certified by an authority that also defines what may be done without supervision. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which you work.

To summarize, a hip fracture is a break of the femur at or near the joint, and in an older patient it may follow a fall from standing height while in a younger patient it follows great force. What to look for is pain in the groin, the thigh or the knee, an inability to bear weight and a limb that lies shortened and rotated outward, remembering that an impacted fracture may still allow walking and that a patient with dementia may report nothing at all. What to do follows the order this course uses everywhere, which is to assess, to stabilize, to examine the neurovascular status, to immobilize, to image, to reassess, and then to refer or to treat definitively, with the findings written before and after every intervention. The limb is supported in the position found, the patient is kept warm, the neurovascular status is recorded and the patient is moved promptly to a facility with imaging and an orthopedic surgeon. What never to do is to straighten the limb, to pull on it, to lift the patient by the leg, to test how far the hip will move, or to remove an embedded object. A hip dislocation is rare and is an emergency for the blood supply of the femoral head, and the responder recognizes it, supports the limb, records the neurovascular findings and transfers the patient urgently, but never attempts to put the joint back, because reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol, and the technique belongs to that training and to no book. Who to call is the emergency service and the receiving hospital, and the handover names the time, the mechanism, the position found and every change since.
