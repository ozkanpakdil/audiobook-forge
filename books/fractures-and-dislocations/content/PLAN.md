# Fractures and Dislocations — Style Contract

This file is the binding contract for every chapter of *Fractures and Dislocations — A
Practical Course for Health Professionals*. It overrides habits, preferences, and
anything else you have read. Each chapter is written by a different author and read
aloud by the same voice, so the rules below are what make seventy chapters sound like
one course.

## What this book is

A course in the assessment, stabilization, initial management and definitive-treatment
principles of fractures and dislocations throughout the body, written to be listened to
rather than read. It is educational material for health professionals, students and
trained responders. It does not replace training, certification, supervision, local
protocol, or the law of the country in which the listener works, and every chapter must
say so in its own words at least once. The course has sixty-six chapters and four
appendices. It runs from the foundations of musculoskeletal trauma, through assessment
and immobilization, region by region from the shoulder girdle to the toes, through the
spine, the child, the older adult, the open fracture, dislocations and reduction,
complications, imaging, definitive treatment and rehabilitation, and closes with
emergency scenarios, a skills laboratory, a decision framework, and the appendices.

The listener cannot see a radiograph, a diagram, a splint, or a page it can turn back
to. Everything the course teaches has to be said in words, in an order that can be
followed while driving, and it has to be said with a reason attached, because a rule
without a reason is forgotten and then broken.

## The one shape every chapter follows

Every injury in this course is taught through the same sequence, and every chapter must
name it and follow it:

**Assess, stabilize, examine neurovascular status, immobilize, image, reassess, refer or
treat definitively.**

Two of those steps are non-negotiable everywhere in the book. The neurovascular
examination is done before any intervention and repeated after it, and its result is
recorded, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be
attributed afterwards. The reassessment happens after every intervention, because the
complication this course is most afraid of is the one caused by the treatment.

## Contract of style

- One heading per file, exactly the heading given in the chapter brief, in the form
  `# Chapter N — Title`, or `# Appendix A — Title` for an appendix. Nothing else in the
  file may be a heading, and there are no sub-headings anywhere.
- Sections are not marked by headings. They are marked by paragraph breaks and by the
  logic of the prose, so the movement from recognition to assessment to management to
  referral must be carried by the sentences themselves.
- Paragraphs of four to eight sentences, separated by one blank line. A paragraph longer
  than eight sentences must be split.
- The last paragraph of the chapter begins with exactly `To summarize, ` and carries no
  heading. It restates the few things a listener must keep: what to look for, what to do,
  what never to do, and who to call.
- Prose only. No bullet points, no numbered lists, no tables, no flowcharts, no arrows,
  no markdown emphasis, no footnotes, no citations, and no web addresses.
- Anything the source presents as a list or an algorithm must be written out as ordered
  prose: "the first step is ... the next is ... only then ...". The listener has nothing
  to look at, so the order must be in the words.
- Never refer to a figure, a table, a box, a diagram or a previous chapter. A chapter is
  listened to on its own.
- Vary the openings of paragraphs and the shape of sentences. Sixty-six sections that
  each began the same way would be heard as one endless section.
- Never start a line with a numeral followed by a full stop, because the build check
  reads that as a numbered list.

## Scope of practice and safety rules

These rules are not decoration. A course that ignores them can kill someone who follows
it.

- **Name the provider level for every intervention.** Say who may do it: the lay first
  aider, the trained first responder, the emergency medical technician or paramedic, the
  nurse, the physician, the orthopedic surgeon. Where an act requires equipment,
  authorization or a protocol, say so in the same breath.
- **Reduction, traction and casting are learned under supervision and performed
  according to local protocol.** No chapter may teach a listener to perform them from a
  book. Describe what they are, why they are done, what they are meant to achieve, what
  can go wrong, and who is qualified to do them, and say plainly that the technique
  itself belongs to supervised clinical training.
- **Never straighten a deformed limb in the field.** It is splinted in the position found,
  and the reason is the risk to the vessels and nerves that lie against the bone.
- **Never remove an embedded object**, and never push exposed bone back into a wound. It
  is covered, stabilized in place, and the patient is moved with it.
- **Never blindly explore an open wound.** Cover it with a sterile dressing, control the
  bleeding that can be controlled, examine the neurovascular status, immobilize, and
  refer urgently. Definitive closure belongs to the operating theatre and to nobody else.
- **Do not attempt reduction when the source lists a contraindication**: a suspected
  complex fracture-dislocation, entrapped fracture fragments, significant neurovascular
  compromise needing specialist intervention, an open injury, an unclear diagnosis,
  inadequate training or equipment, or a previous failed attempt. State the reason, not
  only the rule.
- **Stop if there is resistance or a complication.** Force is not a technique.
- **Reassess after every intervention**, and say what would make the responder stop and
  escalate.
- **Suspect compartment syndrome and act on it.** The constricting bandage, splint or cast
  is released within the limits of local protocol, the limb is not elevated above the
  level of the heart, and the patient is referred urgently, because the diagnosis is
  clinical and time is muscle.
- **No drug dose, no regimen, no route, no frequency.** Where analgesia, antibiotics,
  tetanus protection or sedation must be discussed, describe who decides, what finding
  makes the decision urgent, what the scope of practice forbids, and that the substance
  and the amount belong to the local protocol and the prescriber. A listener must not be
  able to take a prescription from this book.
- **Spinal injury is treated as unstable until it is cleared by someone qualified to clear
  it**, and no chapter may describe a lone manual handling technique as safe.
- **Every number is an illustration.** A time, a pressure, a temperature, a distance or a
  threshold named in this book must be followed by the reminder that it must be confirmed
  against the current guideline and the local protocol, and the reason is that such
  numbers differ between services, countries and editions. Vary the wording; never paste
  the same sentence sixty-six times.
- **Protect against hypothermia** wherever a patient is injured, immobilized, exposed or
  waiting, because cold is a complication of trauma and not a detail of comfort.
- No real patient, no identifiable case, and no institutional detail. Any example must be
  a constructed illustration and must be described as one.

## Numbers, units and symbols

- Numbers are digits. Units are written out in full: "5 centimeters", "12 millimeters",
  "70 beats per minute".
- Never a unit abbreviation: not cm, mm, kg, mL, mg, or any other.
- Ordinals in words: "the second week", "the fourth cranial nerve". Never a Roman numeral
  standing alone and never a figure such as "2nd". The build check reads IV, IM and PO as
  abbreviated drug routes, so those tokens never appear — the cranial nerves are named by
  ordinal word.
- Symbols are never used: no per cent sign, equals, plus, greater than, less than, arrow,
  degree sign, ampersand, or slash used to mean "or". Write "per cent", "equals", "leads
  to", "greater than", "less than", "and".
- Fractions and ranges as words: "one half", "one third", "8 to 12 weeks".

## Acronyms, short forms and spelling

A synthesized voice reads an uppercase token as a sequence of letters, which is sometimes
what a clinician would say and sometimes nonsense.

- The only short forms this course tolerates are **CT**, **MRI**, **CPR**, **PPE**, **ICU**
  and **ABCDE**.
- Spell everything else out at its first mention in every chapter, and prefer the full
  form throughout: "computed tomography", "magnetic resonance imaging", "anterior cruciate
  ligament", "deep vein thrombosis", "open reduction and internal fixation", "range of
  motion", "personal protective equipment", "intensive care unit". A chapter is listened
  to on its own, so the full term is spelled out again in each chapter, however often it
  appeared in the one before.
- Never write CNS, PNS, CSF, DVT, PE, ORIF, ROM, ACL, PCL, MCL, LCL, IV, IM, PO, or any
  other invented short form.
- Use AMERICAN spelling throughout, never British: "hemorrhage", "edema", "anemia",
  "tumor", "color", "behavior", "center", "defense", "pediatric", "fetal", "fibers",
  "esophagus", "anesthesia", "gray matter", "mold", "orthopedic".

## Accuracy rules

- Every statement must be correct and current, and where practice differs between
  countries and editions, say that it differs rather than picking one version as
  universal.
- Describe findings, not diagnoses: what the responder sees, feels and is told, and what
  that finding means.
- Where a sign is unreliable in a particular patient, say so. A chapter that teaches a
  sign as always present will be believed.
- Constructed examples are illustrations and must be described as illustrations.

## Length

- Chapters run between three thousand eight hundred and four thousand four hundred words.
  Appendices run between two thousand two hundred and three thousand two hundred words. A
  little over is fine; badly under is not. The build check fails below three thousand two
  hundred words for a chapter and below one thousand eight hundred for an appendix.
- Write the file once, carefully. Do not rewrite, restructure, or trim it afterwards.
