# Fractures and Dislocations — A Practical Course for Health Professionals

This course sets out a systematic approach to the assessment, stabilization, initial management and definitive-treatment principles of fractures and dislocations anywhere in the body, written to be listened to rather than read. It is made for health professionals, students and trained responders: for the paramedic on the way to a call, the nurse taking a handover, the student revising before an examination, and the physician who wants the first ten minutes of an orthopedic problem clear in the mind. It does not replace training, certification, supervision, local protocol, or the law of the country in which the listener works.

Every injury in the course is taught through the same sequence, and the sequence is worth carrying away on its own: assess the patient, stabilize what threatens life, examine the neurovascular status, immobilize the injury in the position found, obtain the imaging that is indicated, reassess after everything you have done, and then either refer the patient onward or treat the injury definitively within your own scope of practice. Two of those steps are repeated until they become instinct. The neurovascular examination is done before any intervention and again after it, and its result is written down, because a finding that was not recorded before a splint was applied cannot be attributed afterwards. The reassessment happens after every intervention, because the complication this course fears most is the one caused by the treatment.

The course runs from the foundations of musculoskeletal trauma, through the primary and secondary survey and the neurovascular examination, and on to immobilization, splinting and patient handling. It then works region by region: the shoulder girdle and shoulder, the humerus, the elbow and forearm, the wrist and hand, the pelvis and hip, the femur, the knee, the leg, the ankle and the foot. It goes on to the spine, the child, the older adult, and the open fracture, then to dislocations and the principles of reduction, to complications, to imaging, and to definitive treatment and rehabilitation. It closes with a set of emergency scenarios, a practical skills laboratory, a decision framework that can be run in the head at the roadside, and four appendices: an algorithm section, a skills checklist, a quick-reference card, and the core principle of the whole course.

Three warnings belong at the front rather than at the back. The first is that reduction, traction and casting are techniques learned under supervision and performed according to local protocol. This course describes what they are for, what they must achieve and what can go wrong, and it says plainly that the technique itself belongs to supervised clinical training and never to a book. The second is that this course gives no drug, no dose, no route and no frequency. Where analgesia, antibiotics, tetanus protection or sedation must be discussed, it describes who decides, what finding makes the decision urgent, and what the scope of practice forbids; the substance and the amount belong to the local protocol and the prescriber. The third is that any number in this course is an illustration. Times, pressures, distances and thresholds differ between services, countries and editions, and every one of them must be confirmed against the current guideline and the protocol of the service you work in.

The injuries in this course do not sort themselves by severity. A fracture that looks trivial can hide a threatened limb, and a limb that looks ruined can often be saved when the responder protects it, controls what is bleeding, records what the nerves and vessels were doing, immobilizes it in the position found, and gets the patient to the right place quickly. That is the whole skill of this subject, and it is more about restraint than about force. What kills and what costs limbs in the first hours is rarely a lack of sophistication; it is a missed compartment, a splint applied over a numb hand, a deformed limb straightened for tidiness, an open wound explored out of curiosity, or a transfer delayed because a finding was never written down.

This is educational material. It does not replace supervised practice, a training program, a certification, or the judgment of a clinician who can examine the patient, and constructed examples used for teaching are illustrations rather than real cases. Used as a companion to proper teaching and to the protocol of your own service, it is a way to keep a difficult subject calm, ordered and close at hand.
