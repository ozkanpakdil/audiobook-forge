# Clinical Diagnosis — Style Contract

This file is the binding contract for every section of *Clinical Diagnosis: A
Practical Manual for History, Examination and Differential Reasoning*. It overrides
habits, preferences, and anything else you have read. Each section is written by a
different author and read aloud by the same voice, so the rules below are what make
seventy-seven chapters sound like one book.

## What this book is

A narrated manual for the person who has to work out what is wrong with a patient:
the student at the bedside, the house officer on the ward round, the clinical officer
in a district hospital, the nurse practitioner in a clinic with no laboratory, and the
experienced clinician who wants to revisit the ground he or she learned quickly and
never consolidated. It teaches the reasoning rather than the disease list: how a
history is taken, how an examination is performed and what each sign means, how the
findings are assembled into a differential diagnosis that is ranked by both likelihood
and danger, how tests change that ranking, and how the whole process is done when the
tests do not exist.

Every subject is treated at two levels. First the **principle**: why the question is
asked, why the sign appears, what the finding does to the probability of disease, and
what the listener should be thinking while the patient is talking or the hand is on the
abdomen. Then the **practice**: what is actually said, done, looked for, and written
down, in a well-resourced hospital and again in a clinic that has a stethoscope, a
thermometer, a torch, and no imaging. The comparison is deliberate, because the same
diagnostic problem is solved with very different tools depending on where the patient
lies, and the reasoning is what transfers between them.

It is written to be **listened to**. The listener may be walking to a ward, driving
between clinics, or resting between shifts. Nothing can be re-read, looked up, or
highlighted. If a sentence only works when seen on a page, it does not belong in this
book. Where a diagram, a table or a photograph would help on a page, this book must
describe the thing in words precisely enough that the listener can picture it.

## Contract of style

- One heading per section, exactly the heading given in the syllabus below. Nothing
  else may be a heading.
- Prose only. No bullet points, no numbered lists, no tables, no markdown emphasis, no
  footnotes, no citations, no web addresses, no references to figures or tables.
- Paragraphs of four to eight sentences, separated by one blank line. The final
  paragraph begins with exactly `To summarize, ` and carries no heading.
- Explain the reasoning before the maneuvere. A listener who understands why a sign is
  sought will notice it, and will know what its absence means.
- Length: chapters run between three thousand eight hundred and four thousand four
  hundred words. Appendices run between two thousand two hundred and three thousand
  two hundred words. A little over is fine; badly under is not.
- Write the file once, carefully. Do not rewrite, restructure, or trim it afterwards.
- Open each chapter by naming the subject and the reasoning that organizes it, and
  close it with the paragraph that begins `To summarize, `, which must carry the same
  clinical caution as the rest. Vary openings and structures between chapters: this is
  a book of eighty-two sections, and eighty-two openings of the same shape would be
  heard as one.

## Clinical safety rules

This is educational material. It is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is
not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is
actually with the patient. State that plainly in the first chapter and in every
appendix, and assume it everywhere else.

- **No drug doses.** This is a book about diagnosis. Do not give a dose, a regimen, a
  rate, or a duration of treatment for any drug. Where treatment must be mentioned to
  make the reasoning clear, name the decision rather than the drug: not what to give
  and how much, but what finding would make treatment the right answer, and what
  finding would make it the wrong one.
- **No threshold presented as universal.** A score, a cut-off, a reference range, a
  pressure, a temperature, a rate, or a duration named in this book is an illustration
  of reasoning, and the listener must be told so at the point where it appears. Follow
  every such number with the reminder that it must be confirmed against the current
  guideline and the local protocol, because the numbers differ between countries,
  between editions, and between the laboratory and the ward. Write that reminder in
  varied words rather than the same sentence eighty-two times.
- **Diagnose the patient, not the pattern.** Where a framework is given, say what it
  excludes, what it assumes, and the population it was derived in. A rule validated in
  one setting can mislead in another.
- **Name the dangerous possibility early.** In any chapter where a presentation has a
  lethal cause, that cause is named where the presentation is described, together with
  the finding that would reveal it, rather than deferred to a later chapter. Safety is
  not an appendix.
- No real patient, no identifiable case, and no institutional detail. Where an example
  case is needed, it must be a constructed illustration and must be described as one.
- No claim of accuracy about a guideline, an organization, or a published rule. Where a
  named score or rule is used, describe the reasoning it formalizes, not its exact
  contents, and send the listener to the current source.

## Numbers, units and symbols

- Numbers are digits. Units are always written out in full: "38 degrees Celsius", "110
  beats per minute", "90 millimeters of mercury", "5 millimeters", "two liters",
  "12 hours", "3 days", "1 in 4".
- Never a unit abbreviation: not cm, mm, kg, mL, mg, bpm, mmHg, mmol, mg/dL, g/L. Write
  "millimeters of mercury", "beats per minute", "millimoles per liter".
- Fractions as words: "one half", "one third", "two thirds". Ranges use "to": "10 to 15
  minutes", "36 to 38 degrees Celsius". Ratios are spoken: "one in four", "three times
  more likely".
- Symbols are never used: no per cent sign, equals, plus, minus sign, arrows, degree
  sign, ampersand, greater than, less than, or angle brackets. Write "per cent",
  "equals", "leads to", "degrees", "and", "greater than", "less than".
- Frequencies and routes are written out: "every six hours", "by mouth", "into a vein",
  "into the muscle", "under the skin". Never use the abbreviated forms.
- Centuries and ordinals in words: "the nineteenth century", never "19th". But a named
  score that contains a number is written the way it is spoken: "the Curb sixty-five
  score", "the Ottawa ankle rules", "the Cha two D S two Vasc score".
- Latin letters, ordinary punctuation, and the em dash in the heading only. Never
  Cyrillic, Greek, or mathematical characters.

## Acronyms, short forms and mnemonics

A synthesized voice reads an uppercase token as a sequence of letters. That is
sometimes what a clinician would say, and sometimes nonsense, so the rule depends on
what is spoken aloud.

- **Mnemonics that spell a word are written as words**: the Socrates framework for
  chest pain, the Oldcarts framework for the history of the present illness, the
  Vindicate framework for the differential diagnosis, Soap notes for documentation. Never
  SOCRATES, OLDCARTS, VINDICATE, or SOAP in capitals.
- **Letter sequences that are spoken as letters keep their capital letters**, and this
  book tolerates only these: CT, MRI, ECG, ENT, ICU, CPR, PPE, HIV. Everything else is
  written out in full at every mention: "the Glasgow coma scale", "jugular venous
  pressure", "body mass index", "the quick sequential organ failure assessment",
  "systemic inflammatory response syndrome", "integrated management of childhood
  illness", "polymerase chain reaction" (or PCR if the abbreviation is already spoken),
  "chest radiograph", "tuberculosis", "sexually transmitted infection", "the World
  Health Organization", "the intensive care unit" at first mention and ICU thereafter.
- **Spell the term out at its first mention in each chapter, in full, then use the
  short form freely** where a short form is tolerated at all. A chapter is listened to
  on its own.
- **Never** WHO (write "the World Health Organization"), never TB (write
  "tuberculosis"), never STI on first mention, never BMI without spelling it out, never
  GCS, never JVP, never CXR, never RDT on first mention.
- A named score, rule, or mnemonic is written as its authors are spoken: Wells,
  Glasgow, Centor, Ottawa, Apgar, Curb sixty-five, Vindicate, Socrates, Oldcarts. Do not
  invent a score and do not restate the arithmetic of one.
