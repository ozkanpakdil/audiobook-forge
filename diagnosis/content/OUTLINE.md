# Clinical Diagnosis — Section Briefs

One brief per section. The chapter must cover every point named here, in depth, and
must follow the contract in PLAN.md. Length and format rules live in PLAN.md, not
here.

---

## Chapter 1 — How Diagnosis Works

Why diagnosis is a probative process and not a naming ceremony: pattern recognition against hypothetico-deductive reasoning, and the discomfort that ought to switch a clinician from the first to the second. Probability in plain language, pre-test and post-test odds, likelihood ratios, and the way prevalence sets the ground on which every finding is weighed. The same symptom in a district clinic, an emergency department and a referral hospital. State the reasoning first, then show it performed in a fully equipped hospital and again where the tools are a stethoscope, a torch and a thermometer. Name the atypical presentation of a lethal disease as the case that punishes early closure.

---

## Chapter 2 — Cognitive Biases and Diagnostic Error

How a competent clinician gets it wrong: anchoring and the label that survives every later finding, premature closure, the availability of the last dramatic case, confirmation bias in what is asked and what is noticed, search satisficing after the first abnormality, base-rate neglect, framing by the referral, and overconfidence that outruns accuracy. Separate the cognitive failure from the system failure, and treat disclosure and apology as part of the subject. The debiasing moves must be practical enough to perform at the bedside, and the chapter should show them done on a busy ward round and again alone in a clinic with no colleague to ask. Note that a bias delays a dangerous diagnosis more often than a missing test does.

---

## Chapter 3 — Building a Differential Diagnosis

The frameworks and their honest limits: anatomical, working outward from the structure that hurts; pathophysiological, asking which mechanism could produce the finding; and the Vindicate framework, which forces the listener through vascular, infectious, neoplastic, degenerative, iatrogenic and intoxicated, congenital, autoimmune, traumatic and endocrine categories while threatening to bury the common under the exotic. The can't-miss list is drawn up before the examination and holds the lethal possibilities, among them aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, meningitis, ectopic pregnancy and cord compression. Ranking by likelihood and by danger at once, and the use of age, sex, exposures and setting to move entries. The same list is built with a laboratory downstairs and without one.

---

## Chapter 4 — Using Tests Wisely

A test changes a probability and therefore a decision, and never confirms what is already known. Sensitivity and specificity in plain words as the behavior of the test in the sick and the well; predictive values as properties of the patient and the population rather than the test; likelihood ratios as the practical bridge; the threshold at which testing becomes worthwhile and the threshold at which treating without waiting becomes right. The harms: false positives, cascades, incidental findings, overdiagnosis, spectrum bias, and the reference standard that does not exist. When not to test at all. The reasoning holds whether the laboratory runs every hour or opens twice a week, and the chapter should say what changes and what does not.

---

## Chapter 5 — Diagnosis with Limited Resources

The setting changes the diagnosis and not only the treatment, and a clinician with a torch and a stethoscope is practising a different discipline rather than a degraded one. The history as the highest-yield instrument; the signs worth more than any test; repeating the examination and using time itself as a diagnostic instrument. Clinical scoring rules and syndromic management guidance, what they were derived from and the populations in which they mislead. Empirical treatment, and the diagnostic trial understood honestly as a test whose result arrives after the decision. The working diagnosis named to the patient and recorded, safety-netting when the journey back is long, referral thresholds, and one test used well instead of many used badly.

---

## Chapter 6 — Taking the History

The chief complaint in the patient's own words, and the history of the present illness built from open questions that are narrowed only when the story is complete. The Socrates framework for pain and the Oldcarts framework for the whole illness, described as ways of organizing a story rather than as questionnaires to be recited. The effect of silence, of interruption, and of the question that is asked twice because the first answer was not heard. The reasoning behind each question, then its execution on a quiet ward and in a crowded clinic where three minutes are all there is. Note that the history alone can reveal a dangerous diagnosis, so it must be taken before the examination rather than after it.

---

## Chapter 7 — Past, Family, Social and Medication History

The second half of the history and the one most often skipped: past illnesses and operations, the family pattern that shifts a differential toward inherited disease, occupation and its exposures, travel and residence, animal contact, sexual history, alcohol, tobacco and other substances, allergies and their true nature, and the medicines the patient actually takes rather than the ones on the list. Adherence probed without accusation, and the over-the-counter and traditional remedies that are never volunteered. Show the same ground covered in a hospital where the record precedes the patient and in a clinic where the patient is the only record and must be believed.

---

## Chapter 8 — Communication and Trust

Trust as a diagnostic instrument, because a patient who does not trust the clinician withholds the detail that decides the diagnosis. Working with an interpreter, including the rules of seating, first person speech and what must never be translated by a child. Cultural difference in the expression of pain, in the meaning of illness, and in who is permitted to speak. Sensitive topics approached without euphemism and without force: sexual history, violence at home, suicidal thought, substance use. Collateral history from a relative, and what to do when the account conflicts with the patient's. The same conversation conducted with an hour available and with five minutes.

---

## Chapter 9 — Documentation

The written record as the memory of the reasoning: Soap notes described as a structure for the subjective account, the objective findings, the assessment and the plan, and why the assessment is the part that must carry the differential rather than a single label. Problem lists that stay current, and the difference between a problem and a diagnosis. Handover that transfers the reasoning, the uncertainty and the safety net, not only the tasks. What may never be omitted: the drug, the allergy, the result not yet back, the patient who is not to be discharged. The same record kept on a full electronic system and on a single sheet of paper in a clinic with no electricity.

---

## Chapter 10 — General Inspection

The diagnosis that is available before the patient is touched: appearance and distress, nutrition and wasting, hydration, pallor, jaundice, cyanosis, clubbing, edema, the lymph nodes, and the skin as a whole. What each sign means physiologically, so that the listener knows what the finding implies rather than only what it is called. End-of-bed observation of the work of breathing, the posture, the smell, the sweat, and the patient who is sitting forward. The reasoning first, then the sequence performed in a well-lit hospital bay and by torchlight in a room where the patient is wrapped in blankets. Note that a pale, clammy patient may be bleeding internally and that this inspection takes seconds.

---

## Chapter 11 — Vital Signs as Diagnostic Tools

Pulse, respiratory rate, temperature, blood pressure and oxygen saturation read as trends rather than as single numbers, because the direction of travel carries more information than the value. The shock index and the early warning scores as formalizations of the observation that several mildly abnormal signs together are more alarming than one very abnormal sign. The unwell-looking patient whose numbers are still within range. What can be relied on without a monitor: the respiratory rate, the capillary refill, the mental state, the urine output. The same assessment performed with a full monitor and with a watch and a trained hand, and the reminder that a normal reading does not make a patient well.

---

## Chapter 12 — Cardiovascular Examination

Pulses in rate, rhythm, volume and character, the jugular venous pressure and what a raised waveform means, the heart sounds and what each extra sound implies, murmurs described by timing, site, radiation and what they do with posture and respiration, and the peripheral signs of heart failure. The reasoning behind each maneuvere before the maneuvere itself. The patient who is dangerously unwell: the murmur of a valve destroyed by infection, the quiet chest of acute pulmonary edema, the hypotension of tamponade, the absent pulse of an aortic emergency. Performed in a quiet cardiology room and again in a noisy ward with no echocardiograph within reach.

---

## Chapter 13 — Respiratory Examination

The work of breathing observed before auscultation, then percussion, tactile fremitus and breath sounds, with crackles, wheeze, bronchial breathing and the pleural rub described by mechanism rather than by name. The signs that identify consolidation, pleural effusion and pneumothorax, and the reasoning that links each physical finding to the underlying process. The dangerous presentations are named as they arise: tension pneumothorax, which must be recognized without a radiograph, the large effusion compressing the lung, and the patient with a silent chest and an exhausted respiratory effort. The examination is then compared with what it becomes when imaging is immediate and when none will come.

---

## Chapter 14 — Abdominal Examination

Inspection, then auscultation for bowel sounds, then percussion, then palpation in the order that avoids distressing the patient, with the reasoning for each step and the meaning of guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness and peritonism. Organomegaly assessed by its characteristics rather than its size alone; hernias looked for at every orifice; the rectal and pelvic examinations described frankly, including what they reveal and when they must not be omitted. The dangerous causes are named: a ruptured aortic aneurysm, a perforated viscus, a strangulated hernia, an ectopic pregnancy, and the abdomen that is tender but not yet peritonitic. The same examination with imaging at hand and without it.

---

## Chapter 15 — Neurological Examination

A screening examination that can be completed at any bedside and a fuller one that answers a specific question, beginning with mental status and the Glasgow coma scale as a formalization of the level of consciousness. The cranial nerves, motor power and tone, the sensory examination, the reflexes and their localizing value, cerebellar signs and the several ways of testing gait. What each finding says about where in the nervous system the lesion lies, and which findings demand immediate action. The chapter should show the screening version given five minutes and no equipment, then the focused version in a hospital where imaging and specialist help are available, and name the presentations that must never wait, among them the stroke that still has time and the cord compression that does not.

---

## Chapter 16 — Musculoskeletal Examination

The joint examined by inspection, palpation, and active then passive movement, with the normal range and the meaning of a restriction, an effusion, a crepitus and a joint that is hot. The spine, the sacroiliac joints and the difference between mechanical pain and pain that suggests infection or malignancy. The special tests described by what each is meant to exclude and how each is performed, without pretending they are decisive. Gout, septic arthritis, inflammatory arthritis and fracture named among the possibilities, with the signs that separate them. The reasoning is the same in a rheumatology clinic and in a district hospital, though the chapter should say what can be done when the radiograph and the uric acid result are days away.

---

## Chapter 17 — Head, Eyes, Ears, Nose, Throat and Mouth

The examination of a region that can be inspected directly, and the diagnostic value of doing so. The eye for redness, discharge, pupil, acuity and field; the ear for discharge, drum, mastoid tenderness and hearing tested by voice and tuning fork; the nose and sinuses; the throat and the mouth for ulcers, thrush, dentition, the tongue and the mucosa. The dangerous diagnoses are named where they appear: acute glaucoma, a foreign body, a corneal ulcer, epiglottitis, a retropharyngeal abscess, a dental infection tracking into the neck, and the mouth that suggests a systemic disease. Performed with an ophthalmoscope and an otoscope, and again with a torch and a spoon.

---

## Chapter 18 — Skin, Hair and Nails

Describing a lesion so that another clinician could picture it: site, distribution, symmetry, color, border, surface, size, and whether it is flat or raised, blanching or not, scaly, crusted, vesicular, ulcerated or scarred. The vocabulary as a form of precision rather than pedantry, and the pattern recognition that follows description. Hair and nails as windows on systemic disease. The dangerous eruptions are named: the rash that does not blanch in a febrile patient, the blistering drug reaction, the spreading soft tissue infection, the pigmented lesion that has changed, and the rash with mucosal involvement. The same description performed in a dermatology clinic and through a poor telephone image from a remote post.

---

## Chapter 19 — Genitourinary and Breast Examination

The examination of regions that carry shame, and the manner that makes it possible: consent explained, a chaperone offered, privacy protected, and the examination limited to what the question requires. The genitalia, the pelvic examination and what it reveals, the prostate, the breast examined by inspection and palpation with the axilla included, and the instruction in breast self-awareness. Discharge, ulceration, masses and pain described along with the reasoning that links them to infection, malignancy and structural disease. The dangerous possibilities are named, among them ectopic pregnancy, testicular torsion, a breast mass fixed to the chest wall, and pelvic inflammatory disease. The same examination with full facilities and where privacy itself is scarce.

---

## Chapter 20 — Mental State Examination

The structured observation of a mind: appearance and behavior, speech, mood and affect, thought form and content, perception, cognition, insight and judgment, each described by what it observes and what it may indicate. The difference between the mental state examination and a psychiatric diagnosis, and the many physical illnesses that present through it. The dangerous findings are named as they arise: suicidal intent, psychotic command hallucinations, agitation with an unmet physical need, and the delirium that is mistaken for a psychiatric disorder. The chapter should show the examination performed in a quiet interview room and at the bedside of a patient who will not stay still, where it is compressed to what can be observed in two minutes.

---

## Chapter 21 — Fever

Acute, prolonged and recurrent fever as three different problems, each with its own reasoning. The mechanisms of fever and the meaning of its pattern, the value of repeated measurement, and the source hunt through history and examination. Malaria, typhoid, dengue and other tropical causes in one setting and bacterial sepsis, endocarditis, tuberculosis and occult abscess in another. The dangerous diagnoses are named early: the febrile patient with meningism, the immunocompromised patient with almost no signs, the returning traveller with a hemorrhagic fever, and the fever with a new murmur or a stiff neck. The same hunt conducted with cultures and imaging and again with a thermometer, a torch and clinical judgment.

---

## Chapter 22 — Chest Pain

The reasoning that must run in parallel with the history, because the most lethal causes announce themselves first and the diagnosis cannot wait for the electrocardiogram. Cardiac, pulmonary, gastrointestinal and musculoskeletal causes organized by mechanism, with the quality, site, radiation, duration, triggers and relief of the pain as the discriminating features. Name the causes that must not be missed: acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, esophageal rupture and pericarditis with tamponade. The chapter should then show the same assessment in a hospital where troponin and imaging are minutes away and in a clinic where the decision is whether the patient can travel and by what means.

---

## Chapter 23 — Dyspnea and Cough

Breathlessness and cough taken together, since they usually arrive as one story. The origin of the sensation of dyspnea, the value of orthopnea and paroxysmal nocturnal breathlessness, and the meaning of a cough by its duration and character. Asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pneumonia, pleural disease, pulmonary embolism, heart failure and tuberculosis handled as competing explanations, with the findings that separate them at the bedside. The dangerous presentations are named: the patient who cannot finish a sentence, the one with a silent chest, the one with a new unilateral leg swelling, and the one coughing blood. The reasonings then performed with a radiograph available and with none, noting what an examination can exclude.

---

## Chapter 24 — Headache and Dizziness

Headache by pattern: the sudden, the progressive, the postural, the recurrent and the chronic, with the features that change triage rather than the labels that decorate the list. Dizziness divided into vertigo, presyncope, disequilibrium and the vague light-headedness that often has a systemic cause, and the bedside maneuveres that separate them. Name the lethal causes at the point where the symptom is described: subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningitis, a raised intracranial pressure, a cerebral venous thrombosis, a stroke in the posterior circulation, and temporal arteritis in the older patient. Then show the assessment in a hospital with immediate imaging and in a clinic where the decision rests on the history and the fundoscopy.

---

## Chapter 25 — Abdominal Pain

Pain read by its location, its migration, its character and what provokes it, then mapped onto the structures beneath. The acute abdomen as a syndrome that demands recognition rather than a specific diagnosis, with peritonism, rigidity and shock as the findings that force action. Name the emergencies as the regions are discussed: appendicitis, cholecystitis, pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, perforation, mesenteric ischemia, ruptured aneurysm, ectopic pregnancy and testicular torsion referred to the abdomen. The chapter should show the same reasoning in a hospital where imaging and surgery are available at once and in a district clinic where the task is to recognize the abdomen that must travel.

---

## Chapter 26 — Nausea, Vomiting, Diarrhea and Constipation

The four symptoms as a single subject, because they usually describe one disturbance of the gut and often of the whole patient. The mechanisms of vomiting, the meaning of what is vomited and when, the patterns of diarrhea by duration, character and blood, and constipation as a symptom that may signal obstruction rather than habit. The dangerous causes are named where the symptom is presented: bowel obstruction, a perforated viscus, mesenteric ischemia, severe dehydration, invasive bacterial infection, diabetic ketoacidosis and raised intracranial pressure. Rehydration and its assessment belong to the reasoning even though doses do not. The same assessment with a laboratory and with only a scale, a watch and clinical judgment.

---

## Chapter 27 — Gastrointestinal Bleeding and Jaundice

Bleeding from the gut by presentation and by likely origin, with the reasoning that separates an upper from a lower source at the bedside, and the assessment of how much has been lost before the blood count is believed. Jaundice divided into prehepatic, hepatic and posthepatic, with the history, the examination of the liver and spleen, the color of the urine and stool, and the signs of chronic liver disease. Name the causes that must not be missed: a variceal bleed, a perforated ulcer, a ruptured aortic graft, cholangitis, and the obstructive jaundice that is a malignancy until proven otherwise. The same assessment in a hospital with endoscopy and in a clinic where the decision is how fast the patient must travel.

---

## Chapter 28 — Palpitations, Syncope and Collapse

Palpitations described by the patient as rate, regularity and duration, and the reasoning that matches description to mechanism. Syncope divided into cardiac, reflex and orthostatic causes, with the history of the event, the witnesses' account and the examination of the heart and the blood pressure on standing. Name the dangerous causes as the symptom is presented: a tachyarrhythmia with hemodynamic compromise, complete heart block, aortic stenosis, a pulmonary embolism, a ruptured aortic aneurysm, an ectopic pregnancy, a subarachnoid hemorrhage and a gastrointestinal bleed. Then the chapter should show the assessment where an electrocardiogram and monitoring are immediate, and where a single tracing and a careful witness remain the whole investigation.

---

## Chapter 29 — Back and Joint Pain

Back pain sorted by mechanism rather than by severity of complaint, with the features that distinguish mechanical pain from the pain of infection, malignancy, fracture and inflammatory disease. The joint similarly examined for the single hot joint, the polyarticular pattern and the pain referred from elsewhere. The dangerous causes are named where the pain is described: cord compression, the cauda equina syndrome with its saddle anesthesia and its bladder disturbance, vertebral osteomyelitis, a spinal epidural abscess, and a leaking aortic aneurysm presenting as back pain. The same reasoning in a hospital with urgent imaging and in a clinic where the decision to refer rests on the history and a careful neurological examination.

---

## Chapter 30 — Weight Loss, Fatigue and Anorexia

Three symptoms that overlap and that often signify disease the patient has not yet named, so the chapter treats them as a symptom complex requiring a systematic hunt. Involuntary weight loss measured rather than reported, the meaning of appetite lost against appetite preserved, and fatigue distinguished from weakness and from breathlessness. Malignancy, tuberculosis, human immunodeficiency virus, diabetes, thyroid disease, depression, chronic infection, malabsorption and social causes considered in the same frame. The dangerous diagnosis is named early: the weight loss that is a cancer until the assessment says otherwise, and the fatigue that is heart failure or anemia rather than a mood. The same hunt with a full laboratory and with none.

---

## Chapter 31 — Edema, Swelling and Lumps

Swelling classified by whether it is generalized or local, pitting or not, tender or not, and by how it behaves with posture and time. The mechanisms of edema explained through the Starling forces in words, and the value of examining the heart, the liver, the kidney and the veins in the same patient. Lumps examined by site, size, consistency, mobility, attachment and the state of the surrounding skin, with the lymphatic drainage in mind. The dangerous swellings are named: the lymph node that is hard and fixed, the lump that is pulsatile, the swelling of deep vein thrombosis with a pulmonary embolism behind it, and the unilateral leg swelling after immobilization. The same assessment with imaging and without it.

---

## Chapter 32 — Urinary Symptoms and Hematuria

The urinary tract approached through the symptoms that describe it: frequency, urgency, dysuria, hesitancy, retention, incontinence, nocturia, loin pain and the color of the urine. Hematuria divided into visible and non-visible, and the reasoning that separates glomerular from urological bleeding. Infection, stones, obstruction, prostatic disease and malignancy considered together, with the features that move each up the list. Name the dangerous causes as the symptoms are described: an obstructed infected system, acute retention with renal impairment, and hematuria that is a malignancy until it has been investigated. The same assessment with a laboratory, ultrasound and urology on call, and in a clinic where the dipstick is the only test.

---

## Chapter 33 — Altered Consciousness, Confusion and Seizures

A disturbance of consciousness treated as an emergency with a differential that must be run in minutes, and a seizure examined for what caused it rather than only for what it looked like. The level of consciousness assessed and recorded, the difference between delirium, dementia and psychosis, and the reversible causes sought systematically. Name the lethal possibilities at once: hypoglycemia, hypoxia, stroke, meningitis, encephalitis, status epilepticus, raised intracranial pressure, a head injury, poisoning, sepsis and a metabolic derangement. The chapter should show the reasoning in a hospital with imaging, glucose and an intensive care unit behind the door, and in a clinic where the first question is whether the patient can be woken and whether the airway is safe, and where the answer decides the journey.

---

## Chapter 34 — Rash and Skin Complaints

The common eruptions by pattern and distribution, with eczema, psoriasis, fungal infection, scabies, urticaria, viral exanthema and drug eruption described by what distinguishes them at the bedside. The reasoning that links a rash to a systemic illness rather than treating it as a local problem, including the fever with a rash and the rash with mucosal involvement. Name the dangerous eruptions where the rash appears: the purpuric rash that does not blanch, toxic epidermal necrolysis, necrotizing fasciitis, meningococcal disease, and the drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms. The same description performed in a dermatology clinic with good light and in a field clinic where the light is poor and the patient must be asked to return.

---

## Chapter 35 — Bleeding, Bruising and Anemia

Abnormal bleeding and bruising assessed by pattern and by the questions that separate a platelet problem from a coagulation problem from a vascular one, with the family history and the medicines taken as part of the examination. Anemia approached by mechanism rather than by a single number, with the history, the examination of pallor, the tongue, the nails and the spleen as the first instruments. Name the dangerous causes: the anemia of a slow gastrointestinal bleed, the marrow failure that presents as bruising, a hemolytic crisis, the bleeding of advanced liver disease, and the anemia of pregnancy and of childhood. The same assessment with a full blood count and a film, and with nothing but the conjunctivae and the history.

## Chapter 36 — Pain Assessment Across Ages and Cultures

Pain as a subjective experience that the clinician must still measure as carefully as a pulse, through the patient's account, the observed behavior, the functional effect and the response to what has already been tried. The tools that exist for those who cannot speak: the faces scales for children, the behavioral scales for the confused and the demented, and the vital signs that are useful only as supporting evidence. Cultural difference in how pain is expressed, endured and reported, the language of pain in translation, and the clinician's own assumptions about who suffers more. The dangerous diagnosis is named: pain that is being treated rather than explained, and the analgesia that conceals a surgical abdomen. The same assessment with a full range of options and where none exist.

## Chapter 37 — The First Minute

The airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure sequence as the reasoning that identifies immediate threats to life before any diagnosis is attempted, and the simultaneous recognition that the patient is critically ill at all. The findings that demand action in the first minute: an obstructed airway, absent or agonal breathing, a pulse that cannot be felt, a seizure that is continuing, a blood glucose that is low. The sequence performed with a full team, a monitor and a resuscitation trolley, and performed alone in a clinic with a bag and a mask and a hand on the chest. The chapter must also name the common lethal causes, among them hemorrhage, hypoxia, sepsis, cardiac arrest and stroke, and the rule that treatment precedes diagnosis in this minute.

---

## Chapter 38 — Shock

Shock as a failure of tissue perfusion with many causes and one urgency: hypovolemic, septic, cardiogenic and obstructive, each with the findings that point to it and the bedside maneuveres that separate them. The value and the limits of the blood pressure, the meaning of the pulse pressure and the capillary refill, the cold clammy patient against the warm vasodilated one, and the patient who is compensating well and about to stop. Name the lethal causes as they are described, among them hemorrhage, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary embolism and anaphylaxis. The same differentiation with a catheter in the artery and an echocardiograph at the door, and with a hand on the pulse and a watch.

---

## Chapter 39 — Sepsis Recognition

Sepsis as a dysregulated response to infection that is recognized by its trajectory rather than by any single value, with the systemic inflammatory response syndrome described as what it formalizes and the quick sequential organ failure assessment described the same way, along with their limitations and their local adaptations. The signs that precede the obvious fever: a new confusion, a raised respiratory rate, a low pressure, a poor urine output. Name the source hunt and the dangerous presentations, among them the neutropenic patient, the one with a new murmur, the one with a rigid abdomen, and the one whose skin is already mottled. The same recognition with a full laboratory and with a thermometer and a clinical eye.

---

## Chapter 40 — Respiratory Failure and Airway Compromise

The failure of oxygenation against the failure of ventilation, distinguished at the bedside by the work of breathing, the mental state, the chest wall movement and where the problem lies. The signs of an airway that is narrowing rather than of one already closed: stridor, a changed voice, drooling, a muffled cough, inability to lie flat. The dangerous causes are named as the subject is described: epiglottitis, a foreign body, anaphylaxis, a neck hematoma, an expanding tonsillar abscess, and the exhausted patient who is about to stop breathing. The same assessment with an oximeter, a blood gas and a ventilator available, and with nothing but the observation of the patient and the question of whether help must be called now.

---

## Chapter 41 — Acute Neurological Emergencies

The neurological emergencies grouped by the decision each one forces: stroke, which is time-dependent; meningitis, which is treated before it is confirmed; status epilepticus, which is a failure of the usual termination; and raised intracranial pressure, which threatens herniation. The assessment that establishes the level of consciousness, the lateralizing signs, the neck, the fundi and the pattern of the seizure, and the findings that make the diagnosis without imaging. The reasoning then performed in a hospital where a scan and a thrombolysis decision are minutes away, and in a clinic where the decision is whether the patient can be moved at all, since the same disease demands different actions according to the road.

---

## Chapter 42 — Acute Abdomen, Trauma and Hemorrhage

The abdomen after injury and the abdomen without injury, since the two share the same emergency and the same first question, which is whether the patient has internal bleeding and where it is. The primary survey of the trauma patient, the value of mechanism of injury, and the findings that are reliable when consciousness is impaired. The dangerous causes are named as they appear: a ruptured spleen, a liver laceration, a retroperitoneal bleed, a bowel perforation, a pelvic fracture, a ruptured ectopic pregnancy and a leaking abdominal aneurysm. The same assessment with imaging and an operating theatre, and at a roadside or in a clinic where the decision is transport, positioning and the control of external bleeding.

---

## Chapter 43 — Metabolic Emergencies

The metabolic disturbance recognized from the history and the bedside examination, since it is often reversible and often missed. Diabetic ketoacidosis and its mimics, hypoglycemia as the diagnosis that must be considered in every patient with altered consciousness, and the electrolyte disturbances that produce weakness, arrhythmia or seizures. Poisoning as a diagnostic subject in its own right, with the questions about what was taken, when, how much and with what, and the toxidromes described by their observable pattern rather than by a list of antidotes. The same reasoning with an immediate blood glucose and a laboratory, and in a clinic where a glucose meter and the history carry the whole assessment and where hypoglycemia must be excluded before anything else is accepted.

---

## Chapter 44 — Deterioration and Early Warning

The deterioration that announces itself long before it becomes irreversible, and the subtle findings that most often precede it: a rising respiratory rate, a new confusion, a fall in urine output, a change in the patient's color, the relative who says something is wrong. The reasoning behind early warning scores as a formalization of several mildly abnormal signs, and why a single normal value reassures too easily. The patient who is compensating and looks acceptable, and the difference between improvement and exhaustion. Name the situations in which deterioration is expected, among them sepsis, hemorrhage, head injury, pancreatitis and the postoperative patient. The same vigilance on a monitored ward and where the observations are written by hand every four hours.

---

## Chapter 45 — Cardiovascular Disease

The common cardiovascular diseases approached through the way each one presents rather than through a list: hypertension, which is usually silent until it has damaged something; ischemic heart disease, stable and acute; heart failure, with the findings that separate the failing pump from the stiff one; the arrhythmias, with the rate and rhythm that make a patient unstable; valvular disease, with the murmur and the consequences it produces; and venous thromboembolism, which must be considered in any unexplained breathlessness or leg swelling. The dangerous presentations are named at each. The reasoning is then followed through a hospital with angiography and echocardiography and a clinic with a stethoscope.

---

## Chapter 46 — Respiratory Disease

The common respiratory diseases by the pattern each produces: pneumonia, with the findings that separate it from other causes of a febrile breathlessness; asthma, with the severity assessed by the ability to speak and the work of breathing rather than by the peak flow alone; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, with the exacerbation recognized and the patient who is tiring; tuberculosis, with the diagnostic weight of a prolonged cough and the limits of the examination; pleural disease; and pulmonary embolism. The dangerous presentations are named as each disease is described, including the silent chest, the large effusion and the patient with a normal examination and a clot in the lung. The same reasoning with a radiograph and a laboratory, and without them.

## Chapter 47 — Gastrointestinal and Hepatic Disease

The gastrointestinal and liver diseases by their clinical pattern: peptic disease and its complication, hepatitis in its acute and chronic forms, cirrhosis with the examination of the stigmata and the reasoning about decompensation, pancreatitis with its severity assessed at the bedside, appendicitis with its migration of pain, and inflammatory bowel disease with its extraintestinal manifestations. The dangerous presentations are named where each disease appears, among them a perforated ulcer, a variceal hemorrhage, ascending cholangitis, a pancreatic necrosis and a fulminant hepatic failure. The same assessment in a hospital where endoscopy, imaging and surgery are available, and in a clinic where the recognition of the abdomen that must travel is the whole diagnostic task.

## Chapter 48 — Endocrine and Metabolic Disease

The endocrine diseases that announce themselves through ordinary complaints: diabetes and its acute complications, thyroid disease at both extremes, adrenal disorders including the insufficiency that presents as shock, and obesity assessed as a diagnostic subject rather than a moral one. The reasoning that explains how a hormonal disorder produces the signs it produces, so that the listener can predict the findings rather than memorize them. The dangerous presentations are named as they arise, among them diabetic ketoacidosis, hypoglycemia, thyroid storm, myxedema coma, adrenal crisis and hypercalcemia. The same assessment where a laboratory measures hormones within hours and where the diagnosis rests on the appearance, the history and the response to a single correction.

## Chapter 49 — Renal and Urological Disease

Kidney injury divided into the prerenal, the renal and the postrenal, since the division decides the action, and chronic kidney disease recognized from its consequences rather than from a single value. Urinary tract infection from the uncomplicated to the septic, and stones by the pain they cause and the obstruction they may create. The examination of the patient for volume status, a palpable bladder, the flank and the skin, and the reasoning that links the urine output to the rest of the assessment. The dangerous presentations are named: an obstructed infected kidney, advanced kidney failure with its metabolic consequences, and the patient whose urine output has quietly stopped. The same reasoning with a laboratory and a urology service and without them.

---

## Chapter 50 — Hematology and Oncology

The blood diseases and the cancers approached through the reasons a patient presents: anemia, infection and bleeding from marrow failure, the lymphadenopathy and the masses that must be explained, and the general symptoms of malignancy that have no localizing sign. Leukemia and lymphoma described by their presentation rather than their classification. The warning signs of cancer named where each system is mentioned, since a missed malignancy is the commonest serious diagnostic failure in ordinary practice. The chapter should also address the patient who fears cancer and the finding that reassures, and show the reasoning with a full laboratory service and where a blood film and a referral are all that exist.

---

## Chapter 51 — Neurology

The neurological diseases by their clinical course: stroke and its differentiation from a seizure, a migraine and a hypoglycemia; epilepsy by the seizure type and the cause; the neuropathies by their distribution and the conditions behind them; dementia by the pattern of loss and its contrast with delirium; and Parkinson disease by its signs. The dangerous presentations are named as they appear, among them a stroke that is still within time, a subarachnoid hemorrhage misread as a headache, a cord compression and a rapidly progressive weakness. The same reasoning where imaging is immediate and where the whole diagnosis rests on the history, the examination and the recognition that this patient must be referred now.

---

## Chapter 52 — Musculoskeletal and Rheumatology

The joint diseases by the pattern of involvement: arthritis in one joint against many, the acute against the chronic, and the inflammatory against the degenerative. Gout and septic arthritis separated at the bedside by the history and the signs even though the definitive answer needs fluid. Lupus recognized through its multisystem manifestations rather than a single criterion, and osteoporosis as a disease that presents with its consequence rather than before it. The dangerous possibilities are named, among them septic arthritis, a spinal infection, a vasculitic emergency and a fracture that was dismissed as a strain. The reasoning is then carried into a hospital with immunology and imaging and into a clinic with a hand on the joint.

---

## Chapter 53 — Infectious Diseases

The infections grouped by the reasoning each demands: the human immunodeficiency virus and the conditions that signal it, sexually transmitted infections taken without judgment, malaria and the other tropical fevers by their exposure history and their tempo, and the common viral illnesses that must be distinguished from the bacterial ones that imitate them. The dangerous presentations are named where each infection appears, among them severe malaria, a hemorrhagic fever, meningococcal disease, tetanus and the viral illness that is actually an early bacterial sepsis. The chapter should then show the same reasoning where a laboratory identifies organisms within hours and where a syndromic decision must be made on the spot and reviewed as the patient declares himself.

---

## Chapter 54 — Dermatology

The common skin diseases approached through the pattern recognition that dermatology rewards, with eczema, psoriasis, the fungal and the bacterial infections, infestations and the drug eruptions each described by their distribution and their evolution. A working method for the rash that is new to the clinician: describe, compare, consider the whole patient and ask what has been applied. The dangerous eruptions are named and revisited: the one that does not blanch, the one with mucosal involvement, the blistering drug reaction, the necrotizing infection and the lesion that is a skin cancer. The same method with a dermatoscope and a colleague to consult and where the only record is a careful description in the notes.

---

## Chapter 55 — ENT and Ophthalmology

The two sensory systems and the structures around them, approached through the symptoms that bring patients: the red eye, the loss of vision, the loss of hearing, the discharging ear, the blocked nose and the sore throat. The reasoning that separates the trivial from the sight-threatening and the hearing-threatening. The dangerous diagnoses are named where each symptom is described: acute glaucoma, a detached retina, a corneal ulcer, a giant cell arteritis with visual loss, a sudden sensorineural hearing loss, a peritonsillar and a retropharyngeal abscess and a tumor of the head and neck. The same assessment with a slit lamp and an audiometer and with a torch, a spoon and a whispered voice.

---

## Chapter 56 — Psychiatry

The psychiatric presentations as they appear in any setting: depression, anxiety, psychosis, the delirium that is not a psychiatric disorder at all, and substance use with its withdrawal states. The suicide risk assessed directly, without euphemism and without a checklist that replaces the conversation. The physical illnesses that imitate psychiatric disease, since this is where the dangerous diagnosis hides. The reasoning behind the mental state examination is carried into practice in a clinic with time and privacy, and again in a general hospital where the consultation happens at the bedside of a patient who does not want to be seen. The chapter should be explicit that assessment never becomes grounds for dismissing a physical complaint.

---

## Chapter 57 — Pregnancy and Obstetrics

Pregnancy as a state that changes the normal examination and creates its own diagnoses: the normal findings that mimic disease, the danger signs that must be sought at every visit, pre-eclampsia assessed by the pressure and the protein, and bleeding in early and late pregnancy distinguished by its cause and its urgency. The dangerous conditions are named as the subject is described, among them an ectopic pregnancy, a placental abruption, a placenta previa, a uterine rupture, puerperal sepsis and thromboembolism. The same assessment in a hospital with an ultrasound and a theatre, and in a clinic where the questions, the blood pressure, the urine and the recognition of the woman who must leave immediately are the whole of the diagnosis.

---

## Chapter 58 — Newborns and Infants

The newborn examined as a patient with a language of his own: the danger signs that must be recognized without a history, the Apgar score described as a formalization of the immediate condition of the baby at birth rather than a diagnosis, jaundice assessed by its timing and its extent, feeding difficulty, the cry, the tone, the temperature and the weight. The dangerous conditions are named as they arise, among them sepsis, a congenital heart lesion presenting as a collapse, a bowel obstruction, a metabolic disease and the jaundice that is hemolytic or obstructive. The same examination in a neonatal unit and in a clinic where the baby is weighed in a cloth sling and the decision is whether the journey can wait until morning.

---

## Chapter 59 — Children

The child assessed through the developmental expectations for each age, since the normal is what makes the abnormal visible, and through the integrated management of childhood illness as a systematic approach to the common complaints. Fever in the child, respiratory illness with the counting of the breaths and the drawing in of the ribs, and dehydration graded by the signs that can be observed without a laboratory. The dangerous presentations are named as the chapter proceeds, among them a severe pneumonia, a meningitis, a malnutrition with its complications, a severe dehydration and the child who is quiet when he should be crying. The same assessment in a pediatric ward and in a village with a mid-level provider and a single register.

---

## Chapter 60 — Adolescents

The adolescent as a patient who is neither a large child nor a small adult, and whose diagnosis is shaped by development, by confidentiality and by the things he will not say in front of a parent. Growth and puberty assessed against their normal range, the mental health presentations that begin at this age, the risk-taking behavior asked about directly, and the chronic illness that transfers into adult care. The dangerous possibilities are named, among them self-harm and suicide, an eating disorder, a substance intoxication, a pelvic infection and a malignancy of the young. The same consultation with the parent in the room and with the adolescent alone, and the explanation to both of what will and will not be shared.

---

## Chapter 61 — Older Adults

The older patient whose presentation is atypical by default, so that the chapter teaches the search for the diagnosis behind a fall, a confusion, a loss of appetite or a decline in function. Frailty as a clinical state that changes both the diagnosis and the decision, the multiple medicines that produce symptoms of their own, and the delirium distinguished from the dementia it so often accompanies. The dangerous causes are named as they arise, among them sepsis without fever, a hip fracture, a subdural hematoma, a myocardial infarction without pain, a drug interaction and dehydration. The same assessment in a hospital with imaging and a geriatric team, and at home where the carpet, the stairs and the pill organizer are part of the examination.

---

## Chapter 62 — Immunocompromised Patients

The patient whose immune system cannot produce the signs that diagnosis relies upon, so that fever alone may be the only finding and the ordinary examination may be falsely reassuring. The reasons for the state, from the human immunodeficiency virus to chemotherapy, transplantation and the biologic medicines, and the infections that each pattern of immune failure permits. The dangerous presentations are named as they arise, among them a neutropenic sepsis, a pneumocystis pneumonia, a disseminated fungal infection, a tuberculosis reactivation and a central nervous system infection. The same assessment where a laboratory provides a cell count within the hour and where the clinician must treat on suspicion because the result will come after the decision.

---

## Chapter 63 — Palliative and End-of-Life Assessment

The assessment of a patient for whom the diagnostic question has changed from what can be cured to what is happening now and what will happen next. The symptoms that dominate this stage, among them pain, breathlessness, nausea, constipation, delirium and the distress of the family, each assessed by its cause because many of them are reversible. The recognition of the dying patient and the findings that suggest the last days, described honestly and without euphemism. The chapter must also address how the reasoning differs in a hospital with a palliative team and in a home where the family is the nursing staff, and it must be explicit that comfort and diagnosis are not opposing duties.

---

## Chapter 64 — Laboratory Tests

The laboratory used as an extension of the reasoning rather than a substitute for it: the blood count and what each of its parts means, the chemistry panel, the inflammatory markers and their limits, the liver and kidney function tests read against the patient rather than against the range, the urinalysis as the cheapest window on the body, and the cultures with their timing and their contamination. The reasoning that explains how a test can be abnormal without disease and normal with it. The dangerous results are named as they appear, among them a low glucose, a rising creatinine, a critical potassium and a positive culture in a patient who looks well. The same results in a hospital and where the one available test must be chosen well.

---

## Chapter 65 — ECG Interpretation

The electrocardiogram read by a fixed sequence so that nothing is skipped: rate, rhythm, axis, intervals, the chambers, the segments and the comparison with any previous tracing. The reasoning behind each step, and the patterns that must be recognized immediately because they change what is done within minutes, among them the acute coronary occlusion, the wide complex tachycardia, the complete heart block, the hyperkalemia, the pulmonary embolism pattern and the long QT interval. The chapter should show the systematic reading performed with a previous tracing and a senior colleague available, and again with one tracing, no comparison and no one to ask, where the safe default is to treat the patient rather than the paper.

---

## Chapter 66 — Chest and Abdominal X-Ray

The radiograph read by a sequence that does not depend on the finding being obvious: the film checked for its patient and its projection, then the chest examined for the airway, the breathing, the circulation, the diaphragm, the edges and the bones, and the abdomen for the gas pattern, the organ outlines, the free air and the calcifications. The reasoning that explains how each finding is produced so that the listener can predict what a disease will look like. The dangerous findings are named as the sequence passes them, among them free air under the diaphragm, a pneumothorax, a widened mediastinum and a bowel obstruction. The same reading with a radiologist and with a clinician alone in a clinic with a light box.

---

## Chapter 67 — Ultrasound at the Bedside

The focused ultrasound examination as a physical sign rather than an imaging study, performed to answer one question and repeated as the clinical picture changes. The views that a clinician can learn and use: the heart for the pericardial fluid and the failing ventricle, the chest for the effusion and the pneumothorax, the abdomen for the free fluid, the aorta and the gallbladder, the kidneys for the obstruction, and the pregnant uterus for the position and the heartbeat. The reasoning that says what each view can and cannot exclude. The dangerous findings are named where each view is described, among them a cardiac tamponade, a ruptured ectopic pregnancy and a leaking aneurysm. The same probe used in a resuscitation room and in a clinic where it is the only imaging.

---

## Chapter 68 — Point-of-Care and Rapid Tests

The tests that fit in a hand and change a decision within minutes: the glucose meter, the malaria rapid test, the human immunodeficiency virus test, the pregnancy test, the troponin, the urine dipstick, the blood gas and the several tests for infection and coagulation that now exist in that format. What each one actually measures, its false positives and its negatives, and the clinical reasoning that must accompany the strip. The dangerous mistakes are named: a normal glucose meter reading in a patient who is hypoglycemic, a negative malaria test taken as exclusion when the first test is often negative, and the urine dipstick that produces more tests than answers. The same tests where a laboratory exists and where they are the laboratory.

---

## Chapter 69 — Choosing and Interpreting Tests in Context

The discipline that governs all the others: the question asked before the test is ordered, which is what finding would change what I do, and the recognition that a test which cannot alter the decision is a harm. Over-testing as a clinical error with its own pathology, the cascade that follows an unexpected result, the false positive that becomes a diagnosis, and the test ordered to satisfy someone other than the patient. The reasoning then applied in a hospital where tests are unlimited and pressure to order is high, and in a clinic where the test must be justified by its scarcity, with the reminder that the two settings share the same question and the same duty to the result.

---

## Chapter 70 — Scoring Systems and Rules

The scores and rules as formalizations of clinical reasoning rather than as answers: Wells for the probability of a clot, the Curb sixty-five score for the severity of pneumonia, the Cha two D S two Vasc score for the risk of stroke in atrial fibrillation, the Ottawa rules for the decision about a radiograph after injury, and the Centor criteria for the probability of streptococcal pharyngitis. Each is described by the reasoning it organizes, the population it was derived in, the population in which it underperforms, and its danger of replacing judgment. The chapter must also state that the arithmetic is never spoken here and that the current source and the local protocol decide the numbers.

---

## Chapter 71 — Flowcharts and Algorithms

The flowchart as a spoken sequence, since a diagram cannot be shown and the listener must be able to follow the path by ear. The algorithms that exist for common presentations, among them the febrile child, the breathless adult, the chest pain, the shock and the altered consciousness, described by the decisions at each point rather than by the boxes on a page. Why an algorithm clarifies the common path and misleads at the edges, and the discipline of asking what the algorithm assumes about the patient in front of you. The same algorithm followed in a hospital with every option available and in a clinic where the branches that require unavailable tests must be replaced by a referral decision.

---

## Chapter 72 — Red Flags and Referral Criteria

The findings that change the plan from watching to acting, gathered as a discipline rather than a list: the red flags of the history, the examination and the vital signs, and the reason each one is a red flag. The criteria that decide referral, and the difference between a patient who must go now, one who must be referred and one who may be watched with a safety net. The dangerous situations are named where the flags belong to them, among them a fever with a stiff neck, a headache of sudden onset, a testicular pain of sudden onset, a painless hematuria, a weight loss, a new confusion in an older patient. The same criteria applied where referral is a telephone call and where it is a journey of some hours.

---

## Chapter 73 — Follow-Up and Safety-Netting

The part of the consultation that begins when the diagnosis is uncertain: what the patient is told, in words that can be repeated at home, and the specific findings that should bring him back. The reasoning that a working diagnosis is a commitment to review rather than a conclusion, and the difference between a follow-up appointment and a safety net. The chapter must address how a safety net is made real when the journey is long, the telephone unreliable and the literacy limited, and how it is documented. The dangerous situations are named where they belong, among them the discharged chest pain, the child sent home with a fever and the patient whose test result never arrived.

---

## Chapter 74 — Case-Based Learning

The reasoning made visible through worked cases that run from the first words of the presentation to the diagnosis, with the branch points shown rather than hidden. Each case should demonstrate a principle, whether it is the differential that was too narrow, the test that was ordered too early, the sign that was missed or the diagnosis that was correct but reached by luck, and each must be a constructed illustration rather than any identifiable patient. The cases are then re-read in a well-resourced and a low-resource version, so the listener sees the same reasoning with different instruments. The chapter should close on how to review one's own cases in the same way.

---

## Chapter 75 — Clinical Judgment Under Uncertainty

What to do when the diagnosis is not available and a decision is still required: the watchful waiting that is a plan and not an omission, the working diagnosis named honestly, and the assumptions that must be revisited as the patient changes. The difference between uncertainty that is tolerable and uncertainty that demands action, and the discipline of saying aloud what would change the plan. The reasoning is then placed in two settings, one where the patient can be observed with the resources of a hospital and one where the observation happens at home, and the chapter should be explicit about which of the two holds the greater risk and how it is mitigated. The dangerous situations are named, including the patient who is watched for too long.

---

## Chapter 76 — Ethics and Patient-Centered Diagnosis

The diagnosis as something that happens to a person rather than to a disease: how a diagnosis is disclosed, in what words, with what time allowed for the response, and the difference between telling and explaining. Consent to examination and to investigation, including the consent that is implicit in a queue and the consent that must be explicit. Shared decision-making where the options are genuinely balanced, and the honest admission of uncertainty. The chapter must also address the diagnostic error, the duty to disclose it, the apology and the duty to the next patient. The reasoning is then shown in a consultation with time and privacy and in one where neither exists and the essential elements must still be preserved.

---

## Chapter 77 — Teaching and Self-Assessment

How the reasoning of this book is learned and kept: deliberate practice on real cases, the habit of committing to a diagnosis before the result arrives, the review of one's own errors without humiliation, and the audit of a practice against its own outcomes. The feedback that rarely arrives by itself and must be sought, the colleague who can be asked, the meeting that reviews a death, and the record that makes review possible. The chapter should address the teacher as well, with the questioning that draws out reasoning instead of testing recall. It should close with the honest statement that expertise is maintained rather than acquired, and that the clinician who stops examining his own judgment is the one who will be surprised by it.

---

## Appendix A — Examination Checklists by System

The checklists for each system, written to be heard rather than read, in the order the examination is actually performed: general inspection, cardiovascular, respiratory, abdominal, neurological, musculoskeletal, head and neck, skin, genitourinary and breast, and mental state. Each checklist is a sequence of observations rather than a set of headings, so that a clinician can run it from memory at the bedside. The appendix should say which items may be omitted in an emergency and which may never be, and it should be usable in a well-equipped hospital and in a clinic with a stethoscope and a torch. The closing reminder is the same as the first chapter's, that no checklist substitutes for the patient in front of the clinician.

## Appendix B — Differential Diagnosis Tables by Symptom

The differential diagnosis tables spoken as prose, organized by presenting complaint and ordered by both likelihood and danger, covering the presentations of this book from fever and chest pain to bleeding, lumps and the altered mental state. Each table is read as a sequence of possibilities with the finding that moves each one up or down, so that the listener hears the reasoning rather than a list. The appendix should name the can't-miss entry of each table out loud, and it should distinguish what can be separated at the bedside from what needs a test or a referral. The closing caution is that a table organizes memory and does not diagnose anyone.

## Appendix C — Normal Values and Reference Ranges

The reference ranges for the adult, the child and the pregnant patient, spoken with the units written out in full and gathered by system: the vital signs, the blood count, the chemistry, the liver and kidney values, the inflammatory markers, the blood gas and the urine. Every value is presented as a range that illustrates reasoning rather than as a threshold, and the appendix must say at its opening and repeatedly thereafter that ranges differ between laboratories, between countries and between editions, and that the local result and the current guideline always govern. The appendix should also note the values that change with age, with pregnancy and with altitude, and those that must be read as a trend rather than a single measurement.

## Appendix D — Red-Flag Summary Cards

The red flags gathered as cards that could be carried in a pocket and heard in the memory: the dangerous findings of the history, the examination and the vital signs, grouped by presentation so that the clinician who is worried but cannot name why finds the reason. Each card states the finding, what it threatens and the action it requires, and each names the can't-miss diagnosis it protects against, among them the subarachnoid hemorrhage, the meningococcal rash, the ectopic pregnancy, the cord compression and the leaking aortic aneurysm. The appendix should be usable at the roadside and in a teaching hospital, and it should end with the instruction that a red flag is acted upon before it is explained.

## Appendix E — Glossary and Recommended Reading

The glossary of the terms used in this book, each defined in the words a clinician would use when explaining it to a student, and the short forms written out rather than abbreviated. The entries should cover the frameworks and the scores as well as the diseases, so that the listener can return to a single place for the meaning of the Vindicate framework, the Oldcarts framework, the quick sequential organ failure assessment or the Glasgow coma scale. The recommended reading that follows is a spoken guide to the kinds of text that extend each part, without web addresses and without citations. The appendix closes by restating the caution of the whole book, that it teaches reasoning and never replaces supervision, the current guideline or the judgment of the clinician with the patient.
