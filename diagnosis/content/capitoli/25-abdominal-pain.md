# Chapter 25 — Abdominal Pain

Abdominal pain is the symptom that most often asks the clinician to choose between patience and action, because the same dull ache can be a passing indigestion or the first sign of a catastrophe inside the abdomen. The reasoning that organizes this chapter is spatial and temporal at once: where the pain began, where it has traveled, what it feels like, and what makes it better or worse, all mapped onto the structures that lie beneath the skin. Pain is not a disease but a signal, and the clinician's task is to work out which structure is signaling and how urgently it must be answered. This chapter is educational material only, and it cannot replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. Every number that appears is an illustration of the reasoning, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it influences the care of a real person.

The lethal possibilities must be named before the comfortable ones, each with the finding that would betray it. A ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm produces pain that bores into the back or the flank, a pulsatile and expansile upper abdominal mass, a rapid pulse, and a pressure that is falling. A perforated viscus produces a rigid, board-like abdomen that hurts on the slightest movement, with bowel sounds that have gone quiet. A strangulated bowel, whether it is trapped in a hernia or twisted on its own mesentery, gives colicky pain in waves, vomiting, distension, and a failure to pass stool or gas. An ectopic pregnancy in a woman of childbearing age gives lower abdominal pain, a missed or unusual period, and the signs of internal bleeding. Mesenteric ischemia gives pain that is severe and out of proportion to a soft abdomen, and that mismatch is the finding that should alarm rather than reassure. These are the causes that force action, and they are named here so that the search begins with them rather than arriving at them last.

The acute abdomen is a syndrome rather than a diagnosis, and it is recognized by a pattern of findings that point to irritation or compromise of the peritoneum. Involuntary guarding is a tightening of the abdominal wall that the patient cannot relax on request, and rigidity is that tightening taken to a board-like extreme. Rebound tenderness is the pain produced when the examining hand is lifted, and percussion tenderness is the same irritation elicited by a gentler tap. Quiet bowel sounds, pain on coughing or movement, a rising pulse, and a falling pressure complete the picture. The clinician who assembles the whole pattern, rather than resting on one dramatic sign, is far harder to mislead. When peritonism, rigidity, or shock is present, the diagnosis can wait and the decision cannot, because the patient needs surgical help now whatever the underlying cause turns out to be.

The same reasoning must serve in two very different rooms. In a hospital with a laboratory, a bedside ultrasound, computed tomography, and a surgeon within call, the history and examination decide which of those tools to summon and how quickly. In a district clinic whose equipment is a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same history and examination decide something else: whether the abdomen can be watched safely here, or whether the patient must begin a journey that may take hours. The questions do not change between the rooms, only the consequences of the answers. A clinician who reasons well without imaging will use imaging better when it exists, because he already knows which question the image must answer. What travels between the two settings is not the equipment but the method.

Abdominal pain is a good place to practice the diagnostic loop, because the findings arrive in a sequence and each one should change the working explanation. The clinician forms a hypothesis from the site alone, predicts what the examination should then show, and looks for it deliberately, so that the abdomen becomes a set of answers to questions rather than a general impression. The pre-test probability is set by age, sex, the setting, and the pattern of previous illness, and it is why the same tenderness means different things in a young woman and in an older man. Time is part of the instrument, because an abdomen that is equivocal now may declare itself in a few hours, and the interval between observations is information in its own right. The clinician who records the time of each examination, and what changed since the last, is using the cheapest diagnostic test that medicine possesses.

Pain from the abdominal organs travels along nerves that follow the embryological origin of the organ, and that is why the site of early pain so often differs from the site of the disease. The foregut, which includes the stomach, the first part of the small bowel, the liver, the biliary tree, and the pancreas, sends its pain to the upper abdomen around the epigastrium. The midgut, which includes the rest of the small bowel and the first half of the colon, sends its pain to the region around the umbilicus. The hindgut, which includes the remaining colon and the rectum, sends its pain to the lower abdomen. Visceral pain of this kind is usually dull, poorly localized, and accompanied by nausea or sweating, because the organs themselves are not richly supplied with the nerves that localize touch and injury. When inflammation spreads to the peritoneum that lines the wall, the pain becomes sharp, well localized, and tender to the hand, and that change is one of the most useful transitions in the whole assessment.

Location is the first filter, and the clinician should ask the patient to point with one finger to where it hurts worst and to say where it started. Epigastric pain draws attention to the stomach, the duodenum, the pancreas, the biliary tree, and the lower esophagus, but also to the heart, because pain from the heart can be felt in the upper abdomen. Right upper quadrant pain draws attention to the gallbladder, the liver, and the diaphragm, and a right lower lung infection can produce pain in exactly the same place. Right lower quadrant pain draws attention to the appendix, the terminal ileum, the right ovary and tube, the right kidney and ureter, and a hernia at the right groin. Left lower quadrant pain draws attention to the sigmoid colon, the left ovary and tube, and the left kidney and ureter. Central and suprapubic pain draws attention to the bladder, the uterus, and the pelvic organs, and to the small bowel when it is obstructed or inflamed.

The migration of pain is often more informative than its final position. The classic movement of appendicitis is from a vague ache around the umbilicus to a sharp, well localized pain in the right lower quadrant, and that shift is produced when inflammation reaches the peritoneum of the abdominal wall. The migration of pain from the epigastrium to the right lower quadrant, or from the epigastrium to the back, carries its own implications and should be asked about specifically. Pain that begins in one flank and travels toward the groin along the line of the ureter suggests a stone, while pain that begins centrally and then becomes generalized suggests that a hollow organ has perforated. A woman with lower abdominal pain should always be asked whether the pain began suddenly and whether it was first on one side and then spread. The clinician who records when the pain moved, and what the patient was doing at the time, has gathered evidence that a single snapshot of the abdomen cannot provide.

Character is the next filter, and patients describe it in words that are worth recording verbatim. Colicky pain comes in waves, builds to a peak, and then subsides, and it is typical of a hollow organ contracting against an obstruction, whether that is the bowel, the bile duct, or the ureter. Constant pain that never lets go suggests inflammation, stretching of a capsule, or ischemia, and it is the character of pancreatitis, cholecystitis, and appendicitis once the inflammation is established. Burning pain that rises into the chest suggests reflux, while a tearing or ripping pain that moves suggests a dissection of the aorta, and the word the patient chooses should be written down exactly as it was spoken. Pain that is severe beyond the appearance of the abdomen is the signature of mesenteric ischemia, and that discrepancy should change the pace of everything that follows.

What provokes the pain and what relieves it narrows the field further. Pain that is worse after a fatty meal and settles over some hours suggests the gallbladder, while pain that comes when the stomach is empty and eases with food suggests the stomach or the duodenum. Pain that worsens on movement, coughing, or jolting suggests that the peritoneum is involved, and a patient who lies very still and resists being moved is giving a physical sign rather than an answer. Pain that eases when the patient leans forward may come from the pancreas, and pain that improves when the patient lies still with the knees drawn up may come from the peritoneum. Pain related to passing urine, to menstruation, to defecation, or to intercourse points toward the urinary tract, the pelvic organs, or the bowel.

The associated symptoms make the story a whole, and each one should be asked for directly rather than left to the patient to volunteer. Vomiting that comes before the pain suggests a medical cause, while vomiting that follows the pain suggests a surgical one, and the color and content of what is brought up carry further meaning. Diarrhea, constipation, and the failure to pass gas speak to the bowel, and any blood in the stool or vomit must be asked about explicitly. Fever, sweating, and shivering suggest infection or inflammation, while weight loss, a change in appetite, and a new anemia suggest a process that has been present for longer than the pain. Urinary frequency, burning, and blood in the urine shift attention to the urinary tract, and a missed period, vaginal bleeding, or a discharge shift attention to the pelvic organs. A cough, a sore throat, or a chest pain reminds the clinician that the abdomen can be an innocent bystander in a disease of the chest.

The context in which the pain appears changes what it probably means. Age and sex set the prior before a single question is asked, because a young woman with right lower quadrant pain has a different list from an older man with the same complaint. A previous abdominal operation raises the probability of adhesions and obstruction, and a previous hernia, stone, or ulcer raises the probability of its return. Regular medication is part of the history, and steroid treatment, immune suppressing treatment, and pain relief that masks a fever can all make a serious abdomen look quieter than it is. Alcohol use raises the probability of pancreatitis, liver disease, and gastritis, and smoking and age together raise the probability of an aortic aneurysm and of a tumor. Whether the patient is pregnant must be settled in any woman of childbearing age before the reasoning is allowed to settle elsewhere.

Duration changes the meaning of the same pain. A pain that has been present for a few hours is assessed as a possible emergency, while a pain that has been present for months is assessed as a chronic problem with a different list, though neither time frame excludes the other. Recurrent pain that comes in discrete attacks suggests a stone, a peptic ulcer, or an irritable bowel, while pain that has been constant and progressive over weeks suggests inflammation, a tumor, or a chronic infection. A pain that is new in an older patient, or that has changed in character in a patient with a long history, deserves a fresh assessment rather than an old diagnosis. The clinician should ask what the pain was like the first time, what has changed since, and what treatment has already been tried, because those answers often separate a benign pattern from an evolving one.

Examination begins at the end of the bed and not at the abdomen. The pulse, the pressure, the respiratory rate, and the temperature are recorded first, because a patient with a normal abdomen and abnormal vital signs is not a patient with a normal abdomen. The abdomen is then examined in the fixed order of inspection, auscultation, percussion, and palpation, with the most painful region saved for last. The hernial orifices, including both groins and the umbilicus, are sought deliberately in every patient, because a hernia can be the whole cause and can hide from a hurried look. The rectum is examined when there is bleeding, a suspected mass, or an unexplained picture, and the pelvic examination is performed with consent and a chaperone when the pelvic organs are in question. In any man or boy with abdominal pain, the testicles must be examined, because a torsion can present as abdominal pain alone and will be lost if the clinician does not look.

Pain in the right upper quadrant is one of the most common problems in any clinic, and the reasoning there is a good model of how location, character, and provocation combine. Biliary colic is a steady pain that builds over minutes and lasts for hours, often after a fatty meal, and it may settle as the stone falls back. Cholecystitis adds fever, a persistent pain, and tenderness under the right rib margin that catches when the examining hand presses below it while the patient breathes in. A perforated peptic ulcer can present in the same region with a sudden onset and a rigid abdomen, and the loss of liver dullness is one of the findings that points to free gas. A right lower lung infection, a heart attack, and a kidney infection can each produce pain that the patient places in the right upper quadrant, so the chest and the urine belong in the assessment even when the abdomen is the complaint. Where the pain is right upper quadrant and the patient is jaundiced or feverish, the decision about imaging and about help should not wait for the picture to become obvious.

Epigastric pain is shared by the stomach, the pancreas, the biliary tree, the lower esophagus, and the heart, and the examination must be broad enough to hold all of them. Pancreatitis produces a steady, boring pain that often radiates to the back and eases when the patient leans forward, and it is frequently accompanied by vomiting that does not relieve the pain. A perforated ulcer produces a sudden, severe pain with a board-like abdomen, and it is one of the emergencies in which the diagnosis is made at the bedside rather than in the laboratory. A heart attack can present as epigastric pain, sweating, and nausea, and in an older patient with risk factors an electrocardiogram, or ECG, must be part of the assessment. An aortic aneurysm that is leaking or expanding produces pain that reaches the back, and the palpation of the aorta is therefore never omitted in an older patient with new epigastric or back pain.

Right lower quadrant pain assembles a list that runs from the trivial to the fatal. Appendicitis typically begins as a vague ache around the umbilicus, moves to the right lower quadrant, and is accompanied by loss of appetite, nausea, and a low fever. An ectopic pregnancy must be considered in every woman of childbearing age, and a pregnancy test changes the reasoning more than any other single step, while the signs of internal bleeding change the pace. Torsion of the right ovary produces a sudden, severe pain with vomiting and a tender mass, and it is a surgical emergency of the same order as testicular torsion. A hernia at the right groin can present with pain in the quadrant and a swelling that the patient has not noticed, so the groin is examined before the diagnosis is settled. The decision to observe, to image, or to call for surgery turns on how the findings move over the next few hours as much as on the first examination.

Left lower quadrant pain in an older adult usually raises the possibility of inflammation of the sigmoid colon, which produces a steady pain, a low fever, and a change in bowel habit. Torsion or rupture of a left ovarian cyst, a kidney stone, and a hernia at the left groin are the other frequent explanations, and constipation with a loaded colon can imitate them all. Left upper quadrant pain draws attention to the spleen, the stomach, and the tail of the pancreas, and a spleen that is enlarged and tender suggests infection or a blood disorder rather than a surgical cause. Pain that the patient describes as coming from beneath the left ribs and radiating to the left shoulder suggests irritation of the diaphragm, and that finding should send the clinician back to the chest and to the abdominal catastrophes that can leak upward. The region of the pain is a starting point, and the clinician who stops there will mistake a referred pain for a local one. Every abdominal complaint deserves the same pair of questions, which structure is most likely, and which dangerous structure could produce this same pain.

Testicular torsion deserves its own place in a chapter on abdominal pain, because it is a diagnosis that is lost when the clinician trusts the location of the complaint. The testicle develops in the abdomen and descends, and its nerve supply still reaches the abdomen, so torsion can present as lower abdominal pain with vomiting and no complaint about the scrotum at all. The finding that reveals it is a high, swollen, tender testicle that lies horizontally and hurts when it is lifted, and the absence of the cremasteric reflex on that side supports the diagnosis. The window in which the testis can be saved is short, and the clinician who examines the scrotum in every boy and man with lower abdominal pain will find the cases that others miss. This is a constructed reminder rather than a portrait of a real patient, and it exists only to fix the habit of looking beyond the site of the pain.

Pain that is generalized or that has no clear home requires a different order of thinking. Widespread peritonitis produces a rigid abdomen, quiet bowel sounds, and pain on the slightest movement, and it matters less which organ perforated than that the patient needs surgery at once. Bowel obstruction produces colicky pain in waves, vomiting, distension, and the failure to pass stool or gas, and the bowel sounds may be high-pitched and rushing at first and quiet later. A previous operation raises the probability of adhesions, a palpable mass raises the possibility of a tumor, and a hernia at any orifice raises the possibility of strangulation, which turns a mechanical problem into a dead bowel. Mesenteric ischemia produces pain that is severe and unrelenting while the abdomen remains soft and the examination looks deceptively benign, and it is one of the diagnoses most often made too late. Gastroenteritis and a viral illness can produce diffuse pain, but they are diagnoses of exclusion in a patient who is deteriorating, and they are never made at the expense of the dangerous ones.

The patient whose pain is mild or whose abdomen is soft may still be the patient in the most danger, and this is the trap that fills the surgical ward. The older patient, the patient whose immune response is suppressed by illness or treatment, and the patient with poorly controlled diabetes can all mount a blunted response, so fever, rigidity, and even tenderness may be absent while the disease advances. A woman in the later stages of pregnancy has her organs displaced and her abdominal wall stretched, so the pain and the signs may be both shifted and muted. A patient who has received pain relief may look comfortable while the peritoneum is already inflamed, and a patient who is confused or drowsy may not report pain at all. In all of these people the decision rests on the trajectory and the repeated examination rather than on the first set of findings, and the threshold for asking for help is lower rather than higher. A soft abdomen in a patient who looks unwell is a reason to examine again, not a reason to relax.

Where resources are available, the examination becomes the question that tests are asked to answer. A bedside ultrasound can show free fluid, an inflamed gallbladder, a dilated aorta, a pregnancy inside or outside the uterus, and an obstructed kidney, and it can be performed while the patient is still being examined. Computed tomography, or CT, gives a more complete picture of perforation, obstruction, pancreatitis, and masses, while magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answers particular questions in the liver and the biliary tree. A urine test, a blood count, and a pregnancy test are inexpensive and frequently decisive, and they should be chosen because their result would change a decision rather than because they are available. The clinician should say what he expects each test to show before it is performed, so that a result which contradicts the expectation is recognized as a surprise rather than absorbed without thought. A normal scan in a clearly unwell patient is a reason to think again, and never a reason to dismiss the examination that prompted it.

In a district clinic the task is not the same diagnosis but the same reasoning pushed to a sharper decision. Without imaging the clinician must rely on the history, the vital signs, the repeated examination, and the few tests that are actually within reach. A patient who is stable, whose pain is settling, and whose abdomen remains soft after a period of observation may be watched safely, provided that the plan for review is explicit and the patient can return. A patient with peritonism, rigidity, shock, a pulsatile abdominal mass, or a pain that is severe beyond the appearance of the abdomen must travel, and the decision to send should be made early rather than after a second look confirms what the first already suggested. A thermometer turns the impression of fever into a number that can be followed, a torch allows the hernial orifices and the anus to be examined properly, and a watch allows the pulse and the respiratory rate to be counted rather than estimated. The clinic's advantage is not its equipment but its continuity, and the clinician who sees the patient again in an hour may learn more than a scan would have said.

A constructed illustration may fix the reasoning. Imagine a man of about 50 with a sudden severe epigastric pain that has moved to the back, a pulse of 110 beats per minute, and a pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury. Those figures are illustrations of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the rate and the pressure that should trigger action differ with age, fitness, medication, and the definition in use. The deliberate palpation of the aorta, however, reveals an expansile mass, and the whole plan changes within a minute. Now imagine a woman of about 25 with lower abdominal pain and a fainting episode, and the clinician who asks about her last period before deciding that the pain is colic. Neither patient is real, and neither should be read as a portrait, for they are constructed only to show how one further question can reverse the order of a differential.

The pain itself is never a diagnosis, and a patient who is sent away with a label that the findings do not support is a patient at risk. The clinician should record what was found, what was considered, and what was excluded, so that the next reader can pick up the thread rather than start again. Safety netting is part of the decision, because the patient must be told which symptoms should bring him back, how quickly, and where to go, and that advice must be specific enough to be followed. A pain that has been present for a long time and is unchanged is a different problem from a pain that has appeared in the last few hours, and the tempo of a complaint is one of its most important features. The clinician who is uncertain should say so, and should convert that uncertainty into a plan for review rather than into a confident answer that the evidence does not support.

To summarize, abdominal pain is read by its site, its migration, its character, and what provokes and relieves it, and those features are then mapped onto the structures that lie beneath the skin. The acute abdomen is a syndrome rather than a diagnosis, and it is recognized by peritonism, rigidity, and shock, which demand action before the underlying cause is known. The lethal causes, among them a ruptured aortic aneurysm, a perforated viscus, a strangulated bowel, mesenteric ischemia, an ectopic pregnancy, and a testicular torsion, are named at the bedside and each carries its own revealing finding. The same reasoning is used in a hospital where imaging and surgery are at hand and in a district clinic where the task is to recognize the abdomen that must travel. Every number in this chapter, whether a rate, a pressure, a temperature, a score, or an interval, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient whose abdomen is soft but whose story is alarming is a patient to be examined again and discussed, never a patient to be reassured and sent away.
