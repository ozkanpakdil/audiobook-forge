# Chapter 3 — Building a Differential Diagnosis

Every diagnosis begins as a question, and a differential diagnosis is the shape of that question written down. It is not a list of every disease that could produce the patient's complaint, because such a list is both endless and useless. A differential is a working set of explanations, ordered by likelihood and by danger at the same time. The clinician who carries that set into the room behaves differently from one who carries a single hypothesis. One looks for evidence that will strengthen or weaken each possibility, while the other looks only for confirmation of a decision already made. The first is doing diagnosis, and the second is doing something else and calling it diagnosis.

When a learner first tries to be thorough, the result is usually an undifferentiated catalogue in which a common cold and a rare metabolic disease sit side by side with equal weight. The catalogue feels safe because it looks complete, yet it offers no guidance about what to do first, and a list that cannot direct action is not yet a diagnostic tool. Thoroughness in generating possibilities and discipline in ordering them are separate skills, and the second is much the harder. Generating broadly ensures that no dangerous possibility is overlooked, while narrowing spends the patient's time and the clinician's attention on what matters most. Breadth without order becomes paralysis, and order without breadth becomes error. The two must be practised together, in that sequence, at every encounter.

It helps to name the movements explicitly. The first is divergence, which asks what could possibly be going on, and at this stage the clinician should be generous, because the only cost of an idea is a moment of thought. The second is convergence, which asks which of those ideas the evidence supports, and here the clinician should be strict. Many clinicians perform both movements unconsciously and then wonder why their first impression survives, because an unexamined impression quietly becomes the whole list. Writing the divergent list down, even in shorthand, protects against that, since an idea that has been named can be examined and discarded. Breadth comes first, and the knife comes afterwards.

One of the oldest and most dependable frameworks is anatomical. The clinician begins with the structure that hurts, or whose function has failed, and works outward through the layers and neighbours that share its nerve supply, its blood supply, its drainage or its space. Pain in the right upper abdomen draws the mind through the liver and its capsule, the gallbladder, the duodenum, the colon, the diaphragm, the pleura and the rib cage, and even the heart. Each structure suggests its own mechanisms, and the framework is powerful because it forces the question of what lies next door. Anatomy also prevents the clinician from forgetting the compartment that shares a nerve root with the site of complaint, which is how hip disease presents as knee pain.

Anatomy has a second use that is easy to overlook, because it tells the clinician where to look during the examination. If the working list includes the gallbladder, the pleura and the rib cage, then the hands and the stethoscope have a route to follow, and the findings will either narrow the list or reopen its breadth. Anatomy alone, however, is a map rather than an explanation, and it traps the clinician who stops at the organ without asking what is happening inside it. A diseased structure creates a differential, but the diseases of that structure form a separate list. The anatomical frame answers where, and it must be followed at once by a frame that answers how.

The second framework asks which mechanism could produce this finding, and its categories cover almost anything a patient can bring. Obstruction narrows or blocks a tube, whether it carries bile, urine, blood, air or intestinal contents, and it announces itself through pain, distension and the signs of what has backed up. Inflammation damages tissue and recruits the body's defenses, producing pain, heat, swelling and loss of function. Infection is the invasion and multiplication of an organism, local or systemic. Ischemia starves tissue of blood and punishes delay cruelly. Bleeding removes blood from the vessels, sometimes visibly and sometimes into a cavity where nothing is seen. Metabolic disturbance corrupts the chemistry every organ depends on, and malignancy grows without regard for the controls that restrain a cell.

Running these mechanisms against the anatomical list produces a grid far richer than either framework alone, and that grid is what the experienced clinician consults. A tender swelling in the leg may be infection of the skin, a clot in a deep vein, bleeding into the muscle or a tumor, and each demands a different urgency and examination. The mechanism frame also rescues possibilities that are easy to forget because they are not local: a metabolic disturbance may present as confusion, and a malignant process as weight loss long before it produces a lump. When a presentation resists explanation, the fault is often that only one mechanism has been run.

To make that walk through the mechanisms memorable, many teachers use a mnemonic that spells a word: Vindicate. It is pronounced as an ordinary English word, and each letter stands for a family of causes, so the framework can be carried in the head and recited at the bedside. The first letter stands for vascular causes, the diseases of the blood vessels and of the flow within them. The next stands for infectious causes, from the trivial and local to the overwhelming and systemic. The next stands for neoplastic causes, both benign and malignant, and the next for degenerative causes, the slow wearing down of tissue in later life. The remaining letters stand for iatrogenic and intoxicated causes, congenital causes, autoimmune causes, traumatic causes and endocrine causes.

The strength of Vindicate is that it is exhaustive in a way that unaided memory is not. Run honestly, it surfaces the congenital anomaly present since birth, the drug reaction the patient does not think of as a medicine, the endocrine disease that explains a cluster of complaints, and the vascular event that the clinician, absorbed by the inflammation in front of him, has never considered. It is especially valuable when the presentation is vague or the obvious explanation does not fit, because it forces attention onto categories that habit skips. It also guards against the trap of the single organ, since one presentation may begin in another system. Used as a checklist for the written list, it gives the record a completeness a casual note never has.

Vindicate has a serious weakness, the weakness of every generative framework: it treats all its categories as equally available, when they are not equally likely. An honest run through the letters places a rare congenital syndrome and a common infection side by side, and a careless clinician begins to reason as though they were equally likely. The exotic diagnosis is also more memorable and more interesting than the ordinary one, and interest is a quiet bias in its favour. A framework that widens the imagination must therefore be paired with a discipline that narrows it, or the differential becomes a museum of possibilities rather than a tool. Vindicate tells the clinician what to consider, never how likely anything is.

Between the broad list and the probable list sits the can't-miss list, and it is the most important list in the exercise. These are the diagnoses that must be excluded before any other explanation is accepted, because they kill or disable quickly and the opportunity to prevent that harm is brief. The list is drawn up before the patient is examined, from the complaint itself, and kept at the front of the mind throughout. A patient with severe headache has a can't-miss list including bleeding around the brain, infection of the meninges and a rise in pressure inside the skull, and the findings that would reveal each are sought deliberately. A patient with chest pain has one including a heart attack, a torn aorta and a clot in the lung, and the early examination and tests separate those from the far commoner chest wall strain.

The habit of asking, for every patient, what would kill this person soonest is what separates safe practice from merely clever practice. It is not pessimism; it is a triage of reasoning, a decision about which hypothesis earns the first minutes of attention. The dangerous diagnosis is not always the likely one, and the point of the can't-miss list is that risk does not wait for probability to be established. Until the serious possibility is excluded, the comfortable explanation remains a hypothesis rather than a conclusion. The finding that would reveal the danger must be named in advance, because a sign that is not sought is rarely seen.

With the broad generative list, the can't-miss list and the list of what is common in hand, the clinician ranks. Ranking is done by likelihood and by danger at the same time, and those orderings disagree so often that the disagreement becomes information. Likelihood orders possibilities by how frequently they occur in patients like this one, while danger orders them by how much is lost if they are missed. The most probable diagnosis is usually benign and the most dangerous usually uncommon, so the two orderings routinely put different names first. A clinician ranking only by likelihood will miss the catastrophe, and one ranking only by danger will investigate everyone exhaustively and harm them. The skill is to act on the dangerous ranking first while keeping the likely one in view.

Likelihood is not a property of a disease in the abstract; it is a property of a disease in a particular patient in a particular place. Prevalence sets the odds before any finding is known, and the setting determines which prevalences apply. Chest pain that is usually muscular in a young person in a clinic may be usually cardiac in an older person arriving by ambulance, and the starting point must shift. A district hospital with no laboratory, a crowded camp, an urban emergency department and a private office each present a different mix of disease, and a differential imported unchanged from one to another will mislead. This is why the first question about any remembered statistic is where it came from and whether the patient in front of you belongs to that population.

A fever in a returning traveller, in a person whose immune defenses have been destroyed, in a small child and on a surgical ward after an operation are different problems, even when the measured temperature is identical. The clinician who knows the local prevalence carries a useful prior, while one who does not is guided by the most dramatic recent case, a poor substitute. Where a frequency is quoted as something like 1 in 4 in one place, that proportion is a reminder of how much the setting matters and not a number to carry unchanged elsewhere, since every such figure must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, where the numbers differ between countries, editions and the populations served. When prevalence is genuinely unknown, the honest approach is to widen the list, weight the dangerous possibilities more heavily, and use the examination to create information rather than confirm a guess.

After prevalence, the characteristics of the individual patient do the next work of ordering. Age moves whole families of diagnosis up and down: the same joint pain suggests injury or inflammatory disease in a young person and degeneration in an older one, and the same breathlessness suggests asthma in a child and heart failure in an older adult. Sex changes the prior in ways that are sometimes biological and sometimes social, and a stereotype must not stand in for a question. Background includes where the patient has lived and traveled, which infections are common there, and which diseases run in the family. Exposures include work, animals, food, water, sexual history, injected drugs, alcohol, tobacco and every medicine taken, including those bought without a prescription and those borrowed from another person.

Exposures most often convert a vague list into a short one, and they deserve to be asked about directly rather than hoped for. A cough in a person who keeps birds, a rash in a person who has walked through long grass, a fever in a person who has recently received blood, and a weakness in a person who preserves food at home all point in particular directions. None of these details is a diagnosis, and each must be weighed against the whole picture, but each raises the odds of a specific possibility enough to reorder the list. The patient's own beliefs and fears sometimes reveal an exposure the clinician would not have thought to ask about. Listening for the detail that does not fit the expected story is one of the most efficient diagnostic acts available.

A differential diagnosis is not finished on the first day; it is a living picture repainted as the illness evolves. Time is a diagnostic instrument in its own right, because the trajectory of a symptom carries information no measurement can. A pain that was constant and severe and is now settling, a fever that has traced a particular rhythm across 3 days, a weakness that ascends rather than stays still, and a confusion that fluctuates rather than deepens all tell different stories. That interval of 3 days is only an illustration of how a pattern is read, and the interval that truly matters must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such durations differ between countries, editions and wards. The clinician who writes the first list and stops looking has abandoned the most powerful tool available, the patient's own future. The findings that were absent at the beginning must be sought again at every contact, because their appearance changes everything.

A differential is closed in one of a small number of ways, and the clinician should be able to say which has been used. Exclusion means a diagnosis has been made unlikely enough by evidence to be set aside, and it is the most common way a list shrinks. Confirmation means the evidence is strong enough to name the disease, and it is the way a differential finally disappears. Watching means deliberately keeping the list open and letting time reveal which branch the illness follows, which is legitimate when the patient is stable and the dangerous possibilities have been addressed. Accepting a working diagnosis means treating the most probable explanation as the basis for action while remaining ready to revise it, and it is honest only when recorded rather than assumed.

Exclusion is harder than it sounds, because excluding a diagnosis requires more than failing to find it. The absence of a finding is informative only when the clinician knows how often that finding is absent in the disease being excluded, and only when the examination or test could have detected it. A normal examination in a patient who was not examined properly excludes nothing. Confirmation carries the opposite danger: a positive finding may belong to a different disease, or to no disease, and a clinician who stops at the first confirmation may never notice a second process running alongside the first. Both movements require the same question: what would the evidence look like if the hypothesis were false?

Watching deserves more respect than it receives, because many illnesses declare themselves only with time, and a premature label is more dangerous than a deliberate wait. A wait is not the absence of a plan; it is a plan with defined observations, a defined interval, findings that would change the decision, and a route back to care. A plan that promises review after 24 hours but names no finding to look for is not watching, and the interval chosen must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than copied from habit, because safe observation periods differ between conditions, countries and clinics. The patient must be part of that plan, because the clinician cannot watch from a distance. If the waiting patient does not know what to look for and whom to contact, the differential has not been closed but only postponed. Safe watching is written down and explained, and it names the finding that would end the wait immediately.

Accepting a working diagnosis is the everyday end of the process, and it is not a confession of uncertainty but a statement about what is probable enough to act on. The working diagnosis carries the treatment decision, the follow-up plan and the safety net, and it must be revised when the patient does not follow the expected course. The clinician who treats a probable diagnosis after actively considering the dangerous alternatives is practising well. The clinician who treats the first label that came to mind, without ever having written the list, is practising on hope. The difference is invisible in the prescription and visible in the reasoning that preceded it.

Consider a constructed illustration, invented to show the method and describing no real patient. A middle-aged person arrives with upper abdominal pain that began suddenly and has been constant for several hours, with vomiting but no diarrhea. The clinician generates broadly first: the stomach, duodenum, pancreas, gallbladder, liver, colon, aorta and heart all appear on the sheet, together with the possibility that the pain is muscular, referred or metabolic. The can't-miss list is drawn before the examination: a perforated ulcer, pancreatitis, a ruptured aortic aneurysm, a heart attack presenting as abdominal pain, and, if the patient could be pregnant, a pregnancy outside the uterus. The anatomy then directs the hands and the mechanism frame directs the questions, asking whether obstruction, inflammation, ischemia or bleeding is at work. The list is ranked, the patient is examined, and the ranking changes as the findings arrive.

A well-built differential also protects the patient from unnecessary investigation, because a test is worth doing only when its result would move the list. If two possibilities would be managed identically, the test that separates them changes nothing and exposes the patient to cost, discomfort and incidental findings. The list tells the clinician which question is actually open, and it is that open question the next test should answer. When the list is vague, tests multiply; when the list is sharp, tests become few and decisive. This is the practical reason to spend time on the differential before ordering anything, and the reason a good list saves money and harm.

At the moment of first contact, the differential is often little more than a set of dangerous possibilities and a direction of travel. The clinician may not yet have a diagnosis, but the list already tells him what to ask next, what to examine and what to observe. That early list is provisional and should be spoken as provisional, because a diagnosis announced too early is hard to take back and hard to unhear. As the history unfolds, items move up and down, and the clinician should be able to say aloud which item has moved and why. The list is the visible form of the clinician's thinking, and keeping it visible is what makes the thinking correctable.

Every framework in this book is a memory aid and not a diagnostic instrument, and that distinction matters more than it may appear. A framework cannot examine the patient, weigh a finding, know the local prevalence, or notice that this patient is unlike those in whom the rule was derived. Vindicate will produce a category for almost any complaint, but a category is not a probability and a filled-in mnemonic is not a diagnosis. Frameworks also encode the assumptions of those who built them, including the populations studied and the diseases expected, and those assumptions travel badly. The clinician who uses the structure to remember and then returns to the patient to think is safe, while the one who mistakes the structure for the answer is not.

There is a further limit that is easy to state and hard to practice: the differential belongs to the patient and not to the symptom. Two patients with the same complaint may need entirely different lists, because their age, setting, history and physiology differ, and a list copied from a textbook is a starting point rather than a conclusion. The clinician must ask whether the population behind a framework resembles the person in front of him, and must be willing to set the framework aside when the patient does not fit. Rules derived in one setting have repeatedly misled clinicians in another, and the honest response is to hold them loosely rather than discard them. Certainty that arrives too early is the commonest reason a diagnosis turns out to be wrong.

The most seductive error in this process is the diagnosis that explains everything too neatly. A pattern that fits perfectly feels like a solution, and the clinician relaxes, stops generating, and stops looking for the finding that would contradict it. Yet completeness of fit is not evidence of truth, and several diseases can produce the same pattern, so the comfortable explanation may be the one the clinician knows best. The differential is the antidote, because it keeps competing explanations alive until the evidence has separated them. A clinician who can still name the alternatives after reaching a diagnosis is far safer than one who cannot.

A differential that exists only in the clinician's head cannot be checked, taught or handed over, and much of its value comes from being spoken or written in a form someone else can examine. Saying the list aloud forces the clinician to hear which possibilities have been considered and which have been quietly ignored. A colleague who looks at the same patient can attack the reasoning rather than repeat it, and the diagnosis improves. The list should also record what would change it, so the next clinician inherits not a verdict but a working question. This matters most at handover, when the reasoning is often compressed into a single label and everything supporting it lost.

Teaching the differential is largely a matter of teaching the discipline of holding several ideas at once without committing to one. The learner should generate before evaluating, name the dangerous possibilities before the comfortable ones, and say what evidence would move each item up or down the list. A teacher who asks what else it could be, then what would prove that it is not, builds a habit that survives long after the facts are forgotten. A differential debated at the bedside, with permission, also teaches the patient that uncertainty is normal and that the reasoning is being taken seriously.

The frameworks in this book were not designed for well-equipped hospitals, and they work best where resources are fewest, because they replace tests with thought. Where there is no laboratory and no imaging, the differential must be built from the history, the examination and the passage of time, and every dangerous possibility must be identified by a sign the clinician can elicit. In that setting the can't-miss list becomes the central document, and the decision to refer or to treat rests on the findings available rather than the ideal. A framework that requires a scanner to be useful is not useful in a district clinic, and its categories must be translated into questions and hands. The discipline of the differential is portable in a way no piece of equipment can be.

Uncertainty is not a failure of the method; it is the medium in which the method works. A clinician who feels no uncertainty is usually not thinking about the alternatives, and one paralysed by it is not acting on the evidence available. The differential holds those two failures apart, because it acknowledges that the answer is not yet known while organising the next step anyway. Over time, the clinician learns which lists recur, which items are almost always benign, and which quiet possibility deserves a second look even when everything fits. That accumulated judgement is what a framework can guide but never replace.

When the vital signs are abnormal, they reorder the list before any diagnosis is named. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius, a pulse of 110 beats per minute and a systolic pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury each push infection, bleeding and circulatory failure toward the top, whatever the patient came in complaining of. Each of those thresholds is a teaching illustration rather than a universal rule, and every one of them must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, laboratories and wards. The pattern, not the number, is what travels: an abnormal vital sign is a claim on the clinician's attention that outranks the original complaint, and it should send the clinician back to the list rather than to a conclusion.

To summarize, the differential diagnosis is the ordered set of explanations that turns a complaint into a plan, built by generating broadly, framing the problem anatomically and pathophysiologically, running the Vindicate categories as a memory aid, and narrowing against a can't-miss list drawn up before the patient was examined. It is ranked by likelihood and danger at once, adjusted for prevalence, setting, age, sex, background and exposure, and repainted as findings unfold. It is closed by exclusion, by confirmation, by deliberate watching, or by an honestly recorded working diagnosis. None of this replaces seeing the patient, and no framework or remembered statistic can stand in for the examination and judgement of the clinician present. Every number here is an illustration rather than a universal rule, and any threshold must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, laboratories and wards. Educational material of this kind supports training and supervision rather than replacing them, and it is never a reason to delay care for a patient who is deteriorating.
