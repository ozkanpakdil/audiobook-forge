# Chapter 15 — Neurological Examination

The neurological examination is an exercise in localization rather than a parade of tests, and every maneuver in it answers one question about where in the nervous system the trouble lies. A finding earns its place only because it narrows that map, so a clinician who holds the map in mind examines with a purpose. This chapter describes one method in two forms, a screening round for any bedside with nothing beyond a torch, a thermometer and a stethoscope, and a focused examination for a hospital where imaging and specialist help are at hand. What follows is educational description and not a protocol, and it is no substitute for training, supervision, the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient, or the rules of the local service. Two emergencies dominate this territory and are named now so that they are carried through every step: the stroke that still has time for its blood supply to be restored, and the compression of the spinal cord that has almost none. Both are revealed by findings the examination below is designed to expose before any image is made.

Everything begins with the level of consciousness, because a patient who is not fully awake cannot be examined reliably, and because a falling level is the most sensitive sign of a nervous system that is failing. The first observations are made before any instrument is touched: whether the eyes are open, whether the patient answers, whether the answer makes sense, and whether a command is followed. The Glasgow coma scale is the standard formalization of those observations, converting eye opening, verbal response and the best motor response into a number that can be handed to the next clinician without ambiguity. The scale runs from 3 to 15, and the total is less informative than its parts, since a patient who is mute from a stroke and a patient who is deeply unconscious may reach a similar figure by different routes. That range is a convention rather than a law of nature, so confirm the version in use against the current guideline and the local protocol, and remember that a patient who is intubated, sedated, deaf or speaking another language cannot be scored fairly.

Beyond the level of consciousness lies the content of the mind, which localizes more loosely but still matters. Orientation to time, place and person, attention, short term recall and the use of language are tested within the conversation, by asking the patient to repeat three words, to count backwards, to name common objects and to obey a two step command. A patient who is alert but cannot repeat a phrase has a problem of language, while one who speaks fluently but produces nonsense has a problem of comprehension. Confusion that has developed over hours is not a psychiatric event until a metabolic and a structural cause have both been excluded, and the bedside glucose is the most useful test in that search. What the examination adds is whether a focal deficit accompanies the confusion, since that raises the probability of a structural cause and changes the speed of everything that follows.

The most reversible dangerous mimic of a neurological emergency is hypoglycemia, and the finding that reveals it is a glucose measurement made at the bedside before any other interpretation is offered. A drowsy, sweating patient who cannot give a coherent history may be labeled as having had a stroke by an examiner who does not measure glucose, and that error is tragic because the remedy is simple. A blood glucose of 3 millimoles per liter is treated as hypoglycemia in many services, but the level that matters depends on the patient, the meter and the local protocol, so verify the cut off in the current guideline and the local protocol. The same caution applies to a patient known to have diabetes who arrives with focal weakness, because both high and low glucose can produce a deficit that resolves when the level is corrected. In a clinic without a laboratory, a glucose meter is one of the few pieces of equipment worth its space, and where even that is absent the history of missed meals or recent illness must carry the suspicion.

The cranial nerves are examined in the order that follows the face from the eyes outward, and each step answers a question that a different part of the brainstem must satisfy. Pupils are inspected in a dim room with a torch, and size, symmetry and the reaction to light are recorded together, because a pupil of 3 millimeters that reacts briskly means something different from the same pupil that is fixed. Any figure for pupil size is a local convention, so the number that defines abnormality must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, and the more useful question is whether the two pupils are equal and whether each responds. A dilated pupil that does not react, especially with a drooping eyelid and an eye turned outward, points to compression of the third cranial nerve, which in a patient with a headache warns of an aneurysm or of a brain beginning to herniate. Eye movements are tested in all directions, and double vision and nystagmus are recorded for their direction and for the position that provokes them.

Visual fields are tested by confrontation, one eye at a time, and a defect that respects the vertical midline points behind the eye while one that respects the horizontal midline points to the optic nerve. The back of the eye is examined with an ophthalmoscope where one exists, because swelling of the optic disc signals raised pressure inside the head. The face is inspected at rest and then asked to move, and the distinction that matters is whether the forehead wrinkles on the weak side, since a weakness that spares the forehead suggests a lesion above the brainstem while one that includes it suggests the facial nerve itself. Hearing is screened by rubbing fingers beside each ear or by whispering, and the gag reflex and soft palate are observed in any patient who is drowsy, has swallowed badly or has a hoarse voice. Difficulty with speech, swallowing or the protection of the airway demands immediate attention because it predicts aspiration and respiratory failure.

Motor examination begins with observation, because wasting, an abnormal posture or a limb that moves less than its partner is visible before any test is performed. The patient is asked to hold both arms out with the eyes closed, and a slow downward drift of one arm with the palm turning inward is a subtle sign of an upper motor neuron lesion that is easily missed. Tone is assessed by moving each limb passively through its range, and the examiner is feeling for resistance rather than for power. Increased tone that gives way suddenly, like the closing of a clasp knife, suggests a lesion of the corticospinal pathway, while a constant resistance throughout the movement suggests the rigidity of a basal ganglia disorder. Reduced tone suggests a lower motor neuron lesion, a cerebellar lesion or the acute phase after injury to the cord, and the limb that feels different is the one that deserves the closer look.

Power is tested in each limb group, and the examiner resists the movement rather than merely asking for it, because a weak patient will often appear to move well when no load is applied. The conventional grade runs from 0, meaning no movement, to 5, meaning normal power, and the grade is useful only when it is anchored to what the patient was asked to do. These numbers are a shared shorthand rather than a measurement, and the boundaries between them must be read according to the current guideline and the local protocol, which differ between training programmes. The pattern of weakness matters more than its severity, so the examiner notes whether it is proximal or distal, whether it affects one side, both legs or one leg and one arm, and whether the face or tongue is involved. Weakness that begins in the feet and climbs, especially with reduced reflexes and lost sensation, can reach the muscles of breathing, and a record that says only that the patient is weak has thrown away the most valuable half of the finding.

The sensory examination is the most subjective part of the encounter and therefore the one where technique matters most. Light touch is tested with a wisp of cotton, pinprick with a fresh disposable pin, temperature with a cold or a warm object, vibration with a tuning fork on a bony prominence, and joint position by moving a toe or a finger slightly while the eyes are closed. The modalities travel by different pathways, so a patient with normal vibration sense may still have lost pain and temperature, and the reverse is equally possible. The examiner maps any loss by its border rather than its extent, because a sensory level across the trunk is one of the most important localizing findings in neurology, and a border that shifts with distraction suggests that the loss is not organic.

A loss of sensation in a single dermatome points to a spinal nerve root, while a loss that follows the territory of a named nerve points to the nerve itself, and a loss that begins at a level and continues downwards points to the spinal cord. A glove and stocking pattern affecting the feet and hands symmetrically suggests a length dependent process such as the damage of diabetes or of alcohol, and a patchy loss that follows no rule may point to the brain. Cortical sensory testing, in which a number is drawn on the palm or an object is placed in the hand with the eyes closed, is abnormal in lesions of the sensory cortex even when the simpler modalities are preserved. The sensory examination is unreliable in a drowsy, distracted or frightened patient, so a normal result there is weak evidence while an abnormal result in a fully alert patient is strong.

Tendon reflexes are tested with a tendon hammer where one is available and with the edge of a hand, a stethoscope or a firm finger where it is not, and the response is compared left with right. The biceps, triceps, suprapatellar and ankle jerks sample the roots of the upper and lower limbs, and the grading from 0 to 4 is a shared vocabulary whose meaning depends on the examiner and the service. A reflex that is much brisker on one side than the other suggests a lesion of the corticospinal pathway above the level of that reflex, while a reflex that is lost suggests a problem in the nerve, the root or the muscle it supplies. The plantar response is elicited by stroking the sole and watching the toes, since extension of the great toe with fanning of the others is a sign of a corticospinal lesion in an adult, and sustained clonus at the ankle is a further sign of the same pathway. The anal reflex, obtained by stroking the skin around the anus and watching for contraction, is easy to omit and carries decisive information when the spinal cord is in question, and that scale is a local convention to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The cerebellum is examined by asking for movements that require accuracy rather than strength. Finger to nose testing, first with the eyes open and then closed, reveals a tremor that worsens as the target is approached, which is the intention tremor of a cerebellar lesion. Heel to shin testing is performed on each side, and rapid alternating movements such as turning the palm up and down show a slowness and irregularity that is called dysdiadochokinesia. The signs of a cerebellar lesion appear on the same side as the lesion, unlike the signs of a cerebral lesion, and this rule alone prevents a great deal of confusion. Truncal ataxia is tested by asking the patient to sit without support, and a patient who cannot sit steadily should not be asked to walk unaided, while scanning speech and a coarse nystagmus complete the picture of a cerebellar problem.

Gait is the most informative single maneuver in the neurological examination, because it integrates power, tone, sensation, coordination and balance in one act. The patient is asked to walk a few steps while the examiner watches from the side and then the front, noting the width of the base, the length of the stride, the swing of the arms and the posture of the trunk. Walking heel to toe along a line tests balance more finely and reveals a truncal instability that a normal walk can hide, while walking on the heels tests the muscles that lift the foot and walking on the toes tests the calf. Turning is observed because a patient with a basal ganglia disorder turns in several small steps rather than in one movement, and a patient whose legs move well when lying down but not when standing may have a problem of the frontal lobes rather than of the legs. The examiner should be ready to catch the patient, and no gait test should be attempted that cannot be safely supervised.

A hemiplegic gait drags a stiff leg in a circle, with the arm held flexed and the foot scraping, while a parkinsonian gait is slow and shuffling, with small steps, a stooped posture and a reduced arm swing. An ataxic gait is wide based and unsteady, and it worsens when the eyes are closed if the cause is a loss of position sense rather than a cerebellar lesion. A steppage gait lifts the foot high because the muscles that raise the toes are weak, and a waddling gait rolls from side to side because the muscles around the hips are weak. The Romberg test separates the two great causes of unsteadiness, since a patient who is steady with the eyes open and unsteady with them closed has lost position sense, while a patient unsteady in both conditions more likely has a cerebellar or a vestibular problem.

Localization is the discipline that turns these findings into a place. A lesion of the cortex produces a deficit on the opposite side of the body, while a lesion of the brainstem produces a cranial nerve deficit on one side with weakness or sensory loss on the opposite side, and this crossing pattern is one of the most reliable signs in neurology. A lesion of the spinal cord produces a level below which sensation is altered, with weakness and brisk reflexes below that level, and a lesion of a nerve root produces pain in the territory of the root and a loss of the reflex that the root carries. A lesion of a peripheral nerve produces weakness and sensory loss in a territory that follows the nerve, with reduced reflexes and often wasting. A lesion of the muscle produces weakness that is usually symmetrical and proximal with normal sensation, and a lesion at the junction between nerve and muscle produces fatiguable weakness, often of the eyelids, the face and the muscles of swallowing.

The first of the two emergencies that must never wait is the stroke that still has time. A sudden focal deficit, whether weakness, numbness, a disturbance of speech or a loss of vision in one eye, is a stroke until another explanation is proved, so the examination should be brief, targeted and repeated rather than complete. The findings that raise concern are a new weakness of the face and arm, a new inability to understand or produce speech, a new visual field defect, and a deviation of the eyes towards the side of the lesion. The decision that matters is whether the blood supply can be restored, and it depends on the time since onset, the appearance of the imaging, and the presence of conditions that make restoration dangerous. The finding that would make restoration the right answer is a disabling deficit in a patient who is within the window set by the local protocol with imaging that shows no bleeding, while the finding that would make it wrong is hemorrhage on the scan, a deficit already resolving, or an onset that cannot be established. That window must be looked up in the current guideline and the local protocol rather than trusted from memory.

The second emergency is compression of the spinal cord, which is recognized by findings that are easy to collect and easy to overlook. The patient often has back pain that is worse when lying flat and that wakes them at night, sometimes with a history of cancer elsewhere in the body, and the examination may show a band of altered sensation around the trunk and weakness that is worse in the legs than in the arms. The findings that demand immediate action are a sensory level, a loss of the sensation of a full bladder or of urination itself, numbness around the saddle area, and a reduction or loss of the anal tone. When the compression lies below the end of the cord the same findings arise from the cauda equina, and the whole spine is the unit of interest because a second level may exist elsewhere. The decision here is not about a drug but about the speed of imaging and specialist referral, and the finding that makes immediate action right is a deficit that is still incomplete in a patient who can still be helped, while the finding that makes delay acceptable is stable mechanical pain with no neurological signs.

Infection of the nervous system belongs in the same urgent group and is recognized at the bedside by a small number of findings. Neck stiffness, an inability to touch the chin to the chest, and pain on extending the knee while the hip is flexed are the classic signs of meningeal irritation, though they are often absent in the very young, the very old and the very ill. Fever, a new headache, drowsiness, and a rash that does not fade when pressed form a picture that requires immediate action, and the finding that confirms the diagnosis is the examination of the cerebrospinal fluid obtained by lumbar puncture. That examination is itself a decision with a risk, since a swollen brain can be pushed downwards when fluid is removed, so a focal deficit, a falling level of consciousness or a seizure before the puncture is a reason to obtain imaging first where that is possible. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius may be called a fever in one service and not in another, so the definition that governs the decision must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and in a clinic without a laboratory fever with headache, neck stiffness and a change in the level of consciousness is enough to justify urgent referral.

Raised pressure inside the head is another condition in which the examination must lead the decision. The signs that warn of it are a headache that is worse in the morning or on coughing, vomiting without nausea, a falling level of consciousness, and a new weakness or a new dilation of one pupil. The combination of a rising blood pressure, a slowing pulse and an irregular breathing pattern is a late sign rather than an early one, and waiting for it is a mistake, so a patient in whom high pressure is suspected should not be laid flat and no fluid should be removed from the spine until the risk of herniation has been assessed. The finding that changes everything is the swollen optic disc seen through an ophthalmoscope, or the focal deficit that suggests a mass, since either one moves the patient from a medical to a surgical pathway. In a hospital the response is imaging and specialist help.

In the unresponsive patient the examination is reduced but not abandoned, and it is performed in a fixed order so that nothing is omitted. The airway and the breathing are assessed first, since a patient who cannot protect the airway or who is breathing abnormally needs help before any diagnosis is refined. The pupils and the eye movements are then examined, including the response to turning the head or to cold water placed in the ear where that is available, and the pattern of these responses localizes the lesion within the brainstem. The motor response is tested by pressure on a nail bed or on the sternum, and the difference between a purposeful withdrawal, an abnormal flexion and an extension response is recorded in words as well as in a number. In a clinic without imaging, the examination separates a patient who needs immediate transfer from one whose problem is metabolic, and the finding that most often changes that decision is the glucose measurement.

Where the resources are few, the neurological examination is not a diminished version of the hospital examination but the same examination with a smaller instrument tray. A clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, a thermometer and no imaging can still establish the level of consciousness, the pattern of the pupils, the symmetry of the face, the strength of the limbs, the presence of a sensory level, the reflexes, the coordination and the gait. What changes is the use of time and the threshold for referral, since a clinician who cannot image the head or the spine must decide earlier. The five minutes that a screening round is often said to take is an ambition rather than a rule, and how long an examination should last is a matter for the current guideline and the local protocol. Two habits carry the most weight here, the first being to repeat the examination at a defined interval so that a change is detected, and the second being to write down what was found in words that the next clinician can act on.

Where imaging and specialist help are available, the same examination is performed first, and the machines are used to answer a question that the examination has already framed. A computed tomography, or CT, scan of the head separates bleeding from blockage with speed, and magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answers finer questions about the cord, the brainstem and the tissues around them. Imaging does not replace the examination, and a normal scan in a patient with a convincing deficit is a reason to repeat the examination and reconsider the timing rather than a reason to relax. The specialist who is called should receive a summary that includes the level of consciousness, the focal findings, the time of onset and the question that the imaging is meant to answer. A patient who deteriorates during the wait for a scan is a patient who should have been reassessed, so the examination here is faster and better supported but not different in its logic.

Because the nervous system declares itself over time, a series of examinations is more informative than any single one. A weakness that has progressed between two observations describes a different patient from one whose weakness has not changed, and the direction of travel is often clearer than the absolute finding. The level of consciousness, the pupils, the power in the limbs and the effort of breathing are the observations worth repeating at a fixed interval, and that interval should be short enough that a change is caught before it becomes irreversible. The record should state the time of each observation, what the patient could do and what had changed, since a note that says only that the patient is stable gives the next clinician nothing to compare.

A constructed illustration makes the reasoning concrete, and it describes no real person. Consider a middle aged patient brought to a small clinic after the sudden onset of weakness of the right arm and difficulty finding words, whose glucose is normal, whose face droops on the right, and whose right leg is weaker than the left but still moves. The immediate conclusion is that this is a stroke until proved otherwise, and the clinician records the findings and the time of onset before arranging transfer. In a hospital the same patient is examined briefly, imaged quickly and assessed for whether the blood supply can be restored, and if instead the weakness had been accompanied by a band of numbness around the trunk, retention of urine and loss of the anal tone, the leading concern would be compression of the cord or of the cauda equina. The value of the illustration is the habit it builds, which is to ask first what must not be missed and then to let the examination answer.

To summarize, the neurological examination is an act of localization in which every finding is collected for the single purpose of placing the lesion, and the same reasoning serves the clinic with a torch and the hospital with a scanner. It begins with the level of consciousness and the Glasgow coma scale, proceeds through the cranial nerves, tone, power, sensation, reflexes, coordination and gait, and asks at every step what the finding says about where the trouble lies. Two presentations must never wait, the stroke that still has time for its blood supply to be restored and the compression of the spinal cord that has almost none, and both are recognized by findings that can be elicited at any bedside. Every score, grade, cut off and interval mentioned in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning rather than a universal rule, and each must be re-established from the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions and between laboratories. This chapter is educational material, it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it does not replace training, supervision and the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient; if a patient is deteriorating, the examination pauses and the patient is treated.
