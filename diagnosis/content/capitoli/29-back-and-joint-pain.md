# Chapter 29 — Back and Joint Pain

Back pain is the complaint that fills more clinics than almost any other, and joint pain follows close behind, yet both are discussed badly because they are sorted by how much the patient complains rather than by what is producing the pain. A mild ache in a young person can be the first sign of a serious infection, while a dramatic pain in an older person can be a strain that resolves without any decision at all, and the volume of the complaint separates neither. This chapter therefore sorts back pain by mechanism: the pain that comes from the mechanical behavior of the spine, the pain that comes from infection, the pain that comes from a tumor, the pain that comes from a fracture, and the pain that comes from inflammation of the joints of the spine. It treats the joint in the same way, separating the single hot joint from the many painful joints and from the pain that is felt in a joint but produced somewhere else. Each of these mechanisms is examined in a hospital where urgent imaging is available and again in a clinic where a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer are the whole instrument set. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.

Five dangers must be named where the pain is described, because each is revealed by a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. Compression of the spinal cord by a tumor, an abscess, or a broken vertebra threatens permanent paralysis, and the finding that reveals it is a level below which sensation or power is altered. The cauda equina syndrome, in which the bundle of nerves below the end of the cord is compressed, threatens permanent loss of bladder and bowel control, and the findings are numbness in the saddle area and a disturbance of bladder function. A spinal epidural abscess and vertebral osteomyelitis are infections of the spine that can destroy it or spread to the fluid around the brain, and the finding that suggests them is focal tenderness over one vertebra in a patient with fever or with a reason to have infection in the blood. A leaking aneurysm of the abdominal aorta presents as sudden severe back or abdominal pain and can kill quickly, and the findings are a pulsatile mass, a falling pressure, and a difference between the pulses in the two legs. A fracture of a vertebra may be the first sign that bone has been weakened by a tumor or by thinning, and the finding is tenderness over the spinous process after minor trauma or even without any trauma remembered.

Mechanical back pain is the most common kind and the easiest to accept too quickly. It is pain that behaves like a strain: it begins with a movement, a lift, or a twist, it is worse when the spine is loaded or moved and better when the patient lies still, it does not wake the patient in the night, and it is not accompanied by fever, weight loss, or any change in the legs or the bladder. It is common in people who do physical work, who sit for long periods, who are deconditioned, or who have carried something awkwardly, and most such episodes settle over days or weeks without any specific diagnosis being reached. The trap is that a mechanical explanation is so common that it arrives before the dangerous alternatives have been considered, and a clinician who accepts it on the first sentence of the story stops looking for the findings that would overturn it. The safe habit is to name the mechanical hypothesis and then deliberately search for the features that do not belong to it.

The duration of the pain is the first feature that changes the ranking, and it is worth asking for precisely rather than approximately. Pain present for a few days is usually acute and mechanical, while pain present for weeks or months invites the infectious, malignant, and inflammatory explanations. A commonly taught boundary places chronic pain beyond about 6 weeks, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning rather than a rule of nature, so check it against the guideline in use where you work, because the interval is drawn differently in different services and editions. What matters is not the label but the trajectory: pain that is improving, pain that is static, and pain that is worsening tell three different stories. Longstanding pain that has changed character in recent days deserves particular attention, because a new behavior in an old problem is often the only signal of a new disease.

Pain that travels from the back into the leg is a different mechanism from pain confined to the back, and the difference is worth listening for. Nerve root pain follows the line of a nerve, it usually passes below the knee, it is often sharp or burning, and it is worsened by coughing, sneezing, or straining because that raises the pressure around the nerve. A straight leg raise reproduces the pain when the nerve is stretched, and the same maneuver performed on the other side may reproduce it in the first leg, which is a stronger finding. The distribution of the pain, the weakness the patient reports, and the reflex that is reduced together point to the level of the root that is compressed. The clinician should still ask what is compressing the root, because a disc is the common answer and a tumor or an abscess is the one that must not be missed.

The cauda equina syndrome is the emergency that every clinician who touches a back must be able to recognize, because the damage it causes can become permanent if the decision is delayed. The nerves below the end of the spinal cord carry sensation from the saddle area and the genitals and control the bladder, the bowel, and the muscles of the feet. The patient may describe numbness when wiping, a loss of feeling during sex, difficulty starting to pass urine or a loss of the ability to hold it, and weakness or altered feeling in both legs. The examiner looks for reduced sensation in the saddle region, a reduced anal tone on the gloved finger, and weakness of the muscles of the foot, and the finding that most often settles the question is a bladder that retains urine when the patient believes it has emptied. This is a condition in which the decision to image and to involve a surgeon is made on the clinical findings rather than on the result of a test, and no clinician should wait for a scan before considering it. A patient with back pain and a disturbance of bladder function is a patient who must travel now.

Compression of the spinal cord itself is the second emergency, and it announces itself with a pattern that differs from the cauda equina syndrome. The patient may have a known cancer, or may have lost weight, or may have pain that wakes him from sleep and is worse when lying flat, and the pain may be described as a band around the chest or the abdomen. Weakness that begins in the legs and moves upward, a level on the trunk below which sensation feels different, and difficulty passing urine all point to the cord rather than to a single root. The finding that reveals it is the sensory level together with the pattern of weakness, and the examination must include the power of the legs, the reflexes, and the sensation on both sides of the trunk. Cord compression from a tumor is treated as an emergency because the ability to walk that is lost before treatment is often not regained afterwards.

Infection of the spine is easily missed because it begins quietly and because the patient may have no fever at all. Vertebral osteomyelitis is infection of a vertebral body, and a spinal epidural abscess is a collection of pus between the bone and the covering of the cord; the two frequently occur together, and both are more likely in a patient with diabetes, with infection in the bloodstream, with a history of injecting drugs, with a recent spinal procedure, or with a line or a device in the body. The pain is usually focal, aching, and present at rest, it is tender when one spinous process is pressed, and it may be worse at night. Fever and a raised marker of inflammation support the diagnosis, but their absence does not remove it, and a patient whose pain is severe enough to prevent sleep deserves the question to be asked. The finding that reveals the abscess is a neurological deficit that appears or progresses while the patient is being observed, and that finding converts a diagnostic problem into an emergency.

A leaking aneurysm of the abdominal aorta is the third emergency, and it presents as back pain more often than many clinicians expect. The patient is usually older, often a smoker, and may have known disease of the arteries or high blood pressure, and the pain arrives suddenly and severely, often in the back or the flank rather than the abdomen. The patient may faint or feel the world closing in, and the examination may find a pulsatile mass in the abdomen, a pressure that has fallen, and pulses in the legs that differ from one another or are absent. A systolic pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury in such a patient is treated as a warning of shock, but that figure is an illustration and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the pressure that matters depends on the patient's usual value and on age. The finding that reveals the diagnosis may be nothing more than the combination of sudden back pain, a pulsatile mass, and a patient who looks unwell, and that combination should send the patient to a place where surgery can be done without any delay for other tests.

A fracture of a vertebra is the fourth mechanism, and the question is not whether bone broke but why it broke. In a young person a fracture follows a fall or a crash, and the finding is midline tenderness over the injured bone with pain on any movement. In an older person, and especially in one who has taken steroid medicine for a long time, who has an eating disorder, or whose bone has been weakened by a tumor, a fracture can occur with a trivial movement or with no remembered injury at all, and the patient may only notice a loss of height or a new stoop. The examiner looks for a step in the line of the spinous processes, tenderness in the midline rather than in the muscles, and any neurological deficit below the level. A fracture in a patient whose bone is invaded by tumor is the finding that reveals the cancer, and a fracture in a patient with thin bone is the finding that reveals the disease of bone.

Inflammatory back pain is the fifth mechanism, and its features are almost the mirror image of mechanical pain. It begins in a younger person, often before the age of 45, though that figure is only an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the age boundary is stated differently between services and editions. It comes on gradually over weeks, it is worst in the second half of the night and in the morning, and it improves with movement and exercise rather than with rest. Morning stiffness that lasts longer than about 30 minutes is a commonly taught feature, but treat that interval as an illustration and confirm it against the guideline in use where you work, because the boundary is described differently in different editions. The inflammation may also affect the eye, the skin, the gut, or the tendons where they attach to bone, and the patient may mention a red painful eye, a rash, diarrhea, or heel pain without connecting it to the back. The decision that follows is whether the pattern justifies a trial of treatment for inflammation, and the finding that would make that decision wrong is a clear mechanical or malignant explanation that accounts for the pain.

The features that should stop a clinician from settling on a mechanical explanation can be gathered into a short list, and the list is worth running through deliberately at every visit. They are pain that begins before the age of 20 or after the age of 50, pain that follows a fall or a crash, pain that is worse at night or at rest, a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss, fever, a suppressed immune defense, a recent infection or spinal procedure, and any weakness, numbness, or disturbance of the bladder or bowel. The ages of 20 and 50 are quoted here as illustrations of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the ages at which the ranking changes are given differently in different services. The list is not a diagnosis and its absence is not proof of safety, because a dangerous cause can begin without any of these features. Its value is that it forces the clinician to ask the questions while the patient can still answer them.

The single hot joint is the emergency of joint pain, and it must be treated as infection until the assessment says otherwise. The patient holds the joint still, refuses to bear weight on it, and may have a fever and a rapid pulse; the joint is swollen, warm, and exquisitely tender, and the range of movement is reduced in every direction. A crystal arthritis such as gout can produce a joint that looks identical, and a flare of inflammation in a joint that has been replaced, or in a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, can be even harder to distinguish. The finding that reveals the diagnosis is fluid drawn from the joint, examined for organisms and for crystals, and the decision to draw it should be made before treatment begins wherever that is possible. The finding that would make treatment for infection the wrong answer is a joint that is already draining, or a clear crystal arthritis in a patient who is not systemically unwell, and even then the patient must be reviewed closely.

When many joints are painful, the pattern of which joints are involved carries more information than the number of joints. An inflammatory pattern tends to affect the same joints on both sides, to favor the small joints of the hands and the feet and the wrists, to be worst in the morning, and to be accompanied by swelling that is visible and by a general sense of illness. A degenerative pattern tends to affect the large weight-bearing joints, to be worse after use and better after rest, to involve the joints at the base of the thumb and at the ends of the fingers, and to be unaccompanied by fever or weight loss. The clinician should ask about the skin, the eyes, the mouth, the gut, and the urinary tract, because inflammation in those places belongs to the same diseases as the joint pain and may be the finding that names it. The distinction matters because the decision is different in each case, and the finding that would send the patient urgently to a hospital is a single joint that becomes hot and swollen within many painful joints.

Pain felt in a joint or in the back may be produced somewhere else, and the examiner who does not consider referral will treat the wrong place. Disease of the hip commonly presents as pain in the knee or the thigh, and the finding is a hip whose movement is reduced and painful while the knee itself is normal. Disease of the shoulder may present as pain in the arm, and a problem in the neck may present as pain in the shoulder, which is why the joint above and the joint below are always examined. Pain from the kidney travels from the loin toward the groin, pain from the pancreas is felt as a band across the upper back, and pain from the heart may be felt in the arm or the jaw rather than the chest. The rule that ties these together is that every structure sharing a nerve supply with the painful site can be the source, and the examination must include that structure.

The history is the instrument that decides the ranking before the patient is touched, and the Oldcarts framework keeps the account complete. The clinician asks about the onset, the site, the radiation, the character, what makes it better and worse, the timing, and what the patient believes it means, and then asks what has changed in the legs, the bladder, the bowel, and the general health. The occupational history matters, because heavy lifting, prolonged standing, driving, and vibration all load the spine, and the social history matters, because alcohol, smoking, and the medicines already taken change the list of likely causes. The clinician should ask directly about weight loss, night sweats, fever, a previous cancer, a previous episode of tuberculosis, and any recent infection or procedure, because these are the questions that separate the dangerous mechanisms from the benign one. The account of what the patient has already tried, and whether it helped, is often as informative as the account of the pain itself.

The examination of the back is a sequence of observations, and each has a reason. The clinician watches the patient walk and undress, looks at the posture and the curve of the spine from behind, and looks for a step, a tilt, or a patch of hair or skin that marks a hidden defect in the bone beneath. The spinous processes are pressed one at a time while the patient watches for the exact spot that hurts, because tenderness in the midline points to bone and tenderness beside it points to muscle or joint. Movement is tested in flexion, extension, side bending, and rotation, and the clinician notes the range and whether it reproduces the radiating pain. The sacroiliac joints are examined by pressing and by stressing them, the abdomen is examined because the aorta and the organs in front of the spine can produce the pain, and the pulses in the legs and the skin of the feet are checked. The examination is not complete until the nervous system has been tested and the patient has been asked, directly, about the saddle area and about the bladder.

The neurological examination is what converts the history into a decision, and it is performed in a fixed order so that nothing is skipped. Power is tested in the hip, the knee, the ankle, and the great toe, sensation is tested in the saddle region and down each leg, and the reflexes at the knee and the ankle are compared on the two sides. The finding that reveals compression of the cord is a level on the trunk below which sensation is reduced, and the finding that reveals compression of the cauda equina is reduced sensation in the saddle with a reduced anal tone. The examiner should not rely on a single reflex or a single movement, because the nervous system is examined as a pattern and one weak muscle may mean nothing. The gait should be watched, because a patient may walk badly for a reason that the examination of the legs at rest does not explain. Every patient with back pain and any neurological symptom, however slight, deserves this examination and a decision about it before leaving.

In a hospital the reasoning chooses the image rather than the image choosing the diagnosis. A plain radiograph is quick and shows the shape of the bones, a fracture, or a destructive lesion, but it is often normal in early infection and in early cancer, and a normal radiograph therefore cannot remove the suspicion. A computed tomography, or CT, scan shows bone in fine detail and is useful when a fracture or a tumor of bone is the question, while magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, shows the cord and the nerves and the fluid around them and is the examination that answers the question of compression, of an abscess, or of infection within the disc and the bone. The clinician who has formed a hypothesis already knows which image to ask for, and should say what either result would change before the request is made. The decision to image urgently is driven by the findings on examination rather than by the severity of the pain, and the patient with a neurological deficit is imaged at once and discussed with a surgeon while the imaging is arranged.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is done with fewer inputs, and it is not a lesser form of it. The history is taken with more care rather than less, because it is the instrument that never runs out, and it is repeated at the next visit rather than replaced. The torch makes the skin over the spine visible and allows the throat, the eyes, and the perineum to be inspected; the thermometer turns the impression of fever into a figure that can be recorded and compared; the stethoscope allows the abdomen to be listened to and the pulses to be heard. The examination of power, sensation, reflexes, and the saddle area requires no equipment at all and should be performed in every patient with back pain and any symptom in the legs or the bladder. The decision that follows is rarely a diagnosis but a plan: whether the patient can be watched at home, whether treatment can be started on the clinical picture, and whether the journey to a hospital must begin today.

The decision to refer is itself a diagnostic act, and it rests on the history and the examination rather than on a test that is not available. The patient must travel now when there is any weakness, any numbness in the saddle, any disturbance of the bladder or bowel, a fever with focal spinal tenderness, a known cancer with new back pain, or a sudden severe pain in an older patient that could be an aneurysm. The patient must travel soon when the pain has the features of infection or of inflammation, when weight is being lost, or when the pain has not responded to the measures already tried, although the interval that separates soon from now varies and should be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The patients who can be watched at home are those whose pain behaves mechanically, who have no features that suggest a dangerous mechanism, and who can return without difficulty if something changes. The clinician should say which of these three groups the patient belongs to, and should record the reason, because a decision that cannot be inspected cannot be corrected.

The treatment decision belongs to diagnosis because it is reached from the findings, and this book names the decision rather than the medicine. Treatment for infection is the right answer when the history and the examination point to infection of the spine or of a joint and the patient is unwell, and the finding that makes it wrong is a clear alternative explanation such as a confirmed crystal arthritis or a mechanical pain with no supporting features. Surgery is the right answer when there is compression of the cord or the cauda equina, when an abscess must be drained, or when a vertebra has been destroyed and is unstable, and the finding that makes it wrong is a stable spine with no neurological deficit. Treatment for inflammation is the right answer when the pattern of inflammatory back pain is clear and no infection is present, and the finding that makes it wrong is any evidence of infection, because suppressing inflammation in an infected spine is harmful. Naming the finding rather than the medicine keeps the reasoning portable, and it is the reasoning, not the prescription, that this book is written to teach.

To summarize, back and joint pain are sorted by mechanism rather than by the loudness of the complaint, and the mechanisms to keep apart are the mechanical, the infectious, the malignant, the fractured, and the inflammatory. The dangerous causes are named where the pain is described: compression of the cord, the cauda equina syndrome with its saddle numbness and its bladder disturbance, vertebral osteomyelitis and the spinal epidural abscess, the leaking aneurysm of the abdominal aorta, and the fracture that reveals a weakened or invaded bone. The joint is examined for the single hot joint, which is treated as infection until the fluid is examined, for the polyarticular pattern that separates inflammation from degeneration, and for the pain that is referred from the hip, the kidney, the pancreas, or the heart. Every age, interval, pressure, and reference range in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient with back pain and a new neurological finding, or with a joint that is hot and swollen and a fever, is moved to a place where the decision can be made, and the search for the exact cause continues from there.
