# Chapter 9 — Documentation

The written record is the memory of the reasoning, and this chapter is about keeping that memory honest. The idea that organizes everything below is simple: whatever cannot be rebuilt from the record must be rediscovered, and rediscovery costs time that a seriously ill patient may not have. The most dangerous thing a record can do is therefore to make a lethal possibility invisible, either by leaving it out or by writing a comfortable diagnosis over the top of it. The safeguard is a deliberate entry of what was considered and what was excluded, together with the observation that would reveal the danger if it were present. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.

Human memory for detail is unreliable in a way that is invisible from the inside. A clinician who has just examined a patient can recall the shape of the abdomen and the sound of the chest, and by the next morning those impressions have softened into a general sense of how unwell the person seemed. The record exists to hold what memory will lose, and to hold it for readers who were not present and cannot ask what was meant. A note written only for oneself is already incomplete, because the clinician most likely to need it later is the one who has forgotten the encounter.

The most widely taught structure for a clinical note is the one remembered as Soap notes, and it is best understood as a shape of reasoning rather than a form to fill. Its four parts are the subjective account, the objective findings, the assessment, and the plan. The subjective part carries what the patient says, close to the patient's own words, including the story of the illness and what the patient fears. The objective part carries what was measured and observed, including the negatives that were deliberately sought. The assessment is the reasoning about what these facts mean, and the plan states what will be done next and what will be watched for. The structure forces the writer to separate what was said from what was found and from what was concluded, and that separation is what makes the note readable by another mind.

The subjective section deserves more care than it usually receives, because the decisive detail is often buried in an aside. Write the duration and the character of the complaint in the patient's own terms, and note what has changed recently. Record what the patient has already tried and what the patient believes is happening, because those beliefs change what will be accepted and what will be concealed. A subjective section reduced to a single phrase has thrown away the most powerful diagnostic instrument in medicine.

The objective section should contain what was actually done and actually seen, not what is usually seen. Include the measurements with the hour they were taken, the parts of the body examined, and the specific negative findings that shaped the reasoning. Write down when something was not examined, because that gap is a finding about the record and it shows the next reader where the uncertainty lies. A description copied between patients is worse than no description at all, and evidence that was never collected cannot be cited later.

The assessment is the heart of the note and the part most often written badly. A single diagnostic label placed there closes the reasoning that the rest of the note was meant to keep open, and it invites every later reader to accept a conclusion whose grounds have vanished. The assessment should carry the differential rather than one name: the leading explanation, the alternatives that remain plausible, and the dangerous possibilities that were considered. It should say which findings support the leading explanation and which argue against it, so that the reader sees a balance rather than a verdict. Where the evidence is insufficient, the assessment should say so plainly and state what is being done to resolve the question.

A good assessment ranks the possibilities twice, once by how likely each one is and once by how much harm it would do if missed. The ranking by danger sets the order in which the remaining questions are asked, and it belongs in the note because the next clinician may inherit the patient when the balance has shifted. For each serious possibility, write the finding that would confirm it and the finding that would make it very unlikely, so the reader knows what to look for rather than merely what to worry about. When the picture changes, those recorded predictions are the shortest route back into the reasoning.

The plan states what will be done, by whom, and what will trigger a change of direction. It should name the observations that will be repeated, the tests that were requested, and what each result is expected to change. Write the specific trigger for escalation, such as a fall in consciousness or a rising respiratory rate, so that someone who has never met the patient can recognize it. Any interval written down, such as a review in 4 hours, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because review intervals are set locally and differ between wards and clinics.

A problem list is the running summary kept beside the detailed notes, and its discipline is that it stays current. A problem is not the same thing as a diagnosis: it may be a symptom not yet explained, a risk that must be managed, a social difficulty that changes the plan, or a diagnosis already confirmed. Recording a symptom as though it were a diagnosis forecloses the search too early, while recording a settled diagnosis as an open problem keeps a closed question alive. Each entry should carry its status, active or resolved, and the date it was last reviewed. A list that is never pruned becomes a history of everything ever considered, and a reader can no longer tell what is true now.

In a hospital the problem list may be shared between several people, which makes ownership the central difficulty. Someone must decide that an old problem has resolved, and the note should show who decided and on what evidence. In a clinic with a single sheet of paper the same discipline is harder, because the list must be rewritten at each visit and space is short. The solution is to keep a small number of active problems at the top of the sheet and to record resolved ones in a single line rather than deleting them.

Handover is the moment when the record becomes a conversation, and it is where reasoning is most easily lost. A handover that transfers only tasks produces a clinician who knows what to do but not why it matters, and who cannot see when the task is no longer the right one. The receiving clinician needs to hear what is wrong, what is uncertain, what has changed in the last few hours, and what should happen if the patient deteriorates. Uncertainty must be spoken aloud rather than smoothed away, because a confident summary of an unresolved case hides the very risk the next shift must carry.

Handover is also a matter of order, and the sickest patient should be transferred first while attention is fresh. For each patient the account should run from the working explanation through the outstanding questions to the safety net. Pending results must be named, with who is expecting them and what should happen when they arrive. The patient who must not be discharged and the patient whose ceiling of care is settled belong in the handover explicitly, not as an assumption shared by those who were present earlier.

Four things may never be omitted from a record, whatever the setting and however short the note must be. The first is the drug history, because an unexplained symptom may be caused by something the patient already takes. The second is the allergy history, because one omitted allergy can turn a correct decision into a lethal one. The third is the result that has not yet come back, because a pending result with no owner is a result that will be lost. The fourth is the patient who must not be discharged, because a note that fails to say so leaves the decision to whoever reads it last.

The allergy entry should say what the substance was, what happened when it was given, and how the reaction was recognized. A note that says only that the patient is allergic to something is nearly useless, because the next clinician cannot judge whether the risk is real. An intolerance that caused nausea and a reaction that closed the airway require completely different decisions, and that difference survives only if the original event is described. The record should distinguish what the patient reports from what was observed, since many people use the word allergy for an unpleasant effect that was never dangerous.

A pending result is a task without a body, and it is lost whenever the record does not carry it. Write what was sent, when it was sent, where the answer will appear, and who has accepted responsibility for acting on it. Where the patient goes home before the result returns, the record must also say who will make contact, by what route, and what the patient has been told meanwhile. A result that returns to a system with no reader is the same as one never ordered, except that everyone believes the question was answered.

The patient who is not to be discharged is a category that exists everywhere and is frequently left implicit. It includes the patient medically unfit to leave, the patient whose circumstances make discharge unsafe, and the patient whose resuscitation status and ceiling of care have already been decided. That decision must be written where a covering clinician will look first, because the person reading it at 3 in the morning may never have met the patient. Whether active treatment is appropriate depends on findings rather than on habit, and the record should show the reasoning that made such a plan right or wrong for this person.

Some presentations carry a lethal cause that must be visible in the record from the first entry. In chest pain the dangerous possibilities include a tear in the aorta and a clot in the lung, and the note should show that they were considered and what finding would reveal them. In head injury the danger is bleeding inside the skull, and the record should carry the level of consciousness, the interval since the injury, and the plan for observation rather than one reassuring sentence. In fever with confusion the danger is a bloodstream infection with failing organs, and the record should show the measurements that support or oppose it and the hour they were taken. A note that names a danger beside the finding that would reveal it turns every later reader into a second pair of eyes.

Time is part of every finding, and a note that omits it is ambiguous in a way that can be dangerous. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius written without an hour cannot be interpreted, because the same value means one thing on arrival and another after several hours of observation. Write the hour beside every measurement and beside every change in the patient's condition. That threshold of 38 degrees Celsius is used here as an illustration of the reasoning, and the value that defines a fever in your setting must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since definitions differ between countries, editions, and clinical services.

Whatever the medium, the note is written for a reader who is tired, hurried, and unfamiliar with the patient. Handwriting must be legible to someone who did not write it, and shorthand that belongs to one ward should be avoided because it means nothing to a colleague from another service. Measurements should carry their units in full words, so that a pressure written as a bare number cannot be read against the wrong scale. The note should be ordered as the reader needs the information, with the sickest facts first, rather than in the order the work happened.

In a well-resourced hospital the record is usually electronic, and the system changes both the strengths and the failure modes of documentation. It makes the record available at once to everyone who needs it, it times each entry automatically, and it holds results and images in one place. A record that is accurate cannot be produced by a template, however well designed, because the patient is not a template. The clinician must still decide what is true now, and the system will preserve whatever is entered, whether or not it was earned.

Copying forward deserves its own warning because it produces notes that look careful and are quietly false. An examination copied from yesterday describes a patient who no longer exists, and the next shift may act on it as though it were today's finding. The safe habit is to write the current findings afresh, to time every entry, and to delete whatever was not actually re-checked. Where a problem list or a medication list is carried forward, each item should be confirmed rather than inherited.

Templates can improve completeness, but they can also create a false sense of a full examination. When a form invites a clinician to mark that a system was normal, the mark may record an intention rather than an act, and the note then claims more than was done. The same is true of warnings that appear so often they are dismissed without reading, which is why a record that alerts on everything protects nothing. Use the template as a list of questions to answer, not as a set of answers to accept.

In a clinic with no electricity the record is kept on a single sheet of paper, and the principles do not change with the medium. There may be a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, and there may be no laboratory, no imaging, and no telephone that works reliably. The sheet must carry the patient's identity, the date and hour, the account, the findings, the assessment with its differential, the plan, the safety net, and the name of the writer. Because there is only one copy, the writer must decide in advance which facts the next clinician could not rebuild, and those facts must be on the page before the patient leaves.

Where a duplicate book exists, one copy stays at the clinic and the other travels with the patient, which solves the problem of the sheet that leaves the room. Where there is no duplicate book, the essential summary can be written on a patient-held card, so that the patient carries the plan and the reason for it. The card should carry the working explanation, the decision that was made, what to watch for, and where to return, written so that any clinician can read it. The record that travels with the patient is often the only bridge between a remote clinic and a referral hospital.

A referral letter is a handover written down, and it should answer the question the receiving clinician will ask first. That question is usually why this patient needs a higher level of care, and the answer is the finding or the uncertainty the clinic cannot resolve alone. The letter should carry the relevant history, the positive findings, the important negatives, what has already been decided, and the specific question being asked. A letter that lists symptoms without a question leaves the receiving clinician to reconstruct the reasoning from nothing, and the patient pays for the delay.

The record is also confidential, and the room in which it is kept is not the place for everything the patient has said. Sensitive information, such as a diagnosis of human immunodeficiency virus, an experience of violence at home, a pregnancy, or a history of mental illness, must be recorded when it changes the clinical decision and handled so that only those who need it can read it. The patient should be told what is being written and who is likely to see it, because a patient who fears exposure will withhold the history that matters most. Confidentiality is not an obstacle to good documentation; it is one of the conditions that makes honest documentation possible.

Accuracy means that the note describes what happened rather than what should have happened. If part of the examination was not performed, write that it was not performed, and if a test was not ordered, write that it was considered and why it was not needed. A note that claims a complete system review which never took place corrupts every later judgment based on it, including the judgment of the clinician who wrote it. The same honesty applies to the patient's response and to the clinician's own uncertainty, both of which are legitimate contents of a record.

Errors must be corrected in a way that leaves the original visible, because a record altered in silence loses its authority. The accepted practice is a dated addendum explaining what was wrong, what the correct information is, and why the change was made. Erasing, overwriting, or backdating an entry destroys the record as evidence and turns a mistake into a misrepresentation. A late entry is legitimate when it is clearly marked as late and carries both the hour it was written and the hour of the event it describes.

Uncertainty belongs in the record as plainly as a positive finding, and it should be written in a useful form. Writing that the diagnosis is unclear tells the next reader nothing to act on, whereas naming which possibilities remain and what would distinguish them turns uncertainty into a task. The note should say what is being watched for, how long the observation will continue, and what will happen if the picture does not resolve. A duration such as 3 days may separate an acute illness from a chronic one in one classification and not in another, so that interval is illustrative and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol before it shapes a decision.

Numbers in the record must carry their units written out in full, because a bare figure invites a dangerous misreading. Write 110 beats per minute, 90 millimeters of mercury, 5 millimeters, and 38 degrees Celsius with the scale named, and write a proportion as one in four and a span as 12 hours rather than in a form that can be misread. Every rate, pressure, and measurement taken during a resuscitation carries the hour at which it was taken, since the sequence is the message. Each of those figures and intervals is an illustration of how a measurement is written and interpreted, and the threshold that should alarm you must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because normal ranges and reference values differ between countries, editions, and laboratories.

Scores and rules may be entered when they are used, and they should be entered with the reasoning they formalize rather than as a substitute for it. The Glasgow coma scale records consciousness in a way that allows change to be tracked, and its value depends on the components being tested consistently. An early warning score gathers several observations into one figure that triggers review, and its worth depends on the observations being real rather than estimated. Any cut-off attached to such a score is a local matter, and the threshold that should prompt escalation where you work must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the numbers differ between editions and services.

A constructed illustration may make the difference visible. Imagine a patient who arrives with chest pain and breathlessness, whose note says only that the chest was clear, that the electrocardiogram, or ECG, was normal, and that the patient was observed. Nothing there shows what was considered, what the ECG does not exclude, or what the observation was meant to detect, and the next reader inherits a conclusion without its grounds. The same invented patient written well would show the hour of arrival, the character of the pain, the measurements, and the findings deliberately sought. It would state that a tear in the aorta and a clot in the lung were considered, that the examination and the ECG do not exclude them, and it would name the finding that would change the plan. These sentences describe a teaching construction, not the record of any real person.

The record also serves the clinician as an instrument of learning, because it preserves the prediction made before the outcome was known. A note that states what was expected makes it possible to compare expectation with what happened, and that comparison is how confidence becomes calibrated. This is why the assessment and plan should be written before the result arrives, not rewritten afterwards to match it. Where records are reviewed for teaching, the aim should be the improvement of care and the reduction of error rather than the assignment of blame.

The patient is a reader of the record too, and increasingly a participant in it. Where the patient can see the note or a summary, the entry should be written in language the patient can understand without losing precision. Explaining what was written, inviting correction, and confirming what the patient has understood are part of the encounter, not an extra task after it. A record written for the patient as well as for the next clinician tends to be clearer for both.

In the hospital with every resource, the record is long, shared, and easy to search, and its characteristic failure is that it holds everything except the reasoning. In the clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the record is short, single, and easy to lose, and its characteristic failure is that it holds only what there was time to write. The remedy in both places is the same discipline, which is to write the question, the finding that bears on it, and the plan for the uncertainty that remains. Neither setting permits the clinician to omit the lethal possibility, the allergy, the pending result, or the patient who must not be discharged.

Some encounters are recorded under conditions that make an honest note harder to write. When the patient cannot speak, the record must say who provided the history and on what basis, so the reader can weigh the account. When an interpreter was used, the note should identify the language and the fact of interpretation, without recording anything the interpreter was not asked to convey. When the history came from a relative or a carer, the entry should mark clearly what came from the patient and what did not.

A record written once and never updated cannot show deterioration, and deterioration is the event that documentation exists to catch. Each new observation should carry its hour and a note of what has changed since the previous one, because a rising respiratory rate may be the earliest sign that a patient is losing ground. When a patient is getting worse, the note should say so in plain words rather than leave the reader to infer it from a series of figures. The escalation that follows should also be recorded, with the response, so that the next shift knows what has already been tried.

Decisions should be recorded with the information they rested on and the people who took part, because a decision that cannot be rebuilt cannot be reviewed. Where a patient declined a test or a transfer, the record should show what the patient was told, what the patient understood, and what was offered instead. Where the patient's capacity to decide was in question, the reasoning that led to a conclusion belongs in the note, not only the conclusion. A record of a decision is the evidence that it was made with the patient rather than about the patient.

Writing the reasoning down is itself a test of the reasoning, because a hypothesis that cannot be stated clearly is usually one that has not been examined. The act of choosing what to record forces the clinician to decide what matters, and that decision is the same one that determines what to do next. A clinician who writes during the encounter also notices what has not yet been asked, and the gap becomes the next question. Documentation is therefore not the end of the diagnostic process but one of its working parts.

To summarize, documentation is reasoning made visible, kept for a reader who was not present and cannot ask what was meant. The note should separate the patient's account from the findings and both from the assessment, and the assessment should carry the differential, ranked by likelihood and by danger, rather than a single label. Problem lists must stay current, handover must transfer the uncertainty and the safety net rather than only the tasks, and the drug history, the allergy, the pending result, and the patient who must not be discharged may never be omitted. The same discipline holds in a hospital with a full electronic record and in a clinic with one sheet of paper, a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer. Every score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, and duration named here is an illustration of the reasoning, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories.
