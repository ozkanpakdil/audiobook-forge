# Chapter 62 — Immunocompromised Patients

The patient whose immune defenses have failed is the patient in whom the diagnostic method itself breaks down, because nearly every sign the clinician relies upon is produced by the immune response. Redness, warmth, swelling, pus, fever, a raised white cell count, and the shadow on a chest radiograph are all the work of a system that is reacting. When that reaction is blunted, the disease advances while the signs it would have made do not appear, so a chest that sounds clear and a temperature that is normal may accompany a severe infection. Two errors follow, and both are dangerous: accepting a reassuring examination and waiting, and attributing every complaint to the underlying condition while a treatable infection is missed. This chapter holds both errors in view, and it is educational material that does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Certain possibilities must be named before any comfortable explanation is accepted, because each can kill quickly and each announces itself through a finding that can be sought deliberately. The patient whose neutrophil count has fallen may have a neutropenic sepsis, and the finding that betrays it is often nothing more than a fever, with no source anywhere in the body. The patient with a dry cough, breathlessness on exertion, and a falling oxygen level may have pneumocystis pneumonia, and the finding that reveals it is the desaturation that appears when the patient walks, while the chest sounds nearly normal. The patient with a fever that persists despite treatment may have a disseminated fungal infection, and the clue is often a small skin lesion, an eye finding, or a fever that nothing else explains. The patient who met tuberculosis in the past may reactivate it when immunity falls, and the finding is the organism identified in sputum or in another sample, because the radiograph and the symptoms may be unremarkable. The patient with a headache, a change in behavior, or a seizure may have an infection of the central nervous system, and the finding is the abnormal spinal fluid or the lesion on imaging, since the neck may be supple and the classic signs absent.

Immunity is not one thing but a series of layers, and the infection that appears tells the clinician which layer has failed. The first is the barrier of skin and mucous membranes, and when a line, a catheter, or a course of treatment breaks it, organisms that live on the surface enter directly. The second is the neutrophil, the cell that rushes to a site of invasion and consumes bacteria, and when it is reduced, the infections are bacterial and fungal and they arrive quickly. The third is the lymphocyte that coordinates the response and the lymphocyte that produces antibody, and when these are weakened, the infections are the ones that live inside cells, among them viruses, mycobacteria, and certain fungi. The spleen filters the blood and removes organisms that carry a capsule, and a patient without a working spleen is vulnerable to exactly those. The reasons a patient reaches this state therefore predict the risk: infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV; treatment of cancer with medicines that suppress the marrow; transplantation, which requires suppression to prevent rejection or graft-versus-host disease; the biologic medicines; treatment with steroids; a cancer of the blood; malnutrition; poorly controlled diabetes; and advanced age. The task is not to memorize the list but to ask which of these applies to this patient, because the answer predicts what is likely to go wrong.

Fever must be read differently in such a patient, because the response that produces it is itself part of what has failed. The temperature may be the only abnormal finding, and sometimes even the temperature is absent, since the same blunted response that hides redness and pus can hide the rise in temperature as well. A fever of 38 degrees Celsius is commonly taught as the line above which fever is considered present, but in this group the figure is an illustration of the reasoning rather than a threshold to rely on, and it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted value varies with the site of measurement, the age of the patient, and the edition of the reference being followed. The corollary is uncomfortable and important, since a normal temperature does not exclude a serious infection, and a change in behavior, a new confusion, a fall, or a refusal to eat may be the whole of the presentation. The clinician should record the temperature by the same route each time and treat the trend as carrying more weight than any single reading, because the absence of localizing signs is not reassurance here but the reason to look harder.

The history is the most productive instrument in these patients, and it is taken with the specific aim of naming the immune defect. The clinician asks which medicines the patient takes, including those prescribed for another condition, because steroids and the biologic medicines are often not described as treatment for the immune system at all. It is worth asking directly about treatment for cancer, about a transplant and when it took place, about the spleen, and about any illness of the blood, because a patient may not connect an operation years ago with the present fever. Exposure matters as much here as anywhere, so the questions should cover soil, building work, birds and their droppings, farm animals, unpasteurized milk and cheese, untreated water, and anyone with a cough or a rash, while travel and residence determine which infections are even possible. Every line, catheter, drain, and prosthetic device belongs in the same account, together with how long each has been there, and the patient's own account of previous infections is often more specific than any test that follows.

Two dimensions of the defect change the whole probability list, and they are the depth of the suppression and the time since it began. A neutrophil count that has fallen below 500 cells in each microliter is often quoted as the level at which the risk of an overwhelming bacterial infection rises sharply, and that figure is offered as a picture of the reasoning rather than a universal rule, so it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, where the line and the units of reporting may be stated differently. A helper lymphocyte count below 200 cells in each microliter is likewise taught as the point at which certain opportunistic infections become common, and that number is an illustration whose exact value belongs to the current guideline and to the laboratory in use, so it must be checked locally before it is applied. The length of time matters as much as the depth, because a count that has been low only briefly carries a different meaning from one that has been low for a long time, and the period in which the marrow begins to recover is a time when infections can still appear. The clinician who places the patient on this timeline reasons far better than one who counts cells alone.

The examination is performed in a fixed order and must go to the places that are usually left until last. The clinician begins with the general appearance, the level of alertness, the work of breathing, and the state of the circulation, because these decide how much time remains for the rest. The mouth is examined with a torch for thrush, ulcers, and the dry, cracked lining that follows chemotherapy, and the teeth and gums are pressed, since a dental abscess can seed the blood when immunity is low. Every line and catheter is inspected from the skin outward, because the insertion site is a common source and the signs there may be faint, while the perineum and the skin folds are examined directly and gently. A rectal examination is undertaken only when it is clearly needed, because in a patient with a low neutrophil count the examination itself can injure the lining, and the eyes, sinuses, and ears are checked for the changes of a deep infection. The examiner who looks at all of these places without being prompted by a symptom is the one who finds the source.

Neutropenic sepsis is the presentation that must be assumed before it is proved, because it can kill within a short time and because its signs are so few. The patient may have a fever and nothing else, or may look merely tired, and the ordinary signs of infection are missing because the cells that would produce them are absent. The findings that support the diagnosis are a temperature that is raised or has fallen, a pulse and a breathing rate that have climbed, a pressure that is falling, cool or mottled skin, and a reduced passage of urine. A systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the point at which shock is assumed, but that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the pressure that matters depends on the patient's usual value and on age. The decision to treat is made on suspicion, and it is the right decision when the patient is known to be neutropenic or is likely to become so; it would be the wrong decision only if a clear non-infectious cause explained the whole picture, and even then the patient must be watched closely. Blood cultures should be drawn before treatment begins where that can be done without delaying it, because cultures taken afterwards are far less likely to name the organism.

Pneumocystis pneumonia is the infection that best illustrates how misleading a quiet examination can be. It appears in patients whose cellular immunity is deeply suppressed, and it begins with a dry cough, a breathlessness noticed first on exertion, and a tiredness that the patient attributes to the underlying illness. The chest may sound clear and the chest radiograph may look nearly normal early in the disease, which is exactly why the clinician who relies on either alone will be caught out. The finding that reveals it is a fall in oxygen saturation when the patient is asked to walk or to climb a step, because the lungs fail under the demand of exercise before they fail at rest. An oxygen saturation below 92 per cent is often quoted as the level at which the patient needs assessment in a place with oxygen and monitoring, but that figure is a teaching illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the accepted level shifts with the patient, the altitude, and the instrument in use. Treatment is the right answer when the picture fits and the patient is short of oxygen, and the decision to add or to withhold a steroid as part of that treatment belongs to the clinician and to the local protocol rather than to this book.

Reactivation of tuberculosis is the danger that hides in the patient whose immunity has been suppressed by a medicine rather than by a disease. It is most often seen after the biologic medicines that block a single messenger of the immune response, and after treatment with steroids, and it may appear long after the medicine was begun, which is why the connection is easily missed. The presentation is often not the classic one, because the cough may be dry, the fever low, the weight loss attributed to the underlying illness, and the radiograph may show changes in places other than the top of the lung. The finding that reveals it is the organism identified in sputum, in fluid, in a lymph node, or in a sample of tissue, and where that is not possible the clinical picture and the response to treatment may have to carry the decision together, with the current guideline and the local protocol as the authority. A decision to look for latent infection before starting such a medicine is one of the most valuable moves in this field, and the clinician who treats such a fever with an antibacterial medicine alone may watch the patient improve briefly and then relapse.

Disseminated fungal infection is the quietest of the lethal possibilities and the one most often discovered late. It appears in patients whose neutrophil count is very low or whose cellular immunity is deeply impaired, and it may announce itself through nothing more than a fever that will not settle on treatment. The skin may show small, painless lesions that the patient has not noticed, and the eyes may show a change that only an examination of the fundus reveals, and these are the findings that turn a vague fever into a specific hunt. The sinuses, the lungs, and the brain are the places where these infections settle, and the finding that confirms one is the organism grown from blood or tissue, or seen under the microscope in a sample from the affected site, although the blood culture may be negative even in advanced disease. The clinician who waits for a positive culture before acting may wait too long, and the decision to begin treatment on the strength of the clinical picture belongs to the specialist and to the local protocol rather than to this book.

Infection of the central nervous system is the presentation in which the classic signs are least reliable and the consequences of delay are greatest. The patient may have a headache, a fever, a change in behavior, a confusion, or a seizure, and the neck may be perfectly supple, because the inflammation that stiffens it in an ordinary patient may not occur. The causes in this group include a fungus that produces a slowly worsening meningitis, a parasite that forms abscesses in the brain, a virus that damages the white matter, and a cancer of the lymph tissue that grows within the nervous system, and each of these looks different and each demands a different response. The clinician should record the level of consciousness with a formal scale, and the Glasgow coma scale is the one most widely used; it formalizes the response to voice, to pain, and to command, and the figure at which it should cause alarm must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the version in use varies between services and editions. The decisive step is the examination of the spinal fluid, taken after the questions of safety have been answered, together with imaging of the brain where it is available. A change in behavior in an immunosuppressed patient is never merely a mood, and it should be investigated with the same urgency as a fever.

Infection with HIV deserves attention of its own, because it produces a predictable sequence of immune failure and therefore a predictable sequence of infections. The virus destroys the helper lymphocytes over time, and the illnesses that appear change as the count falls, so that the earlier phase is marked by infections of the surface and by tuberculosis, while the later phase opens the door to the opportunistic infections of the lung, the gut, the eye, and the brain. The acute phase may itself cause a fever, a rash, a sore throat, and enlarged lymph nodes, and it is easily mistaken for a common viral illness, which is why the offer of a test after a possible exposure matters so much. The finding that reveals the state of the immune system is the count of helper lymphocytes, and the finding that confirms the infection is the test for the virus itself, and neither should be guessed from the appearance of the patient. The decision to test must be made with consent and with confidentiality, and the conversation around it deserves the same care as the examination. Treatment of the infection is highly effective and belongs to the service that manages it, and this book names the decision rather than the medicine, because a patient with HIV and a fever should never be treated as though the fever were explained by the virus alone.

The transplant recipient presents a different pattern at each stage after the operation, and the stage is as important as the symptoms. In the earliest period the risks are those of any operation, including infection at the wound, in the chest, in the urine, and on the lines, and the organisms are the ones that live in the hospital. In the period that follows, the risks shift to the infections that a suppressed cellular immunity permits, among them a virus of the herpes family, the fungus that causes pneumocystis pneumonia, and tuberculosis. In the later period, as suppression is reduced, the patient becomes vulnerable once more to the infections of ordinary community life, and a pneumonia that would be mild in another person can be severe in this one. In the recipient of marrow or of stem cells there is the added problem of graft-versus-host disease, in which the donated cells attack the patient's own tissues, producing a rash, diarrhea, and liver injury that may be mistaken for infection while it also increases the risk of it. The clinician who asks when the transplant took place, what suppression is being given, and whether there is graft-versus-host disease has already narrowed the list of likely causes.

The biologic medicines and the steroids deserve particular attention because their risk is concentrated and specific. A medicine that blocks a single messenger of inflammation, which is the principle of the biologic treatments, removes one part of the defense while leaving the rest intact, and the infections that follow are the ones that messenger was holding back, of which tuberculosis is the best known and fungal infection another. Both may appear long after the medicine was started, when the connection to the treatment has been forgotten by everyone including the patient, and steroids suppress immunity in a way that depends on the patient and on the medicine. The clinician should therefore ask directly about steroids, about medicines bought without a prescription, about traditional remedies, and about treatments given in another country, because the immune effect of these is often unknown and sometimes substantial. The finding that would justify delaying or stopping such a medicine is an active infection, and that decision belongs to the clinician who prescribed it, together with the specialist and the local protocol, while the most useful act is often a question asked before the medicine is given about exposure to tuberculosis and previous infection.

A patient without a spleen, or with a spleen that no longer works, has a specific and lifelong vulnerability that is easy to overlook. The spleen filters the blood and removes organisms that carry a capsule, and when it is absent those organisms can multiply in the bloodstream with astonishing speed, which is why a fever in such a patient is an emergency rather than a complaint. The history should include removal of the spleen after injury, a spleen damaged by sickle cell disease or by another disorder of the blood, and a spleen shrunk by disease, because the patient may not know that the organ matters. Cancer of the blood and of the marrow produces immune failure by more than one route, since the disease may crowd the marrow so that neutrophils are produced in inadequate numbers, and it may disturb the lymphocytes that coordinate the response and produce antibody. The examination looks for the signs of the underlying disease, among them pallor, bleeding into the skin, enlarged lymph nodes, an enlarged liver or spleen, and bone tenderness, and it looks at the same time for the focus of the present infection. A fever in a patient with a cancer of the blood is often the first sign that the disease has advanced, and it should never be attributed to the cancer alone.

In a hospital the same reasoning is carried out with instruments that can name the organism, but the sequence of the reasoning does not change. The blood count tells the clinician the depth of the suppression, and a count that can be returned while the clinician waits changes the speed of the decision, while a count that will arrive later must not be allowed to delay treatment in a patient who looks unwell. Cultures of the blood, the urine, the sputum, and any fluid collection should be taken before treatment where that can be done without postponing it, because a culture taken afterwards is a weaker witness. Imaging of the chest and of the abdomen, the computed tomography, or CT, scan, the ultrasound, and the magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, each answers a specific question, and the clinician who has formed a hypothesis knows which one to ask for. The polymerase chain reaction, which detects the genetic material of an organism, is useful for the viruses and for some of the difficult bacteria, and a sample of tissue may be the only way to identify a fungus or a mycobacterium. Every test should be ordered because its result would change what is done, and a test whose result would change nothing is a delay rather than a help.

The clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer cannot name the organism, but it can still do the reasoning that decides the next move. The history carries almost the entire weight, so it should be taken with more care rather than less: which medicines, which treatment, which operation, which device, which exposure, which travel, and which infection in the past. The temperature, the pulse, and the breathing counted properly turn an impression into a record that can be compared at the next visit, and the trend of those numbers is often the most sensitive sign available. The torch makes the mouth, the throat, the skin, the perineum, and the fundus of the eye visible, and thrush in the mouth of a patient not known to be immunosuppressed is itself a finding that changes the list of diagnoses. Asking the patient to walk across the room while the breathing and the pulse are observed is a test of the whole circulation and of the lungs, and it costs nothing. The decision that follows is rarely a diagnosis but a plan, and a normal examination in a patient whose history is alarming is a reason to look again rather than a reason to relax.

The decision about treatment is part of diagnosis, and in this group it is often made before the cause is known. Treatment is the right answer when the patient is known or likely to be neutropenic, when the signs of organ failure are present, when the patient looks seriously ill, or when a treatable infection is strongly suspected and waiting would cost more than acting. Treatment is the wrong answer when a clear non-infectious cause explains the whole picture, such as a reaction to a medicine or a clot, and even then the patient must be watched because a second problem may be present. The clinician should name the finding that would justify or forbid the decision rather than the medicine, because the reasoning transfers between settings even when the available medicines do not, and because a listener must not be able to take a prescription from this book. Protection of the patient follows the same reasoning, because a patient with a suppressed immune system should not be placed beside a patient with an infectious illness.

A constructed illustration may make these habits concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a patient receiving chemotherapy who arrives with a fever but no other complaint, whose chest sounds clear and whose only abnormal finding is a pulse that has climbed and a pressure that has begun to fall; the clinician treats on suspicion, takes cultures where that can be done without delay, and searches the perineum and every line for the source. Now imagine a patient whose immunity is suppressed by steroids, who has a dry cough and a breathlessness that the examination at rest does not explain, and in whom the finding that settles the question is a fall in oxygen saturation when asked to walk; the clinician treats the pneumonia on the picture and sends the samples afterwards. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment; what both show is the move that matters, since the examination was not trusted to be reassuring.

To summarize, the immunosuppressed patient is the patient in whom the signs of infection may be absent, so the clinician reasons from the immune defect rather than from the examination alone. The reasons for the state, among them infection with HIV, treatment of cancer, transplantation, the biologic medicines, steroids, and disease of the blood, each predict a different pattern of infection and a different speed of danger. The lethal possibilities are named at the front of the assessment, among them a neutropenic sepsis, a pneumocystis pneumonia, a disseminated fungal infection, a reactivation of tuberculosis, and an infection of the central nervous system. The same reasoning is carried out where a laboratory can return a count while the clinician waits and where the clinician must treat on suspicion because the answer will come after the decision. Every figure in this chapter, whether a count, a cut-off, a temperature, a pressure, an oxygen level, or a duration, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A fever in this group is never dismissed, and the patient whose immunity has failed is met with a lower threshold for action rather than a higher one.
