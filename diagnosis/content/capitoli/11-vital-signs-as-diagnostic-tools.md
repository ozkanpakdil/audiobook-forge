# Chapter 11 — Vital Signs as Diagnostic Tools

Vital signs occupy an odd position in clinical practice, because they are recorded so routinely that they are often written down without ever being read. A pulse, a respiratory rate, a temperature, a blood pressure, and an oxygen saturation are the cheapest physiological information available at the bedside, and they are frequently the first evidence to contradict a reassuring story. This chapter treats them not as a ritual performed before the clinician arrives but as a set of instruments for reasoning, each with its own physiology, its own failure modes, and its own way of moving the probability of disease. The idea that organizes everything that follows is that a vital sign is a trend before it is a number, and that its meaning depends on the person in whom it was measured. What is said here is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local rules of the place where you work.

One reading is a point and two readings are a direction, which is why the interval between observations is part of the measurement rather than an administrative detail. Physiology usually moves before it fails, so a value sitting at the edge of normal while drifting steadily in the wrong direction carries more warning than a value that is frankly abnormal and stable. A constructed illustration makes the reasoning visible. A young person is brought to an emergency department after a collision, and the first recording shows a blood pressure of 110 over 70 millimeters of mercury with a pulse of 110 beats per minute. Those figures look acceptable when each is read alone, yet the pulse is fast for a resting adult and the pressure is lower than one would expect in a person who is frightened and in pain, and the repeat recording an hour later shows the pressure falling while the pulse climbs again. The lethal cause to carry in mind at that moment is hemorrhage, into the abdomen or into the chest, and the bedside findings that reveal it are a rising pulse, a narrowing pulse pressure, capillary refill that has become sluggish in a cool periphery, and a patient who is increasingly restless and thirsty. Those figures are an illustration rather than a rule, and the values your own service treats as abnormal must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because reference ranges differ between countries, between editions, and between the populations from which they were derived.

Infection follows the same priority, and the dangerous possibility has to be named before the comfortable one. A fever with an obvious source, in a patient who is alert, drinking, and passing urine, may reasonably be watched, but the same fever accompanied by a climbing respiratory rate, a new confusion, a cold and mottled periphery, and a falling urine output points towards septic shock and demands immediate assessment. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is treated as a fever by some protocols and not by others, and the figure that matters is the one your own service uses, confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since definitions of fever shift with the country, the edition, and the population. The most dangerous trap is the infection that does not raise the temperature at all, because a normal or low reading allows everyone to relax. A rash that does not fade when pressed, a patient who is drowsy or difficult to rouse, and a cold periphery with a normal thermometer together suggest a severe infection of the bloodstream or of the meninges, and that combination is a reason to act rather than to observe.

The pulse is the most familiar of the signs and the one most often reduced to a single number, which wastes most of what it can say. Its rate is the first branch of the reasoning, and a fast pulse at rest has only a few broad explanations: fear or pain, fever, a failing heart, a disturbance of rhythm, a hormonal excess, or a loss of circulating volume. The clinician's task is to decide which of those the whole picture supports rather than to treat the number itself as a diagnosis. A young person with a fast pulse, warm hands, clear mentation, and no pain is very different from an older person with the same rate, cool hands, and a new confusion, even though the two recordings are identical. The pulse rate is therefore evidence about compensation, and its significance depends on whether the body is coping or beginning to fail.

Several common situations blunt or exaggerate the pulse, and the clinician who forgets them will misread the trend. A patient taking a drug that slows the heart may mount no tachycardia at all during serious illness, so a normal rate in such a patient is not reassurance. A patient with an artificial pacemaker may have a rate that is set by the device rather than by physiology. Trained athletes and healthy young adults can compensate for a long time before the pulse reveals distress, while infants and small children may compensate and then deteriorate very quickly. In each of these groups the pulse must be interpreted against what is normal for that person, and any figure taken from a textbook must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The respiratory rate is the most sensitive of the vital signs and the one most frequently recorded as an approximation rather than a count. Breathing responds early to a great many problems, including infection, metabolic disturbance, pain, anxiety, and a failing heart, so a rate that is rising may be the first sign that something serious is developing. It is neglected because it takes a full minute of quiet attention, and because the person recording it can often see the chest moving and assumes that watching is the same as counting. The assumption is dangerous, since an estimated rate tends to be pulled towards whatever number the clinician expects. Counting for a full minute, without telling the patient what is being counted, converts an impression into evidence.

Where and how the temperature is taken changes the number, and a comparison between readings taken by different routes is a comparison between different measurements. A reading taken in the mouth, in the ear, under the arm, or in the rectum will not agree exactly, and the clinician must know which route the previous recording used before deciding that a patient has improved or worsened. A normal temperature is often quoted as lying between 36 and 38 degrees Celsius, yet that range is an illustration drawn from particular populations, so confirm the range in use at your institution against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it differs between countries, editions, and methods of measurement. The most important teaching point here is negative: a normal temperature does not exclude serious infection, and it is common in older patients, in people whose immune defenses are suppressed, and in the early hours of a severe illness.

A low temperature is a danger sign rather than a comfort, and it deserves the same attention as a fever. In older adults a serious infection may present with a temperature below 35 degrees Celsius, with confusion or a fall instead of the expected symptoms, so the combination of a low reading and an altered mental state should prompt a search for infection. Cold exposure, hormonal failure, and severe metabolic disturbance can also lower the temperature, and each of those carries its own urgent decisions. That figure is an example rather than a universal definition, and the threshold that should alarm you must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Blood pressure is the vital sign most trusted by clinicians and the one most likely to be falsely reassuring. The circulation defends its pressure with several mechanisms at once, tightening the peripheral vessels, increasing the heart rate, and retaining salt and water, so a patient can lose a considerable amount of volume or develop a severe illness while the measured pressure remains within the expected range. Hypotension is a late finding in a previously healthy adult, which is why a normal blood pressure is never by itself a reason to relax. The pressure has to be read together with the pulse, the perfusion of the skin, the mental state, and the urine output, and it is the combination that identifies the patient who is compensating.

The systolic pressure is the number usually quoted, and the difference between the systolic and diastolic pressures is often more informative. A narrowing pulse pressure, in which the two figures come closer together, suggests that the heart is ejecting less with each beat while the periphery is tightly constricted, and it may appear before the systolic value itself falls. A substantial difference between the two arms, measured properly, suggests a problem in the aorta or in one of the arteries supplying an arm, and it should be sought in any patient with chest pain or an unexplained collapse. A fall in pressure on standing, accompanied by symptoms, is a clue to volume loss, to abnormal reflexes controlling the circulation, or to the effects of medication. Each of these observations is a piece of reasoning rather than a routine step, and each changes what the clinician does next.

Oxygen saturation measures how much of the hemoglobin in arterial blood is carrying oxygen, and it is a measure of binding rather than of delivery. It answers whether the blood is loaded, not whether enough blood is reaching the tissues, and not whether the tissues can use the oxygen that arrives. This distinction explains why a patient with severe anemia, a failing heart, or an inability to use oxygen at the tissue level can have a perfectly normal saturation while remaining desperately ill. Saturation is therefore an important sign and a limited one, and it must be read alongside the pulse, the pressure, the perfusion, and the mental state rather than instead of them.

The device has several well-known ways of lying, and the clinician should know them before trusting a reassuring number. Cold hands, poor blood flow to the finger, movement, and nail covering can all prevent the sensor from finding a stable signal, and a device that cannot find a signal may display a plausible value rather than an error. Skin pigmentation can affect the accuracy of some sensors, and calibration may drift across the range of saturations. The most dangerous failure is that ordinary saturation monitors cannot distinguish hemoglobin carrying oxygen from hemoglobin bound to carbon monoxide, so a patient with carbon monoxide poisoning may display a normal saturation while being seriously poisoned. Whenever the reading does not fit the patient in front of you, trust the patient and seek another way to measure or another explanation.

A saturation of 94 per cent or higher at rest is often taken as adequate, but that figure is an illustration, because the target differs in pregnancy, in chronic lung disease, in children, and at altitude, so confirm the value that applies to your patient against the current guideline and the local protocol. A saturation that is normal now may also fall when the patient moves, coughs, or lies flat, so the measurement should be repeated in the position and at the activity that provokes the symptoms. Falling saturation during exertion or on lying flat is itself a diagnostic finding, because it points to the heart or to the lungs as the source of the problem. A single normal reading, taken at rest, in a comfortable position, at one moment of the day, settles very little.

The shock index exists because the pulse and the pressure compensate together, and each one can look acceptable while the relationship between them has already changed. It expresses that relationship as a single number, and it rises when the pulse increases faster than the systolic pressure holds up, which is exactly the pattern of concealed bleeding or early shock. Its value is that it draws attention to the pair rather than to either figure alone, and it can be worked out at the bedside in a clinic as easily as in a hospital. Like every index, it is a prompt rather than an answer, and it must be interpreted in the light of the patient's usual values and the rest of the examination. No index replaces the eyes, the hands, and the question of what dangerous thing might be happening.

An early warning score is a formalization of a piece of bedside wisdom, which is that several signs each a little abnormal are more alarming than one sign that is very abnormal. It works by giving points for each sign that has moved away from normal, with more points as the movement becomes larger, and then adding the points into a single figure that prompts a response. The reasoning behind it is sound, because physiology fails in a coordinated way, and the pattern of small deviations is often the earliest visible stage of a serious illness. The score also serves as a shared language, allowing one observer to communicate the seriousness of a situation to another in a way that is difficult to ignore.

The score has failure modes, and the clinician must know them or will be captured by the number. A total can hide a single extreme value, so a patient with one danger sign and several normal ones may receive a reassuring total, which is why the individual readings must still be read. Missing observations, particularly a respiratory rate that was estimated rather than counted, corrupt the total and the trend, and a score calculated in a different setting or with different equipment will not mean the same thing. A score derived in one population may also perform poorly in another, so a rule validated in one hospital should be re-examined before it is trusted in a clinic or in a different country. The score prompts a response; it does not make the diagnosis and it does not replace the examination.

The most difficult patient is the one who does not look ill enough to explain the concern, or who looks unwell while every number is within range. A patient who is compensating may maintain a normal pressure, a normal saturation, and a temperature that has not yet risen, while the respiratory rate has crept up and the manner has changed. The clinician who relies on the numbers alone will send that patient home, and the clinician who relies on the impression alone may over-investigate a patient who is merely anxious. The resolution is to use both, and to give the impression the same evidential weight as the measurement, asking what the body is doing that the instruments have not yet detected. An experienced clinician who is uneasy about a patient whose numbers are normal should treat the unease as data and arrange a review.

A constructed illustration shows how the two worlds meet. A middle-aged patient attends a clinic with a cough, a mild fever, and a pulse that is a little fast, and the clinician, pressed for time, records the numbers as satisfactory and advises a symptomatic treatment. The patient returns two days later, more breathless, with a respiratory rate that has doubled and a saturation that falls when walking, and is found to have a serious infection of the lung. The finding that should have changed the first decision was not a single abnormal number but the trend, the work of breathing, and the clinician's own sense that the story did not fit. That example is constructed to make a point and does not describe any real patient, and it is not a criticism of the clinician, because the lesson is about the limits of any single observation.

Where there is no monitor, the clinician still has four measurements that require nothing but attention, and they are the same ones that matter most in the hospital. The respiratory rate can be counted with a watch and quiet observation, the capillary refill can be timed with the eye, the mental state can be assessed by conversation, and the urine output can be followed with a bottle and a record. Those four observations detect most of the deterioration that a monitor would detect, and they detect it earlier than a single set of electronic readings, because they are repeated by a person who is looking at the patient. A stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer extend them further, allowing the clinician to listen to the chest, to look for a rash or a pale conjunctiva, and to measure the temperature directly. That is the whole examination of the unstable patient in a setting with almost nothing, and it is enough to decide who must be referred and who may be watched.

Capillary refill is the simplest test of peripheral perfusion and the most useful when it is performed consistently. The clinician presses the tip of a finger or the skin over the sternum for a few seconds, releases the pressure, and watches how quickly the color returns, with a delay beyond 3 seconds suggesting that the periphery is poorly perfused. That figure is an illustration rather than a fixed rule, and the cut-off that should concern you must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it varies with age, with ambient temperature, with the site tested, and with the lighting. The test is valuable because it changes quickly with the patient's condition and because it requires no equipment at all, but it is also easy to perform badly, and the result depends on the observer. It should be repeated at intervals and interpreted together with the respiratory rate, the mental state, and the urine output rather than alone.

The mental state is a vital sign that many clinicians forget to measure, and it is often the first to change. A patient who was alert and orientated an hour ago and who is now drowsy, agitated, or speaking nonsense has deteriorated, whatever the monitor shows, and that change requires an explanation. The causes range from low oxygen and low blood pressure through infection, metabolic disturbance, and drugs to bleeding around the brain, and the search should begin immediately rather than after the next set of observations. In older adults a new confusion may be the only sign of a serious illness, and it should never be dismissed as age or as the hospital environment. A brief, repeated assessment of attention and orientation costs seconds and can be recorded as a trend in the same way as a pulse.

Urine output is the vital sign that reports on the recent past, because it summarizes whether the kidneys have been adequately perfused over the hours that have just passed. A fall in output below about half a milliliter for each kilogram of body weight in each hour suggests that the circulation is failing to reach the kidneys, and it may precede any change in the blood pressure. That figure is an illustration and the definition of adequate output differs with age, with pregnancy, with kidney disease, and with the fluid the patient has been given, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol. Recorded hourly in a hospital and observed roughly in a clinic, output is one of the most reliable signs of a circulation in trouble, provided that the collection is complete and the times are accurate. It is also easily lost, because a patient who is not asked and not measured has no output to report.

In a well-resourced hospital the same assessment is performed with continuous monitoring, and the additional information is real. A continuous trace shows the rhythm rather than a single beat, an arterial line shows the pressure beat by beat and allows a sample to be taken, and a monitor that displays the respiratory rate, the saturation, and the pressure together makes the relationship between them visible. The monitor also stores the trend, so the direction of travel can be reviewed without relying on memory. Those advantages are genuine, and they are the reason such monitoring changes outcomes when it is used well. The danger is that the display becomes a substitute for the patient, so that the clinician manages the numbers and stops looking at the person.

Monitors fail in predictable ways. They produce artifacts when the patient moves, they alarm when nothing is wrong, and they fall silent when a lead has come off, and the clinician who has heard a thousand false alarms will begin to ignore the true ones. A value measured by a machine is not necessarily more accurate than one measured by hand, because the machine is calibrated, positioned, and interpreted by people. An arterial trace can read falsely high or falsely low if the transducer is at the wrong height, and a saturation reading depends on a sensor that may be cold, displaced, or poorly perfused. The monitor is an instrument, and an instrument is only as good as the person who decides what its output means.

The two worlds differ in tools and not in reasoning, and the same questions organize both. In the hospital the clinician asks whether the trend over the last hour is reassuring, and has a continuous trace with which to answer. In the clinic the clinician asks the same question and answers it with a note of the time, a counted respiratory rate, and a comparison with the previous visit. Neither setting allows the clinician to conclude that a patient is safe on the basis of a single normal set of readings, and neither permits the dangerous possibility to be set aside because the numbers look ordinary. The clinician who understands the physiology can transfer the reasoning from the monitor to the watch and the hand without losing the thread. That portability is the whole purpose of learning the principle behind the measurement.

Children and young adults deserve particular caution because their compensation is so effective and so brief. A child can maintain a normal blood pressure and a normal appearance until the circulation is close to failure, and then deteriorate very quickly, so the trends in the pulse, the respiratory rate, the perfusion, and the behavior matter more than any single figure. The normal ranges themselves differ with age, so a rate that is ordinary in an infant may be alarming in an adolescent, and any table of values must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The child who is quiet, floppy, or difficult to rouse is more concerning than the child who is screaming, because the quiet child may have stopped compensating. Watching the child interact with the parent, drink, and play is a form of monitoring that no device can replace.

The interval between observations is a clinical decision, and it depends on how uncertain and how unstable the patient is. A patient who is clearly unwell and whose diagnosis is unclear may need review every 15 minutes until the direction is established, while a patient who is improving may need only a daily check, and the figure chosen must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because practice varies between services. The interval should be shortened when a finding is unexpected, when a treatment has just been started, and when the clinician cannot explain what is happening. Whatever the interval, the recording should include the time, so that the next observer can see not only the value but how long it has been there. Repeating the measurement is one of the most powerful diagnostic acts available, because it turns a snapshot into a trajectory.

The final safeguard is the clinician's own discomfort, which is a form of evidence that no instrument records. A patient who looks unwell while the numbers are ordinary should be examined again, and the question should be what the numbers have missed rather than how to make the impression fit them. A patient whose numbers are abnormal but who looks comfortable should still be taken seriously, because the measurements describe physiology and not the person's resilience. The correct response to a discrepancy is to observe more closely, to repeat the measurement sooner, and to seek another opinion rather than to choose the more convenient interpretation. That habit, more than any device, is what prevents the deterioration that is discovered too late.

To summarize, vital signs are instruments for reasoning rather than items on a form, and they are most informative when they are read as trends in a particular person. The direction of travel, the relationship between the signs, and the impression of the patient together carry more weight than any single reading, and the dangerous possibilities must be named early so that the finding which would reveal them is deliberately sought. Every value, score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, and duration mentioned in this chapter is an illustration of that reasoning, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. A normal set of numbers does not make a patient well, a monitor does not think, and a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer still answer most of the questions when there is nothing else. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local protocol, and if a patient is deteriorating then the measurement stops and the patient is treated.
