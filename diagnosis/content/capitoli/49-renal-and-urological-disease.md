# Chapter 49 — Renal and Urological Disease

The kidney is the organ that keeps the internal environment stable while it removes what the body cannot use, and when it fails, the failure is read not from the organ itself but from what the blood and the urine reveal about it. Renal and urological disease is therefore organized around one question that decides almost everything that follows: where in the stream the problem has occurred. The stream begins before the kidney, in the circulation that must deliver enough blood at enough pressure; it continues inside the kidney, in the filters and the tubules; and it ends after the kidney, in the tubes that carry urine to the bladder and then out of the body. A problem in any of those three places can produce the same symptoms and the same blood results, yet each demands a different action, and separating them early is the work of this chapter. This is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local protocol of your own service.

Three dangers deserve to be named before the ordinary reasoning begins, because each is exposed by a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. An infected kidney that is also obstructed can destroy the organ and seed the bloodstream within hours, and the finding that betrays it is a fever with a tender, swollen flank on one side, often with a bladder that cannot empty and a collecting system that is dilated behind the blockage. Advanced kidney failure kills through what the kidney can no longer remove, and the findings that announce it are a potassium that has climbed with the changes it makes on an electrocardiogram, or ECG, deep sighing breathing from acid that cannot be excreted, confusion with a flapping tremor, or a rubbing sound heard over the heart when the pericardium is inflamed. The patient whose urine output has quietly stopped is the third danger, because a complete absence of urine is easy to overlook in someone who looks settled and complains of nothing. Each is treated as a question to be answered rather than a diagnosis assumed, and the answer is found by looking, measuring, and testing in a fixed order.

Once the dangers are in mind, the ordinary assessment is built on the division of kidney injury into the prerenal, the renal, and the postrenal. The prerenal problem is a kidney that is not being perfused well enough to work, the renal problem is injury to the kidney's own tissue, and the postrenal problem is an obstruction that prevents the urine from leaving. The division matters because it decides the action rather than the label, since a prerenal kidney usually recovers when the circulation is restored, a renal kidney may need time and specific treatment, and a postrenal kidney may recover as soon as the blockage is relieved. The clinician who asks which of the three is dominant at this moment is already closer to the right decision than one who recites a list of kidney diseases.

The first part of the stream is the circulation, and a kidney that is not perfused cannot make urine whatever its own structure may be. The causes are those that reduce the effective volume of blood, among them bleeding, vomiting, diarrhea, burns, a fever with poor intake, and the movement of fluid into the tissues or the abdominal cavity where it does no good. Heart failure and advanced liver disease reduce the flow forward even when the body is swollen with fluid, which is why a patient can be edematous and still be short of the volume that the kidney needs. Medicines that narrow the vessels or interfere with the kidney's own regulation of its blood flow are a common and often reversible cause, so the history of what was started and when belongs in every renal assessment. The findings that point here are a low jugular venous pressure, a pulse that rises on standing, dry mucous membranes, a falling weight, and a small volume of dark, concentrated urine, and the decisive test is the response to restoring the circulation.

When the problem lies inside the kidney, the picture changes, because restoring the circulation no longer repairs it. The filters may be inflamed by the body's own defenses in the glomerular diseases, injured by a prolonged lack of blood or by a poison, or damaged by an allergic reaction to a medicine that produces inflammation between the tubules. The findings that point to the glomerulus are blood and protein in the urine together, swelling around the eyes in the morning, a rising blood pressure, and sometimes a rash, joint pain, or a recent sore throat or skin infection that preceded the illness. A kidney injured by a medicine or by a period of low blood flow usually produces a milder sediment in the urine, and the history of the preceding days and weeks is what separates the two. A sample of kidney tissue may be needed to name the process, and the decision to ask for it belongs to the clinician who can act on the answer.

The end of the stream is the part that can be relieved, which makes it the most important to exclude in any patient who is unwell. Urine leaves the kidney through a narrow tube that can be blocked by a stone, a blood clot, a tumor, a narrowed segment, or a mass pressing from outside, and below the bladder the prostate, a stricture, a loaded bowel, or a bladder muscle that has failed can obstruct the flow. The finding that reveals obstruction above the bladder is a flank that is tender on one side, sometimes with an enlarged kidney felt by the examining hand, while obstruction at the bladder is revealed by a rounded, dull swelling above the pubic bone that the patient cannot empty. An obstructed kidney that becomes infected is the emergency that must never be missed, because treatment alone cannot clear an infection that sits behind a blockage, and the finding that secures it is a dilated collecting system in a patient who is clearly septic. Relief of the obstruction is a diagnostic act as well as a treatment, since the urine that follows confirms the cause and the change in the blood results confirms the effect.

The examination of the renal patient is first an examination of volume, and it begins with the general appearance. The clinician looks at the mucous membranes, the skin turgor, the eyes, and the weight compared with the last recorded weight, because a change in weight is the most reliable available measure of fluid gained or lost. The jugular venous pressure is estimated with the patient reclined, the pulse is felt for rate and volume and felt again after standing, and the blood pressure is measured lying and standing, since a fall on standing is a sign of a circulation that is short of volume. The skin is examined for pallor, bruising, scratching marks, a rash, and the small bleeding spots of a vasculitis, and the legs and the lower back are pressed for edema. Every one of these findings is combined with the others rather than trusted alone, because dryness, swelling, and a low pressure each mean different things in different patients.

The abdomen is then examined with the kidney in mind, since the kidney lies behind the peritoneum where its signs are easy to miss. The clinician inspects the flanks for swelling, asymmetry, and the scar of an earlier operation, and percusses the renal angle at the back with the flat of the hand before pressing more firmly, because tenderness there is a useful localizing sign. The kidneys are sought by bimanual palpation, with one hand lifting from behind and the other pressing forward, and a palpable kidney is described by its size, its surface, and whether it moves with breathing. The bladder is examined above the pubic bone for a rounded, dull swelling that the patient cannot empty, and the groin and the genitalia are inspected in any man with urinary symptoms. A bruit heard over the flank or the upper abdomen suggests that the blood supply to the kidney is narrowed, and it is worth seeking in a patient with difficult blood pressure or deteriorating function.

Urine is the kidney's report, and its volume, color, and contents carry more information than any single blood value. The volume is measured rather than estimated, over a fixed interval and against the fluid the patient has taken, and in a hospital this is done every hour or every few hours while in a clinic a jug and a watch may be all that is needed. An output of 400 milliliters in a day is often quoted as the point at which urine is said to be reduced, and a passage of almost nothing over 2 days is called anuria, but both figures are conventions that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the boundaries are written differently for children and adults and for different services. The passage of a small volume of concentrated urine suggests that the kidney is working while the circulation is short, whereas a normal volume of dilute urine in a patient who is clearly unwell suggests that the kidney has lost its ability to concentrate. A complete absence of urine is a different problem again, and the first question is whether the bladder is full or empty, because retention and failure look alike to the patient and entirely different to the examiner.

Chronic kidney disease is recognized from its consequences and its duration rather than from a single reading, because one abnormal value may be a passing injury. A kidney that has been damaged for months loses its ability to make red blood cells, so anemia appears; it loses its ability to activate vitamin D and to clear phosphate, so the bones soften and the calcium falls; it cannot excrete acid, so the blood becomes acidic; and it cannot rid the body of potassium, so the potassium creeps upward. The estimated glomerular filtration rate, calculated from the blood creatinine together with age and sex, is the figure used to describe the stage of the loss, and the boundaries between the stages must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since laboratories and editions report the value differently. A previous measurement of kidney function, an old record of the urine, or a note of an earlier blood result is worth more than any calculation made today, because it establishes the baseline against which the present value must be read. In chronic disease the kidneys are usually small and the patient anemic, while in an acute injury the kidneys are often of normal size and the illness is short with a clear trigger.

Infection of the urinary tract is the commonest renal problem in ordinary practice, and it is a spectrum rather than a single illness. At one end is the uncomplicated infection of the bladder, which produces burning on passing urine, a need to go often and urgently, pain above the pubic bone, and urine that is cloudy or offensive, with no fever and no flank pain. At the other end is infection that has climbed to the kidney and from there into the blood, which produces fever, shivering, pain in one flank, vomiting, and sometimes confusion or a fall in blood pressure. Between those ends lie the infections that are complicated by an underlying problem, among them a stone, a catheter, a pregnancy, diabetes, a kidney that is malformed, a prostate that does not drain, or defenses suppressed by illness or treatment. The place of a patient on that spectrum is decided by the whole picture rather than by a urine test alone, because a positive test can be a harmless finding in someone with no symptoms.

The dangerous forms of urinary infection are named as they arise, together with the finding that reveals each. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius or above with pain in one flank suggests that the kidney is involved, though that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. A patient with a known stone, a recent instrument passed into the urinary tract, or a known obstruction who becomes feverish may have an infected obstructed kidney, and the finding that reveals it is a dilated collecting system behind the blockage. An older patient may show no urinary symptoms at all and present instead with confusion, a fall, or a general decline, so the urine is examined in any unexplained illness in that group. A pregnant woman with a urinary infection and fever is treated as a more serious problem than the same infection outside pregnancy, because the risks to her and to the fetus are greater, and the decision to involve obstetric care is made early rather than late.

The diagnosis of urinary infection rests on the combination of symptoms, signs, and a test of the urine, and it is easily overcalled. A dipstick that reacts to nitrite or to leukocyte products supports the diagnosis, but neither finding is decisive, and a sample from a bag, a catheter, or a container that has stood too long may grow organisms that were never causing disease. Where a laboratory exists, a sample sent before treatment begins names the organism and its resistance, which matters most in a patient who is seriously ill, in a man, and in pregnancy. Where no laboratory exists, the clinician reasons from the symptoms, the temperature, the flank, and the bladder, and treats when the picture is convincing while watching for the findings that would demand referral. A patient who fails to improve, whose fever climbs, or who develops flank pain or vomiting needs reassessment rather than another course by habit.

Stones are common, they hurt intensely, and their importance lies less in the stone itself than in what it obstructs. The pain of a stone in the ureter begins in the flank and travels downward toward the groin, comes in waves, and is so severe that the patient cannot lie still, which is a useful contrast with the patient who has peritonitis and holds himself motionless. Blood in the urine is often present, though its absence means nothing, and the patient may be nauseated, may pass small gravel, and may have a frequency that mimics infection. The finding that turns a stone into an emergency is obstruction with infection, or obstruction of a single working kidney, and the anuria that follows complete blockage is a quiet catastrophe. A stone of 5 millimeters is often expected to pass on its own, but whether a particular stone will pass depends on its size, its position, and the anatomy of the patient, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the guidance differs between services and editions.

Pain in the flank is not always renal, and the clinician who assumes a stone may miss a lethal alternative. A leaking or ruptured aneurysm of the aorta produces pain that bores into the back or the flank, sometimes with a pulsatile mass and a falling blood pressure, and it must be considered in any older patient with sudden severe flank or back pain. A systolic reading below 90 millimeters of mercury is a late and dangerous sign in that situation and must never be awaited, though it is a common teaching illustration that needs checking against the current guideline and the local protocol. Infection of the kidney, inflammation of the pancreas, an inflamed gallbladder, a twisted ovary, an ectopic pregnancy, a spinal problem, and a torn muscle can each present in the same region, and the urine, the temperature, and the abdominal examination separate them. A patient with flank pain who is passing no urine, who has only one kidney, or who is feverish needs a decision rather than reassurance, and the findings that would make a stone the wrong answer are a pulsatile abdominal mass, a positive pregnancy test, or a fever with a tender flank that does not fit the colicky pattern.

Blood in the urine is a finding that must always be explained, and the explanation depends on where in the tract it came from. Blood that is mixed evenly through the urine, brownish or smoky, with protein and no clots, usually comes from the kidney itself, while bright blood with clots, or blood at the start or the end of the stream, points to the bladder or the lower tract. The finding that should most concern the clinician is painless visible blood in an older patient, in a smoker, or in someone with a history of exposure to dyes or rubber, because a tumor of the urinary tract can bleed without hurting and can be present for a long time before anything else appears. A young patient with visible blood after a sore throat or a skin infection may have inflammation of the glomerulus, and the findings that point there are blood and protein together with a rising blood pressure and swelling. A patient with visible blood needs the cause identified rather than the symptom treated, and where resources allow the urine is examined under the microscope, sent for culture, and the upper tract imaged.

The glomerular diseases deserve separate mention because they present through the whole body rather than through the urinary tract alone. The filters leak protein when they are inflamed, so the urine becomes frothy, the face and the ankles swell, and the weight rises; they leak blood, so the urine darkens; and they lose the ability to regulate salt and pressure, so the blood pressure climbs. The patient may report a recent infection, a rash, joint pains, mouth ulcers, hair loss, or a change in the color of the fingers in the cold, and any of these turns the urinary findings into a systemic question. A large loss of protein in the urine is dangerous, because it reduces the protein in the blood and increases the tendency to form clots. The findings that demand urgent help are breathlessness from fluid in the lungs, a blood pressure that is dangerously high, a rapidly rising creatinine, or a patient who is passing almost no urine.

Retention of urine is the urological emergency that is often mistaken for kidney failure, and the distinction is made with the hands. The patient cannot pass urine, has pain and a swelling above the pubic bone, and may leak small amounts constantly, which is overflow rather than control; the bladder is felt as a rounded, dull, tender mass that rises out of the pelvis as it fills, and catheterization releases a large volume and gives immediate relief. The causes include an enlarged prostate, a stricture, a stone, constipation, a nerve injury, and medicines that weaken the bladder, and in a catheterized patient the catheter itself may be blocked. Long-standing retention can damage the kidneys silently, so a patient with a chronically full bladder, a poor stream, and a rising creatinine needs assessment even when he is not in pain. A patient whose bladder control has changed suddenly, especially with back pain, weakness or numbness in the legs, or numbness around the saddle area, may have compression of the spinal cord or the nerves below it, and that patient needs urgent help rather than a wait.

Treatment in renal disease is a decision about which problem is reversible, and the finding that makes treatment right is the reversible one. A patient whose circulation is short needs volume restored, and the finding that supports that decision is a low jugular venous pressure with a concentrated urine that improves as the circulation is filled. A patient with obstruction and infection needs the kidney drained, while a patient with obstruction alone may be watched briefly if the kidney is working and the patient is comfortable. A patient whose kidney function has fallen for a long time may need preparation for replacement treatment rather than a search for a cure, and the findings that indicate this are small kidneys, anemia, bone change, and a history of abnormal results over months. The decision to start, to withhold, or to stop any of these treatments belongs to the clinician who is with the patient and knows the protocol, and the reasoning is set out here only so that the listener can follow it. A rapidly worsening picture, an uncertain cause, or a failure to respond makes the next step help rather than another observation.

In a hospital the renal patient is assessed with the laboratory and the imaging service, but the questions remain the same. The blood is examined for creatinine, potassium, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphate, and the count of red cells, while the urine is examined for protein, blood, cells, casts, and organisms, and each test is worth sending only if its result would change what is done next. An ultrasound at the bedside can show whether the collecting systems are dilated, whether the bladder is full, and whether the kidneys are small, which answers the first three questions of this chapter within minutes. A computed tomography scan with contrast may be needed to find a stone or a mass, though the contrast itself must be weighed against the risk of further injury in a kidney that is already failing. A kidney biopsy names a glomerular disease that no other test can identify, and a culture with its sensitivities directs treatment when the infection is severe or has not responded. The clinician should say what each test is expected to show before it is ordered, so that a contradictory result is recognized as a surprise.

Where the equipment is a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is conducted with fewer answers available. The clinician measures the temperature and counts the pulse and the breathing, looks at the patient's color and hydration, examines the flanks with both hands, and percusses the bladder above the pubic bone. A jug and a watch turn the urine into a figure that can be followed, and the torch makes the meatus, the perineum, and the skin visible. The urine itself is inspected for color, cloudiness, blood, and clots, and a dipstick, if one is available, extends the examination rather than replacing it. The decision that follows is usually not a diagnosis but a disposition, which is whether the patient can be treated here, whether the obstruction can be relieved, and whether the journey must begin now. A patient with fever and a tender flank, a patient who is passing no urine with a full bladder, and a patient who is breathless with swelling and a rising blood pressure are all patients for whom the journey should start before the picture is complete, and the clinic's strength is that it can see the patient again, so that strength should be used deliberately.

A patient sent home with a renal or urinary problem must be told what would bring him back, and the instructions should name the findings rather than the disease. The patient should return if the pain worsens, if a fever appears or climbs, if he cannot pass urine at all, if he passes blood or clots, if he vomits and cannot keep fluid down, if he becomes breathless or swollen, or if he becomes confused or unusually sleepy. An arranged review is worth more than an open invitation, and the clinician should say where to go if the patient cannot wait. The record should show the urine volume with the time, the weight, the blood pressure lying and standing, the medicines recently started or stopped, and the reasoning that led to the plan. A note that records only a diagnosis leaves the next clinician unable to see what was considered, and in kidney disease the trajectory over hours matters most.

Two constructed illustrations may fix the habits of this chapter, and neither describes a real person. Imagine a woman of about 60 who arrives with fever, shivering, and pain in the right flank, whose urine is cloudy and whose blood pressure is falling; the clinician's first thought is not the urine test but the possibility of an infected obstructed kidney, and an ultrasound showing a dilated system changes the plan within the hour. Now imagine a young man in a clinic who has taken a painkiller for a sore back, has passed almost no urine for a day, and whose weight has risen; the questions about the medicine and the fluid intake are asked before any assumption is made. Neither story is real and neither should be applied to a patient, for they exist only to show how one finding can reverse the order of a differential.

To summarize, renal and urological disease is organized by asking where in the stream the problem lies, whether before the kidney in the circulation, inside it in the filters and tubules, or after it in the tubes and the bladder. The dangers are named at the bedside and not deferred, among them the infected obstructed kidney with a tender flank and a dilated collecting system, the advanced kidney failure whose consequences show on an electrocardiogram and in the breathing, the confusion, and the heart sounds, and the patient whose urine output has quietly stopped. The examination is an examination of volume as much as of organs, and the urine volume measured over time against the intake and the weight carries more than any single blood value. The same reasoning serves in a hospital with a laboratory and an imaging service and in a clinic where a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer must carry the decision. Every figure here, whether a temperature, a pressure, a volume, a concentration, or a duration, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient whose urine has stopped, whose flank is tender with fever, or whose kidney function is failing without explanation needs help and re-examination, never reassurance.
