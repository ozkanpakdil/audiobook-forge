# Chapter 30 — Weight Loss, Fatigue and Anorexia

Three complaints arrive together so often that they are best treated as one problem rather than three, and the reasoning that organizes them is a deliberate hunt for disease the patient has not yet named. Unintentional weight loss, fatigue, and loss of appetite, which clinicians call anorexia, overlap in their causes and reinforce one another in their effects, and each is vague enough on its own to be dismissed as stress, age, or overwork. The clinician who waits for the patient to volunteer a diagnosis will wait past the moment when the diagnosis could have been made, because the patient usually arrives with a symptom and no explanation, and often with a private fear that has not been spoken. The organizing principle of this chapter is measurement before interpretation: what has actually changed in the body, over what interval, and in which direction. Two dangers must be named here, at the point where the symptoms are described, because each is revealed by a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. The weight loss that has no explanation is a cancer until the assessment says otherwise, and the findings that betray it include a new mass, a lymph node that is hard and fixed, blood in the stool or the urine, a mole that has changed, and bleeding after the menopause. Fatigue is not always a mood, and it may be the voice of a failing heart or a falling blood count, announced by a raised jugular venous pressure, by crackles at the lung bases, or by pallor of the conjunctivae and the palms.

The three symptoms travel together because the same illnesses produce all of them, and because each one can make the others worse. A tumor, a chronic infection, or an inflammatory disease releases substances that suppress appetite and consume energy, so that the patient eats less while the body demands more, and the weight falls from both sides at once. Lost weight then removes the reserve that would have carried the patient through the illness, and muscle that is broken down for energy leaves the patient weaker and more tired, which reduces activity and appetite still further. The loss of appetite has its own consequences, because a patient who eats little takes in too little protein, iron, and vitamins, and a shortage of these produces fatigue and pallor that look like a separate disease. Fatigue also has many causes that have nothing to do with weight, among them anemia, a failing heart, a thyroid that is underactive, depression, poor sleep, and the sedating effect of medicines, which is why the three complaints must be examined separately even when they are considered together. The clinician should resist the temptation to give the whole picture one name too early, because the patient may carry two conditions, and a treatable one may hide behind a dramatic one.

The single most useful act in the assessment of weight loss is to measure it rather than accept it as a report. A patient's recollection of how much has been lost is shaped by the fit of clothing, by the comment of a relative, and by the meaning the loss carries, and it is frequently wrong in both directions. The clinician therefore weighs the patient on a reliable scale, in similar clothing, at the same time of day, and records the value beside the previous one, so that a trend can be seen rather than a moment. Where no previous record exists, the patient's own clothes, a belt that had to be tightened, a ring that has become loose, and photographs from a few years earlier can be used as witnesses, and the clinician should ask who else has noticed the change and when. A loss of more than 5 kilograms over 6 months is often taken as the point at which assessment is warranted, but both the amount and the interval are conventions rather than natural laws, so they must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, where they may be stated differently. The direction of the weight matters as much as the amount, because a patient who is still losing is in a different position from one whose weight has fallen and then settled, and the clinician should record the trajectory at every visit rather than the latest value alone. The scale should also be used in the patient who is not obviously thin, because a person who began with a large body can lose a great deal and still look well nourished.

Weight must be interpreted in relation to the patient's height, which is why the body mass index is useful as a description, though it is a crude one. The body mass index relates weight to height and is used to place a patient in a band, with a value below 18.5 often described as underweight, but that edge is an illustration and shifts with age, population, and edition, so confirm the value your service uses against the current guideline and the local protocol. The measure has obvious limits that the clinician should state aloud: it cannot distinguish fat from muscle, it does not say where fat is stored, and it is distorted by fluid retained as edema, by ascites, by a large tumor, and by pregnancy. A patient with a normal body mass index may have lost a great deal of muscle, and a patient with a low one may be lean rather than ill, which is why serial weights and the appearance of the body carry more information than a single value. The next question is whether the loss was intended, because a patient may have changed diet deliberately or may be taking a medicine that reduces appetite, and the finding of a deliberate program changes the whole interpretation. Alongside the weight, the clinician should ask what the patient eats in an ordinary day, who prepares it, and whether the food is available, because the answer often reveals the cause without a test.

Fatigue is the most easily misunderstood of the three complaints, and the clinician should unpack the word before reasoning from it. Patients use fatigue to describe at least four different experiences: a sense that effort costs more than it should and that rest does not restore the energy, a loss of power in a limb or a group of muscles that can be demonstrated on testing, a discomfort of breathing that appears on exertion, and a tendency to fall asleep during the day. True fatigue is a feeling rather than a measurable loss, and it is defined by its relation to effort and by the recovery that follows; weakness, by contrast, is found when the clinician tests power and finds it reduced, and breathlessness is found when the patient describes it, which clinicians call dyspnea, or when the breathing climbs on walking. The pattern in time is often more informative than the severity, so the clinician asks whether the tiredness is worst in the morning or the evening, whether it comes in waves, how long it lasts after effort, whether sleep refreshes it, and whether it began suddenly or crept in over months. Fatigue that has lasted more than a month deserves a deliberate search rather than reassurance, and the interval that should prompt that search must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the boundary is drawn differently in different services. The clinician should also ask what the patient has stopped doing, because a change in work, in walking, or in housework is a more reliable measure of fatigue than any adjective.

The hunt begins with a history that is broad on purpose, because the causes of this triad range from a curable infection to a hidden cancer, and narrow questions find only what is already suspected. The Vindicate framework is one way of keeping the search open, since it prompts the clinician to consider infection, cancer, disease of the blood vessels, inflammatory disease, degenerative disease, and the effects of medicines and social circumstances in the same patient rather than one after another. The clinician asks directly about fever and night sweats, a cough that has lasted for weeks, blood in the sputum, diarrhea or a change in bowel habit, blood in the stool, urinary symptoms, pain anywhere, and the pattern of menstruation. The history also covers travel and residence, contact with animals, water and food sources, alcohol and other substance use, smoking, whether there is any risk of a sexually transmitted infection, dental health, and difficulty in swallowing, because each of these changes the short list of likely causes. Every medicine should be reviewed by name and by purpose, including supplements and traditional remedies, since some cause loss of appetite, weight change, or fatigue directly. The clinician should ask what the patient believes is happening and what the patient fears, because the answer often names the diagnosis that has been quietly worrying the patient, and it also reveals how the illness is understood at home. A family history of cancer, tuberculosis, thyroid disease, or diabetes belongs in the same conversation, as does the question of who else in the household is unwell.

The examination of a patient with this triad is a systematic search for a cause, and it is performed in a fixed order so that nothing is skipped. The clinician begins with general appearance, hydration, and the distribution of fat and muscle, looking for wasting at the temples, loose rings, a watch that now turns on the wrist, and clothing that is too large. The skin is inspected for pallor, bruising, pigmentation, rashes, and dryness, and the hair and nails for thinning and brittleness, because these reflect long-standing shortage as well as recent illness. The lymph nodes are palpated in every accessible region, including the neck, the armpits, the groin, and above the collarbone, and the thyroid is felt for enlargement or a nodule. The mouth is examined with a torch for ulcers, thrush, a coated tongue, and poor teeth, and the abdomen is examined for an enlarged liver or spleen, a mass, or ascites, with a rectal or pelvic examination when the history suggests a source there. The heart and lungs are examined for the signs that betray failure or infection, and the nervous system is examined for weakness, altered sensation, and a change in the reflexes that might indicate disease of the nerves or the spinal cord. In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same examination is performed as completely as the setting allows, and the clinician should repeat it at the next visit rather than treat one normal examination as proof that nothing is wrong.

The weight loss that has no explanation is treated as a cancer until the assessment says otherwise, and the clinician's task is to look for the site rather than to wait for the patient to point to it. The clues are often small and specific: a cough that has changed or that brings blood, a hoarse voice that persists, difficulty in swallowing, a change in bowel habit, blood in the stool, urinary symptoms in an older man, a lump in the breast or the testicle, bleeding after the menopause, a mole that has altered in color or shape, and pain in a bone that is worse at night. Night sweats that soak the bedding, an itch without a rash, and an enlarged lymph node that is hard, fixed, and painless are findings of particular weight, because they suggest disease of the blood or the lymph nodes. In a hospital the reasoning directs the choice of test rather than following it: a tumor in the chest or the abdomen is sought with computed tomography or with magnetic resonance imaging, an ulcer or a growth in the gut is seen through an endoscope, and the diagnosis is settled by a sample of tissue examined under the microscope. The clinician should decide what each possible result would change before ordering it, because an image that would not alter the plan exposes the patient to cost and risk for nothing. In a clinic with none of these tools, the decision that matters is not the diagnosis but the referral, and it rests on the pattern, the interval, the presence of a mass, and the red flags in the history. The finding that makes cancer treatment the right answer is a confirmed tumor, and the finding that makes it the wrong answer is a benign explanation that fully accounts for the weight loss, the fatigue, and the loss of appetite together.

Tuberculosis is one of the infections that most often hides inside this triad, and it must be considered in every patient with weight loss, night sweats, a prolonged fever, and a cough that does not settle, whatever the first suspicion was. The disease can lie in the lung or outside it, and it is more common and more easily missed in a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, so a normal chest radiograph does not exclude it. The finding that reveals it is the organism identified in the sputum, in fluid, or in a sample of tissue, and where that is not possible the clinical picture, the image, and the response to treatment may have to carry the decision together, with the help of the program that manages the disease. Infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, produces weight loss, a chronic diarrhea, thrush in the mouth, enlarged lymph nodes, and a fatigue that no rest relieves, and it deserves testing in any patient with this picture. Testing is offered with counseling and consent, because the result changes not only the patient's own care but the care of others, and the finding that makes treatment the right answer is a confirmed infection, while the finding that makes it the wrong answer is a different confirmed cause that explains the picture completely. Other chronic infections belong in the same frame, among them an abscess in the abdomen or beside the kidney, infection of the lining of the heart, and infection of a bone, each of which can produce weight loss and fatigue for weeks before it declares itself. These are found by looking deliberately at the places that are easy to forget, including the teeth, the back, the perineum, and every line or wound in the body.

Diabetes is a cause that the clinician can often suspect from the history alone, because the weight loss is accompanied by an appetite that is preserved or even increased, together with thirst, a large volume of urine, blurred vision, and a fatigue that fluctuates with the glucose. A bedside test of the urine or a drop of blood can confirm the suspicion in a clinic with almost no equipment, and a fasting glucose of 7 millimoles per liter is one commonly used line for the diagnosis, though that value must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol because the threshold differs between populations and editions. The danger that follows unrecognized diabetes is severe dehydration and a build-up of acid in the blood, and the finding that reveals it is deep rapid breathing, a fruity odor on the breath, drowsiness, and a passage of large volumes of urine, which together demand urgent help. An overactive thyroid produces weight loss with a good appetite, heat intolerance, sweating, tremor, palpitations, and a fast pulse, and the finding may be a smooth enlarged gland or a single nodule felt in the neck. An underactive thyroid produces the opposite picture of fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, and slowed thought, and in an older patient it may present with weight loss rather than gain, so the laboratory is needed to separate the two. Adrenal insufficiency is a rarer but dangerous cause of fatigue and weight loss, and the findings that suggest it are a fall in blood pressure on standing, a darkening of the skin creases and the gums, and a craving for salt. Each of these conditions changes the plan when it is found, and each is found by a test that is cheap and widely available, which is why the search for them should not wait for the patient to become more ill.

When appetite is preserved and weight still falls, the clinician should think about food that is eaten but not absorbed, and about a body that is burning more than it takes in. Disease of the small bowel, among them celiac disease and tropical sprue, damages the surface that absorbs nutrients and produces diarrhea, bloating, anemia, and a stool that is pale, bulky, and difficult to flush away. Disease of the pancreas reduces the enzymes that digest fat, so the stool becomes greasy and the patient loses weight despite eating well, and disease of the liver or the bile ducts interferes with the absorption of fat and vitamins in a similar way. Chronic infection of the gut and inflammatory bowel disease produce weight loss with diarrhea, blood, and abdominal pain, and they are often accompanied by mouth ulcers, joint pain, and a rash that the patient has learned to ignore. The history should search for difficulty in swallowing, pain on swallowing, a sore mouth, and bad teeth, because these reduce the intake of food directly and are easily overlooked in a patient who is trying to eat. In a hospital the stool can be examined for fat, the blood for antibodies and shortages, and the small bowel for damage through a biopsy, while in a clinic with no laboratory the appearance of the stool and the patient's own account of it are the evidence that directs referral.

Anemia is among the commonest explanations for fatigue, and it deserves a deliberate search because it is usually treatable and because it may point to a source of bleeding that is not benign. The findings are pallor of the conjunctivae, the palms, and the creases of the hand, a fast pulse, breathlessness on effort, and a murmur that is soft and heard across the whole chest rather than in one place. A hemoglobin of 10 grams per deciliter is one value that is often described as mild anemia, but the line that matters depends on age, sex, pregnancy, and altitude, so it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The clinician should then hunt for the site of blood loss rather than stop at the diagnosis of anemia, asking about heavy menstruation, bleeding between periods, blood in the stool, dark stools, and blood in the urine, and testing the stool when that is possible. In an older patient, an anemia caused by a shortage of iron is treated as a possible cancer of the gut until the gut has been examined, because the tumor may bleed slowly for months before the patient notices anything. The heart can also be the cause of the fatigue, and the findings that reveal a failing heart are a raised jugular venous pressure, crackles at the base of the lungs, a third heart sound, swelling of the ankles, and breathlessness on lying flat. In advanced heart failure the patient may also lose appetite and weight, so the cardiac examination belongs in every assessment of this triad, however little else the history suggests.

Depression can produce all three symptoms at once, with a loss of appetite, a weight that falls, a fatigue that is worst in the morning, an inability to take pleasure in anything, and a disturbed sleep that ends too early. It is a real diagnosis and a treatable one, and the clinician should ask about mood directly rather than wait for the patient to raise it, because the question is rarely resented when it is asked with care. But depression is also a diagnosis that can hide another, and the finding that should stop the clinician from resting on it is any red flag in the history or the examination, such as a mass, a blood loss, a fever, or a weight loss that continues after the mood has improved. In a younger patient, especially a young woman, a deliberate restriction of food may explain the weight loss, and the condition is then an eating disorder in which the appetite is denied rather than lost, and the danger there is not the weight itself but the disturbance of body salts that can follow the return to eating, a risk that belongs to the clinician who supervises it. The social history is part of the same assessment, because food may be unavailable, cooking may have become impossible, income may have fallen, or the patient may be living alone after a bereavement, and none of these will be found unless the question is asked plainly. Alcohol and other substances may replace food and damage the liver, the gut, and the nerves, and medicines prescribed for another condition may reduce appetite or cause fatigue, so the list of medicines should be read again with this question in mind. Abuse and neglect, in an older person or in anyone who depends on another for care, are causes that the clinician must be willing to consider, because an unexplained weight loss in a person who is not in control of their own food is a serious finding.

The two settings in which this problem is met call for the same reasoning and offer very different instruments. In a well-resourced hospital the clinician can order a blood count, a glucose, tests of thyroid, liver, kidney, and bowel function, markers of inflammation, a chest radiograph, and cultures, and can reach for computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, endoscopy, and biopsy when the question demands them, but each of these is worth doing only if its result would change what happens next. In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is carried by the history and by repeated examination, and the instruments that remain are more powerful than they look. The scale measures the weight and its direction, the thermometer turns the impression of fever into a number that can be compared with the next one, the torch makes the mouth, the skin, the eyes, and the perineum visible, and a strip of urine test paper can detect glucose, protein, and blood. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is one common line for fever, but the accepted value depends on where it was measured and on the patient, so confirm the threshold your service uses against the current guideline and the local protocol. The decision in either setting is often the same: whether the patient can be watched, whether a treatable cause should be treated before the diagnosis is complete, and whether the journey to a hospital must start now. Follow-up is part of the diagnosis rather than an afterthought, so the clinician should give the patient a card on which the weight is recorded and a date on which to return, and should say plainly which changes should bring the patient back sooner, among them a weight that keeps falling, a new pain, bleeding, a fever that will not settle, or a worsening that the patient can see for himself.

A constructed illustration may make these habits concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a description of any real person. Imagine a middle-aged patient who has lost a large amount of weight over several months without trying, whose appetite is good, and whose only complaint is that clothes no longer fit, and in whom a urine test finds glucose and a blood test confirms diabetes. Now imagine an older patient who reports tiredness rather than weight loss, whose examination finds pale conjunctivae and a soft murmur, and whose stool test is positive for blood, so that the search turns to the gut rather than to the mood. Now imagine a young patient with fatigue, a reduced appetite, and a weight that has fallen, whose examination, weight record, and blood tests are all normal, and in whom the mood is low and the sleep is broken, so that the working diagnosis is depression while the red flags are written down and the patient is asked to return. None of these people is real, and none of these stories should be applied to a real patient without a full assessment. What the three share is the move that matters: the clinician asked what had actually changed, sought the finding that the symptom pointed to, and decided with both the likelihood and the danger in view.

To summarize, weight loss, fatigue, and anorexia are three symptoms that belong to one assessment, because the same diseases produce them and because each can worsen the others. The clinician measures the weight rather than accepting a report of it, distinguishes loss of appetite from appetite preserved, and separates true fatigue from weakness, from breathlessness, and from sleepiness, because each distinction changes the list of causes. The dangerous possibilities are named at the bedside and not deferred: the weight loss that is a cancer until the assessment says otherwise, the fatigue that is a failing heart or a falling blood count, tuberculosis, and infection with the human immunodeficiency virus. The hunt is the same in a hospital with a laboratory and a scanner and in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, and in both it is directed by the history, by repeated examination, and by the decision to refer when the pattern is not explained. Every value in this chapter, whether a weight, an interval, a body mass index, a glucose, a hemoglobin, or a temperature, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such values differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and the patient who is losing weight without explanation deserves a plan for review and a clear statement of the signs that should bring them back sooner.
