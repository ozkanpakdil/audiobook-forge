# Chapter 5 — Diagnosis with Limited Resources

Diagnosis in a setting with few resources begins from a different premise, and the premise is not that the medicine is worse. The clinician who holds a torch, a thermometer and a stethoscope is not practising a degraded version of hospital medicine; that clinician is practising a different discipline, with its own instruments, its own tempo and its own particular strengths. The setting changes what can be learned, so it changes what should be looked for, and it changes the order in which the questions are asked. Where a laboratory can be summoned within the hour, the reasoning may lean on the result; where no laboratory exists, the reasoning must lean on the patient, on the examination repeated over time, and on the clinician's willingness to name uncertainty aloud. This is the first thing to understand about limited resources: they remove tests, but they do not remove evidence. The evidence simply moves back to the bedside, into the history and into the hands and eyes of the person who is present. A clinic with no imaging can still reach a correct diagnosis, and can often reach it more safely than a hurried unit that orders everything and examines nothing.

The reason the setting changes the diagnosis rather than only the treatment is that the available evidence changes, and with it the cost of being wrong. In a referral hospital, a missed diagnosis may be caught by the next scan or the next blood count, while in a district clinic the same missed diagnosis may declare itself only when the patient returns too late, or does not return at all. That asymmetry should make the clinician more cautious, not less, and it should make certain lethal possibilities the first things to exclude. The febrile child who cannot be roused or whose neck is stiff may have bacterial meningitis, and the finding that reveals it is the examination of alertness and of the neck. The woman who bleeds heavily after childbirth may be losing her circulating volume, and the finding is a uterus that stays soft and a pulse above 110 beats per minute, a rate whose exact cut-off must be confirmed in the current guideline and the local protocol because it differs between countries, editions and wards. The older patient with chest pain that radiates to the jaw and arrives with sweat and breathlessness may be having a myocardial infarction, and the finding is the pattern of the pain together with a systolic blood pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury and a tracing on the electrocardiogram, both of which must be read against the values the guidance in force defines for your machine and your ward. Each of these possibilities must be named at the bedside and named early, because in a setting without rescue technology the diagnosis is the rescue, and a number carried in the head is no substitute for the guideline on the shelf.

The history is the highest-yield instrument available anywhere, and in a clinic without a laboratory it is the instrument that carries the diagnosis. A question asked well costs nothing, harms no one, and can separate a self-limiting illness from a lethal one before the patient has taken off a coat. The duration of the fever, the order in which the symptoms arrived, the travel, the contact with a person who coughed for weeks, the pregnancy, the medicine already taken, and the family's own account of how this illness differs from previous ones, all of these are data that no machine supplies. A fever that has lasted more than 7 days, a cut-off that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because it moves with the disease and the place, changes the list of possibilities at once. The clinician who learns to listen in this setting is not settling for a lesser method; that clinician is using the method with the highest diagnostic yield that medicine has ever had. The discipline lies in asking a complete set of questions in the same order each time, so that nothing is skipped because the room is busy, and in noticing which answer does not fit the story. A history is not a formality that precedes the examination; it is the first and often the decisive test.

Examination follows the history and, where no test exists, it must be performed with more care than it would receive in a teaching hospital, not less. Certain signs are worth more than any result a clinic could obtain, because they change the diagnosis on their own. A respiratory rate above 30 breaths per minute in an adult, a value that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because age and setting move it, is one such sign. So is a capillary refill that stays slow, a chest that moves less on one side, a liver edge felt below the costal margin, a pupil that does not react, a calf that is tender and swollen, or a rash that does not fade when pressed. Each of these is a finding that a laboratory cannot produce and that no queue can replace, and each carries its meaning only when the reasoning behind it is understood. The clinician should therefore say out loud what is being looked for and what its presence would mean, because a sign sought with a question in mind is seen, while a sign passed over in a hurry is missed.

When the laboratory is missing, the examination can be repeated, and repetition is a diagnostic instrument in its own right. Time is the reagent that no clinic runs out of, and a finding that changes over 6 hours carries information that a single measurement never could, with the interval itself confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than assumed. A child who was alert at the first visit and is drowsy at the second has declared a direction of travel, and that direction may matter more than any single number the clinic could have measured. The clinician should therefore build return into the plan deliberately, deciding at the first encounter what the next observation should show if the working diagnosis is right and what it should show if it is wrong. A diagnosis that declares itself over a day or two is often the safer route, because it allows the clinician to watch the disease behave rather than to freeze a single instant and guess. Patience here is not passivity; it is a scheduled sequence of examinations with a purpose attached to each one. The question to carry between visits is simple: is the patient moving toward recovery or away from it, and does the speed of that movement fit the illness I have named?

Clinical scoring rules and syndromic management guidelines were built for exactly this kind of setting, and they are allies as long as the clinician knows what they assume. A score takes several findings that a person without a laboratory can obtain, assigns each a weight, and returns a summary that points toward a decision. Syndromic guidance does something similar for a symptom, grouping the possible causes under a named syndrome and directing the clinician along a path that covers the dangerous ones together. Both were derived from a particular population, studied at a particular time, and validated against outcomes that the authors chose, and those details travel with the rule wherever it goes. A sign that was studied where a disease is present in 1 in 10 patients carries a different meaning from the same sign studied where the disease is present in 1 in 100, and both figures must be confirmed against the epidemiology of your own district and the current guideline, because prevalence is the quiet assumption inside every rule. A rule derived where one disease is common will perform differently where a different disease dominates, and a rule that assumes a certain prevalence can mislead precisely where prevalence differs most.

Consider how the Glasgow coma scale behaves in this setting, since it appears in almost every guideline for the unwell patient. The scale formalizes three observations that any trained clinician can make without equipment: the opening of the eyes, the content of speech, and the response of the limbs to a command or a painful stimulus. Its total is often used as a threshold for referral or for a decision about the airway, and that threshold differs between the guideline that trained you and the guideline that governs the hospital you are calling, so confirm the current value with the receiving team before you let it decide the journey. Integrated management of childhood illness works in the same spirit, taking a small set of signs that a nurse or clinical officer can see and touch, and sorting children into groups that receive urgent referral, specific treatment, or advice at home. The strength of such guidance is that it is designed by people who know what a rural clinic contains, and its limit is that its categories were cut to fit the diseases of the places where it was developed. Where a different disease is common, the same signs may sort a child into the wrong group, and the clinician who understands the reasoning can notice the misfit rather than follow the chart into error.

A rule travels badly when the population changes, and the clinics with the fewest resources are often the places where the population is least like the one the rule was derived from. A scoring rule for pneumonia that was built where the disease is common in older adults may sort a child poorly, and a rule for fever that assumed malaria in every case may mislead where the same fever is now caused by something else. The clinician should ask, whenever a score is applied, what the authors studied, what they excluded, and what prevalence they assumed, because those three answers decide whether the rule applies at all. The safest posture is to use the rule to make sure nothing dangerous is overlooked, and then to reason beyond it about the patient in front of you. A pattern is a guide to probability, and the patient is the only source of truth.

When a test is unavailable, the clinician sometimes has to treat on the strength of the working diagnosis, and that decision deserves a clearer name than it usually gets. A diagnostic trial is a test whose result arrives after the decision, and it should be understood honestly as such. The logic is that if the working diagnosis is right, the patient will improve in a way that confirms it, and that if the patient does not improve, the diagnosis is wrong and must be revised. That logic is sound only when the response is unambiguous, when improvement could not have happened anyway, and when the clinician has defined in advance what a response would look like and by when. The clinician should therefore write down the expectation before starting, naming the finding that would count as a response and the interval over which it should appear, and should confirm that interval against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than trusting a remembered figure. Without that discipline, a diagnostic trial becomes a story told after the fact, in which every outcome is made to fit the original guess.

There is a second and more dangerous use of treatment, and it must be distinguished from the diagnostic trial with care. A therapeutic trial given to relieve symptoms can hide the disease it was meant to reveal, and the concealment can be permanent. The pain that settles after an analgesic may be the pain of an appendix that is about to perforate, and the fever that falls after an antipyretic may belong to a bloodstream infection that is still spreading. When a clinician treats first and observes second, the observation is contaminated by the treatment, and a later improvement no longer carries the diagnostic meaning it would otherwise have. This is why the decision to treat for a symptom must be separated from the decision to treat for a diagnosis, and why the reasoning should be recorded when the two are merged for the patient's comfort. The rule to carry is that relief is not evidence, and that a patient whose symptoms are suppressed must be watched more closely, not less.

A working diagnosis is the clinician's current best explanation, written in plain words and revised when the evidence changes. Its value lies less in the label than in the reasoning around it, and that reasoning must be recorded so that the next clinician inherits it. A note that says only the name of a disease tells the next person what was thought; a note that says which findings supported it, which findings argued against it, what was excluded and why, and what would change the opinion, tells the next person how to think. In a clinic where the same patient may be seen by several different people in a week, this record is the closest thing to continuity that exists. The working diagnosis should also be named to the patient, in language the patient can hold, because a patient who understands what is suspected can report the change that matters and can cooperate with the plan. The label should be offered as a provisional explanation, never as a verdict, and the uncertainty should be spoken rather than hidden.

Safety-netting is the central skill of the under-resourced clinic, and it is a clinical act rather than a courtesy at the end of the visit. It means telling the patient, in specific and checkable terms, what to watch for, when to return, and how to return. The instruction should name the signs that would make the illness dangerous rather than listing every possible symptom: a fever that will not settle, a breathing that becomes fast or laboured, a drowsiness that deepens, a bleeding that does not stop, a pain that moves and worsens, a swelling that spreads. It should also name the time frame in which the patient should return if things are unchanged, and the time frame in which the patient should return regardless, and those intervals, whether they are measured in 3 days or in hours, must be checked against the local guidance rather than quoted from memory. The patient should be asked to repeat the plan back, because a plan understood is the only plan that works, and the clinician should check that the patient can actually make the journey. Where the road is long, the instruction has to be made real: a named person who will bring the patient, a means of transport identified, a note carried, and a reason to return that the family believes in. If a clinic sees 1 in 4 of its patients again only when the illness is advanced, the safety net has failed, and that figure should prompt a review of how the instruction is given, with any threshold drawn from it confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Making safety-netting real when the journey is long requires the clinician to think like the family rather than like the hospital. The distance, the cost, the loss of a day's work, the care of other children, the season of the roads, and the permission of a husband or an elder can each decide whether a return visit happens. A clinician who understands these constraints can design an instruction that survives them, perhaps by setting the return around a market day, by involving a community health worker, by giving the patient a written note that carries the reasoning, or by arranging a review at a clinic closer to home. The patient who cannot return must be told what to do where they are, and the limits of that advice must be stated honestly. Safety-netting is therefore a negotiation about the future, conducted at the end of a consultation that already feels finished.

Referral thresholds exist to mark the point where the clinic's resources stop being enough, and applying them well is a diagnostic skill. The decision to send a patient away is made on the balance of danger and treatability: how likely the lethal diagnosis is, how quickly it might declare itself, and whether anything the clinic can do would change the outcome while the patient travels. Certain findings should lower the threshold sharply, among them a patient who cannot be woken, a breathing that is failing at fewer than 8 breaths per minute in an adult, a suspected obstruction or perforation, a pregnancy with bleeding or convulsion, and a child who is not feeding or not drinking. That respiratory figure, like every threshold in this chapter, is an illustration whose exact value must be confirmed in the current guideline and the local protocol, since adults, children and newborns are cut differently. A referral is not a failure of the clinic; it is a diagnosis of the clinic's limits, made in time. The clinician should also send the reasoning with the patient, so that the receiving team begins where the referring team stopped.

When a single test is available, the question is not how many times it can be used but what it could change. A test earns its place only if its result would alter a decision, and a result that alters nothing is a cost with no return. The clinician should therefore ask, before the sample is taken, what action follows a positive result and what action follows a negative one, and if the answer to both is the same, the test can be set aside. If only one test exists, it should be chosen for the hypothesis whose treatment is most dangerous to get wrong, or for the hypothesis whose exclusion would most change the plan. The test should also be performed as well as the clinic allows, because a poorly taken sample produces a confident wrong answer, and a confident wrong answer is more dangerous than no answer at all. One test used with a clear question attached is worth more than several used because they happen to be there.

The specific risks of this setting deserve to be named plainly, because they are not the same risks a hospital faces. One of these is the false reassurance of an absent result, since a test that was not done, or a result that has not returned, can quietly harden into an assumption that the disease is not there. A negative result on an imperfect test is not the absence of disease, and a test that has not been performed is not evidence of any kind. The clinician should record not only what was found but what was not looked for, so that the next reader knows the difference between an excluded diagnosis and an unexamined one. This habit protects the patient twice, once from the missed disease and once from the misplaced confidence of the clinician who inherits the note.

A further risk is the borrowed authority of a piece of equipment, which can make a weak observation feel like a strong one. A machine that prints a value invites the clinician to stop reasoning, and the value may be wrong, poorly calibrated, or right for a different patient. The stethoscope, the torch and the thermometer are tools for the senses, and they must remain subordinate to the reasoning that directs them. Another risk is the pressure of the queue, which rewards speed over completeness and punishes the clinician who spends an extra minute with the patient who most needs it. Under that pressure, the temptation is to reach a diagnosis that lets the line move, and the diagnosis that lets the line move is often the benign one. The clinician must therefore defend a small amount of time for the dangerous possibilities, and must be willing to slow down precisely when the room is fastest.

None of this means that the reasoning taught in a teaching hospital becomes obsolete when the tools are taken away, because the reasoning was never the tools. The teaching hospital teaches a method: take a history, examine the patient, assemble the findings, rank the possibilities by likelihood and danger, and let the tests change the ranking. Remove the tests and the method remains, thinner in evidence but identical in shape. What the teaching hospital sometimes forgets to teach is the confidence to act on that method alone, and what the limited setting teaches is exactly that confidence, earned by watching patients recover or deteriorate in front of you. The clinician who can reason without a laboratory is not behind the clinician who has one; that clinician is simply working with a different balance of uncertainty.

Consider a constructed illustration, assembled rather than drawn from any real patient, to see how the pieces fit together. A man walks into a clinic that has a thermometer, a torch and no laboratory, and says he has had a fever for several days and a cough that will not leave. The history reveals that he has lost weight, that the cough has lasted long enough to matter, and that a neighbour was treated for tuberculosis, so the dangerous possibility is named at the first visit rather than deferred. The examination finds him thin, with a temperature of 38 degrees Celsius, a reading that must be confirmed against the reference range used by the current guideline and the local protocol before any conclusion rests on it. The clinic has no chest radiograph and no sputum laboratory, so the working diagnosis is written as suspected tuberculosis, together with the findings that support it and the plan to refer for confirmation. The patient is told what to watch for, when to return, and how the journey will be arranged, and the note carries the reasoning so the next clinician can continue it. Nothing in this illustration is a real case, and the details serve only to show the method.

Fever is the presentation where syndromic thinking earns its keep, because the same symptom can belong to malaria, typhoid, tuberculosis, a urinary infection, a bloodstream infection, or the first sign of something the clinic will never name. The syndromic path asks the clinician to look for the signs that separate the dangerous from the self-limiting: a stiff neck, a rash that does not fade, a breathless chest, a tender abdomen, a pregnancy, a very young or very old patient, and a patient who is drowsy or confused. In a patient with human immunodeficiency virus infection, the fever may belong to an opportunistic infection that no test in the clinic can identify, and the threshold for referral should fall accordingly. Where the response is not what was expected, the working diagnosis is revised rather than repeated, and the patient is either referred or brought back for another examination. The syndromic approach is not a shortcut around reasoning; it is reasoning compressed into a form that a busy clinic can actually use, and it works only when the clinician continues to think inside the category.

Treating empirically is sometimes the only defensible choice, and it should be made with the same structure as any other diagnostic decision. The clinician names the working diagnosis, states what the treatment is expected to do, sets the interval over which a response should appear, and defines what will be done if the response does not come. The interval and the expected response must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because those are exactly the details that differ between settings and editions. If the patient does not improve, the clinician treats that as a result, and returns to the differential diagnosis rather than to the same prescription. The honesty of the trial lies in naming which of these two outcomes would count as failure before the treatment begins.

The decision that a patient must travel is easier when the clinician has thought in advance about what the local clinic can and cannot finish. The clinician should ask whether the journey itself is safe, whether the patient needs company, and whether the receiving hospital has been told to expect a patient with this problem. A referral without a reason is a transfer of uncertainty rather than of care, and it burdens the receiving team with a question that could have been framed locally. The framing is part of the diagnosis: the referring clinician should state what is suspected, what has been excluded, and what is feared. Where the nearest hospital is a long way away, the threshold for sending should be lower and the preparation more careful.

To summarize, the clinic with few resources does not practice a lesser medicine; it practises a medicine whose evidence comes from the history, the examination and time, and whose safety depends on naming the dangerous possibility early and arranging what happens next. The reasoning of the teaching hospital survives the removal of its tools, because the tools were never the method. Every number, threshold, score, interval and reference range mentioned in this chapter is an illustration of that reasoning, and none of them should be acted on until it has been confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such figures differ between countries, editions, laboratories and wards. Nothing here is medical advice, and nothing here replaces training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is actually with the patient. The clinician who works this way is not guessing; the clinician is deciding with the evidence that exists, recording the decision, and making the patient a partner in the watch that follows. Let the caution travel with the patient, and let the record carry the reasoning, because in a place without tests the reasoning is the most valuable thing the clinic can hand on.
