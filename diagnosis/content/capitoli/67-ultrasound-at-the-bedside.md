# Chapter 67 — Ultrasound at the Bedside

Ultrasound at the bedside is not a small version of the radiology department, and the clinician who treats it that way will use it badly. It is better understood as a physical sign, an extension of the hand that can be laid against the body and made to report whether a space contains fluid or tissue, whether a structure moves, and whether a chamber empties. The reasoning that organizes it is therefore the reasoning of the examination rather than the reasoning of imaging: the clinician decides on one question that matters for the next decision, chooses the view that answers it, and repeats the view when the patient changes. The whole examination may last only a minute or two, and its value lies in that narrowness rather than in completeness. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

To use the probe well, the clinician has to picture what it produces. The machine sends a pulse of sound into the body and listens for what returns, and it draws a picture from the strength and the delay of those returning echoes. Fluid returns very little and appears dark, while the walls of organs, the fibrous sheets that separate tissues, and bone return a great deal and appear bright, and it is the contrast between the two that carries the information. A dark collection in a place where there should be no collection is the finding that usually changes a decision, and a bright line that should move when it is watched is the finding that usually reassures. Because the picture is built from a narrow slice of the body, the clinician must move the probe to build a mental map of the space rather than trusting a single still frame. Nothing in this chapter should be memorized as a set of measurements, because the machine, the probe, the settings, and the patient all change what is displayed.

Several lethal possibilities have a bedside view that reveals them, and naming them before the ordinary reasoning begins keeps attention where it belongs. The patient whose heart is compressed by fluid in the sac around it may be in cardiac tamponade, and the finding that reveals it is a dark rim of fluid together with a chamber that collapses as it fills, often with a large vein in the abdomen that does not change with breathing. The patient with abdominal pain and a missed period may have a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, and the finding that reveals it is dark fluid in the abdomen together with an empty uterus. The older patient with back pain, a pulsating abdominal mass, or a sudden collapse may have a leaking aneurysm of the aorta, and the finding that reveals it is a dilated aorta with dark fluid collecting around it. The patient who has suffered an injury and whose pressure is falling may be bleeding into the abdomen, the chest, or around the heart, and each of those spaces has a view that can be obtained in the resuscitation room.

The probe does not replace the history, the hands, or the stethoscope, and the clinician who reaches for it first usually asks the wrong question. The ordinary examination tells the clinician where to look: a quiet chest on one side suggests fluid or air in that space, a tender right upper abdomen suggests the gallbladder, a pulsating mass suggests the aorta, and a missed period with pain suggests the pelvis. The probe is then used to test that suspicion rather than to survey the body, and a negative view is interpreted in the light of the suspicion that prompted it. This is why a clinician who is unsure what question to ask should not perform the examination at all, because an image made without a question produces an answer that cannot be used. The skill that has to be learned first is not the handling of the probe but the habit of deciding, before the probe is placed, what finding would change the plan.

Competence with the probe is a specific skill, and it is not conferred by owning the machine. A clinician who performs these examinations should have been taught the standard views, should have practiced them under supervision, and should know the limits of what each view can show. Where a service uses bedside ultrasound, it usually keeps a record of who is credentialed for which examination, and the clinician who works outside that record is practicing beyond what can be defended. The learner should keep a log of examinations and compare the interpretation with a formal study when one is available, because that comparison is what corrects a private vocabulary of images. None of this is a reason to withhold the probe in an emergency; it is a reason to use it deliberately and to state honestly what the clinician could and could not see.

The heart is examined at the bedside through windows that avoid the ribs and the lungs, most often below the breastbone and beside it in the third or fourth space on the left. The clinician looks first at the sac around the heart, then at the size of the chambers, then at how well the walls move, and finally at how much blood leaves with each beat. Fluid in the sac appears as a dark band between the heart and the bright line of the pericardium, and its depth and its distribution matter more than its mere presence, because a small collection that has accumulated quickly can compress the heart while a larger one that has accumulated slowly may not. The view of how the heart empties is judged by watching the walls come together, and a heart whose walls barely move is failing, while a heart whose walls move vigorously may be underfilled or may be obstructed. This is a visual judgment that improves with repetition, and it should be described in words that a colleague can follow rather than in a figure that the clinician cannot justify.

The reasoning about tamponade is worth following step by step, because it is the clearest example of a bedside view changing an immediate decision. Fluid collects in the sac until the pressure inside it equals the pressure that fills the heart, and at that point the chambers can no longer expand, so less blood enters and less blood leaves. The patient may be breathless, may have a rapid pulse, may have a falling pressure, and may have distended neck veins, but each of those signs can have another cause. The findings that make the picture convincing are a dark rim of fluid, a chamber that collapses when it should be filling, and a large vein in the abdomen that stays distended instead of narrowing with the breath. When those appear in a patient whose pressure is falling, the decision to remove the fluid belongs to the clinician who can do it safely, and the probe has done its work by moving that decision earlier. The finding that would make the diagnosis less likely is a heart that fills normally with no collapse and no distended vein, and even then the patient must be watched.

A view of the heart that shows weak contraction tells the clinician that the pump is failing, and a view that shows a normal-sized chamber with vigorous contraction makes failure of the pump less likely, but neither view excludes the other causes of the same symptoms. A clot in the lung, a loss of blood, an infection, and a leaky valve can all produce breathlessness with a heart that looks busy on the screen. The view cannot measure the pressure inside the chambers, cannot show the coronary arteries, and cannot reliably show a clot inside the heart. The clinician who uses it must therefore say what was seen and what remains possible, and should not allow a reassuring image to close a question that the history has opened. Where a fuller study is available, the bedside view is a bridge to it rather than a replacement for it.

The chest is examined for fluid around the lung, which appears as a dark region above the bright line of the diaphragm, and for air outside the lung, which changes the way the two layers of the pleura move against each other. A normal examination shows the lung sliding against the chest wall with each breath, a shimmering movement that is easier to recognize than to describe. When air lies between the layers, that movement disappears, and the point at which the sliding reappears marks the edge of the air. The clinician who watches for that movement must be sure the probe is in the right place and the patient is breathing before concluding that it is absent, because a still image from a poor window can mimic the finding. A small collection of fluid or a small rim of air may be invisible at the bedside, so a normal view is not the same as a normal chest. The chest radiograph and the computed tomography, or CT, remain the studies that answer the question fully where they are available.

The patient who becomes suddenly breathless after an injury, a procedure, or a line placed in the chest is the patient in whom the view of the pleura is most valuable, because the decision it informs is immediate. If the sliding is absent on one side and the patient is unstable, the clinician treats for air in the chest before any image confirms it, and the probe has supported a decision that could not wait. The view is less useful in the patient who is stable and whose breathlessness has many possible causes, because it cannot distinguish a small collection of air from a large one by itself. A layer of air may also be missed in the patient who has been ventilated, whose lungs may not move as they should. The clinician should therefore treat a negative view as one piece of information and not as a verdict, and should keep the clinical picture in charge.

The abdomen is examined for free fluid, which collects in the dependent parts and appears as dark streaks between the bright loops of bowel. In the injured patient, the presence of fluid in more than one place changes the decision about surgery, and its absence in a single view does not exclude bleeding, because blood may lie where the probe is not. In the patient with abdominal pain, a small amount of free fluid may accompany inflammation, and a large amount with a tender abdomen is a reason to think of a perforated organ or a collection of pus. The clinician looks for fluid beside the liver, around the spleen, in the flanks, and deep in the pelvis, and repeats the examination if the first view is not conclusive. What the view cannot do is tell the clinician what the fluid contains, because blood, pus, urine, and the fluid of ascites all appear dark. That limit is why the view is always read together with the history, the examination, and, where it exists, the laboratory.

The aorta is examined along its course in the abdomen, and the clinician measures its width at the point where it is widest, looking at it in more than one direction so that an oblique view does not exaggerate the size. An aneurysm is a segment that has stretched, and a leaking aneurysm is one that has begun to release blood into the tissues around it. A width of 3 centimeters is often quoted as the point above which an abdominal aorta is called dilated, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line varies with age, sex, and body size. The finding that matters most is not the figure itself but a dilated segment together with dark fluid in the space around it, which suggests that blood has escaped and makes the patient a surgical emergency. The view also shows the aorta in only part of its length, so a normal appearance in the abdomen says nothing about the portion in the chest.

The gallbladder is examined for stones, for thickening of its wall, and for fluid around it, and the finding that supports inflammation of the gallbladder is a tender organ with a thickened wall and a collection of fluid beside it. Stones appear as bright shapes that cast a dark shadow behind them, and their mere presence is common in people who have no symptoms at all, so a stone alone is not the diagnosis. The view is most useful when the history and the examination already point to the right upper abdomen, and it is easily made difficult by gas in the bowel, by a patient who cannot lie still, or by a gallbladder that is hidden behind the ribs. It cannot show the ducts clearly, and it cannot tell the clinician whether a stone is blocking the flow of bile, which is a question for a formal study or for the laboratory. The clinician should describe what was seen and what could not be seen, and should not let a normal-looking gallbladder overrule a story of recurring pain after fatty meals.

The kidneys are examined for dark fluid in the collecting system, which appears as branching spaces inside the bright tissue of the kidney, and that finding suggests that urine is not draining. Obstruction is the usual cause, and it may come from a stone, a tumor, a clot, an enlarged prostate, or pressure from outside. The view can show that the system is dilated, but it cannot tell whether the obstruction is recent or has been present for years, and it cannot always separate obstruction from a normal variant or from the dilation of pregnancy. A patient with a single working kidney, or with obstruction on both sides, may have little time before the chemistry of the blood becomes dangerous, so the finding of dilation in that patient deserves an urgent conversation. The clinician should also remember that a kidney can be obstructed without dilation early in the process, and that a normal view does not exclude a stone that is causing severe pain.

The pregnant uterus is examined for the position of the pregnancy, the amount of fluid, the presence of a heartbeat, and the movement of the fetus, and each of these answers a different question. The danger that this view is used to address is the ectopic pregnancy, in which the pregnancy lies outside the uterus and may rupture, and the finding that reveals it is an empty uterus together with free fluid in the abdomen in a patient who has a positive pregnancy test and pain. An empty uterus on its own does not prove an ectopic pregnancy, because the pregnancy may simply be earlier than the view can show, and the clinician must combine the view with the result of the pregnancy test, the history, and the trend of the hormone level if it can be measured. The view cannot exclude an ectopic pregnancy with certainty, and a patient with pain and a positive test who has no intrauterine pregnancy must be managed as a possible ectopic until someone with the right skills has settled the question. In a clinic where the probe is the only imaging, this view is often the one that decides whether the patient can be watched or must travel at once.

In the injured patient the bedside examination is performed as a rapid sequence, and the reasoning is that the patient may be bleeding where no external wound can show it. The clinician looks for fluid around the heart, fluid in the chest, fluid in the abdomen, and air outside the lung, and returns to the same views after any change in the patient's condition. The sequence is repeated because a patient who was bleeding slowly at the first look may have a large collection a short time later, and because the decision to operate is often made on a repeated finding rather than a single one. A normal first sequence is reassuring only for the moment in which it was performed, and it never replaces the observation of the patient, the pressure, the pulse, and the urine. Where the patient is stable and the injury is complex, the full study by CT gives more complete information, and the bedside view has served its purpose by identifying who cannot wait for it.

The patient who arrives breathless and undifferentiated is a good example of how views are combined into one question. The clinician examines the heart for fluid and for weak contraction, the chest for fluid and for air, the large vein in the abdomen for its size and its change with breathing, and the legs for a clot in the deep veins. Each view narrows the field, and together they may separate a failing heart, an infection of the lungs, a clot in the lung, and a collection of fluid or air around the lung, which are the causes that most often look alike. The views must be performed in an order that follows the danger, so that the immediately treatable cause is sought first and the patient is not left waiting while a complete survey is assembled. Where the probe cannot answer the question, the clinician says so and arranges the study that can, and a normal set of views in a patient who is working hard to breathe is a reason to keep looking rather than a reason to relax.

A swollen leg is another problem in which the bedside view can change a decision within minutes. The clinician presses the probe along the deep veins of the thigh and the calf and watches whether the walls of the vein come together, because a vein that stays open and does not compress may contain a clot. The finding of a vein that will not compress supports the diagnosis, and its absence in a single view does not exclude it, because a clot may lie below the knee, in the pelvis, or in a segment that the probe did not reach. The view is also used to distinguish swelling caused by fluid in the tissues from swelling caused by a collection of blood or pus, which are managed differently. Where the probability of a clot is low and a reliable blood test is available, that test may settle the question, and where it is high the clinician treats while arranging the confirmatory study. The clinician who records which deep veins were seen and compressed, and which segments were not, has written a note that a colleague can trust.

In a clinic without a laboratory, a patient with abdominal pain can be examined with the same probe, and the questions are chosen from the story rather than from a list. A young patient with a missed period is examined for a pregnancy in the uterus and for fluid in the abdomen, because a ruptured ectopic pregnancy is the danger that must be set aside first. An older patient with back pain and a pulsating sensation is examined for the aorta, because a leaking aneurysm is the danger in that story. A patient with pain after fatty food is examined for the gallbladder, and a patient with pain in the flank and a change in the urine is examined for the kidneys. Each view is followed by the question of what its result would mean, and the answer decides whether the patient is observed at the clinic, treated on the clinical findings, or sent onward now. A probe that cannot answer the question has still been useful, because it tells the clinician that the decision must be made on other grounds.

In a well-resourced hospital the bedside examination sits inside a larger system, and its role is to answer the question that cannot wait for the formal study. It identifies the patient with fluid around the heart, the patient bleeding into the abdomen, and the patient with air outside the lung, and it sends each of them to the right next step without delay. The formal ultrasound performed by a trained sonographer or a radiologist remains the study that measures, characterizes, and documents, and the bedside view is not a substitute for it. Computed tomography, or CT, and magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answer questions that the probe cannot reach, including the detail of the organs, the vessels in the chest, and the brain. The clinician who uses the probe in the hospital must therefore know which questions belong to it and which belong to the department, and must not let the convenience of the bedside view delay a definitive test. Used in that way, the probe shortens the time to the right decision rather than replacing the decision itself.

In a clinic where the probe is the only imaging, the same reasoning is stretched to cover more ground, and the clinician must be honest about how far it can go. Electricity, a charged battery, a supply of gel, and a probe that is kept clean are the practical requirements, and a service that cannot maintain them cannot rely on the examination. The views that repay the effort most are the heart for fluid, the abdomen for free fluid and for the aorta, the kidneys for obstruction, the gallbladder for inflammation, and the uterus in a patient who may be pregnant. The clinician who finds a dangerous picture in such a clinic has gained the most valuable thing available, which is time, because the decision to travel can be made before the patient collapses. The clinician who finds nothing must still decide what the absence means, and in most cases it means that the question remains open and the patient must be watched or referred.

The limits of the bedside examination deserve a paragraph of their own, because most harm comes from what it cannot do. It cannot see through bone or through gas, so the ribs, the bowel, and the lungs hide much of what lies behind them. It cannot show a structure that the operator does not know how to find, and it cannot show what the operator does not think to look for. It rewards confidence, which is a dangerous quality in an operator whose skill is small, and it can produce a normal-looking image that closes a question which should have stayed open. The clinician should therefore treat every view as a question that was asked of one small window, and should say plainly what the window did not include, because a fuller study, a second opinion, or simply more time with the patient may answer what the probe could not.

A constructed illustration may show how the reasoning works, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a young patient who arrives with lower abdominal pain and a missed period, whose pregnancy test is positive, and in whom the probe shows an empty uterus and dark fluid in the abdomen; the clinician recognizes the pattern of a possible ruptured ectopic pregnancy and arranges urgent surgical care while keeping the patient still. Now imagine an older patient in a clinic with sudden back pain and a pulsating feeling in the abdomen, in whom the probe shows a dilated aorta with dark fluid around it; the clinician stops examining, calls for help, and arranges transfer for the leaking aneurysm that the picture suggests. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment.

A clinician who sends a patient home after a bedside examination should say what should bring the patient back. The patient or the family should be told which signs matter, among them a worsening pain, a fever, a faint, a change in consciousness, a failure to pass urine, bleeding, and a breathlessness that grows. A follow-up visit arranged in advance is worth more than a promise to return if things worsen, because many patients cannot judge when their illness has changed. Where the probe was used to set aside a dangerous cause, the clinician should say what would have to happen for that conclusion to be reconsidered. The habit of stating the uncertainty out loud is what makes a limited examination safe rather than merely convenient.

To summarize, ultrasound at the bedside is best understood as a physical sign rather than an imaging study, performed to answer one question that matters for the next decision and repeated as the patient changes. The views that a clinician can learn are few and each carries a clear purpose: the heart for fluid and for a failing ventricle, the chest for fluid and for air outside the lung, the abdomen for free fluid, the aorta, and the gallbladder, the kidneys for obstruction, and the pregnant uterus for the position and the heartbeat. The dangerous findings are named where the view is described, among them a cardiac tamponade, a ruptured ectopic pregnancy, and a leaking aneurysm, and each is revealed by a pattern rather than by a single measurement. What a view can set aside is narrow and what it cannot set aside is wide, so a normal image never closes a question that the history has opened. Every figure in this chapter, whether a width, a depth, a stage of pregnancy, or any other measurement, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. The same reasoning serves the resuscitation room and the clinic where the probe is the only imaging, because the question, the view, and the limit are the same in both. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient who looks unwell is treated and helped first, and the probe is used to make that help faster rather than to delay it.
