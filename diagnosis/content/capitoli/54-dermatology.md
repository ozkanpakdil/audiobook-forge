# Chapter 54 — Dermatology

The skin is the one organ a patient can show and a clinician can see without an instrument, and it is also the organ whose diseases are most often misread, because a rash is a pattern rather than a sentence. Dermatology rewards a method: describe what is there before naming it, compare one part of the body with another, consider the whole patient, and ask what has already been applied. That method, rather than a memory for esoteric names, separates the clinician who recognizes the common diseases from the one who does not. This chapter follows the common skin diseases by their distribution and their evolution, returns to the eruptions that kill, and ends with the same reasoning in a hospital and in a clinic where the only record is a careful description in the notes. The material is educational and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Several dangerous eruptions must be named before any comfortable diagnosis is entertained, because each is revealed by a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. A rash that does not fade when a clear glass is pressed against it may mean bleeding into the skin, and a feverish, unwell patient with such a rash may have an infection in the bloodstream that kills within hours. A rash involving the lips, the mouth, the eyes, or the genitals, especially with fever and skin that burns, may be a blistering reaction to a medicine that can strip the skin from large areas of the body. Skin that is dusky, intensely painful, and spreading fast, with pain far greater than the appearance suggests, may be a necrotizing infection of the deep tissues rather than a simple cellulitis. A lesion that grows, changes, bleeds, or refuses to heal may be a skin cancer, and pale skin and years of sun exposure are not the only risks. A patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, whether by infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, by a transplant, or by cancer treatment, may show any of these dangers in a muted form that is easy to dismiss.

The method works because the skin shows its mechanism on the surface. Disease in the outer layer scales, blisters, or breaks; disease in the deeper layer swells, reddens, or lumps; disease in the vessels bleeds or blocks; and disease in the fat beneath produces nodules and dimpling. Distribution adds meaning, because a rash in the creases speaks of an internal tendency, a rash on exposed surfaces speaks of something outside the body, and a rash following a nerve speaks of a structure beneath the skin. Evolution adds more, since the same disease looks different on the first day, at its height, and as it fades. A clinician who records what the rash is doing today, and what it did last week, has narrowed the field more than any single name can.

Describing a rash needs a small, precise vocabulary, and using it well is worth more than knowing many diagnoses. A macule is a flat change in color, a papule is a small raised lesion, and a plaque is a larger raised area with a flat top. A vesicle is a small blister, a bulla is a larger one, and a pustule is filled with pus. A wheal is a transient raised swelling, a nodule is a deeper solid lump, and a scale is a flake of detached surface skin. Crust is dried fluid, an erosion is a break that does not pass through the full thickness of the skin, and an ulcer is a break that does. Purpura is bleeding into the skin that does not fade when pressed, and it is the most important of these words because of what it can mean; alongside the lesion the clinician records color, warmth, tenderness, border, surface, arrangement, and the exact parts of the body involved.

The examination begins in good daylight, because skin color is almost impossible to judge under the yellow light of a ward at night, and a torch is a poor substitute when it is all there is. The patient must be undressed far enough to show the whole eruption, which often means examining the back, the buttocks, the groins, the armpits, the feet, and the spaces between the toes, since patients show the part that itches and hide the part that shames them. The scalp, the nails, and the moist linings of the mouth, the eyes, and the genitals are part of the skin examination and are often where the diagnosis is made. The clinician compares one side with the other and the lesions with each other, because a rash in which every lesion looks the same suggests one mechanism while a rash of mixed stages suggests another. The fingers feel for warmth, hardening, and tenderness, and the hand can detect a deep swelling the eye cannot see. What is built is not a snapshot but a map, and every later decision is read against that map.

The skin is not a separate organ, and the patient must be considered as a whole before the rash is named. Fever, weight loss, night sweats, joint pain and swelling, mouth ulcers, eye redness, and a general feeling of illness change the meaning of a skin finding, because they move the reasoning from a local problem to a systemic one. The medicines the patient takes, including those bought without a prescription, are among the commonest causes of a new rash, and the timing of each against the timing of the eruption is the clue. The history should also ask about travel, animals, work with chemicals or plants, new cosmetics or detergents, sexual history where infection of the skin or the genitals is possible, and whether anyone else at home or at work has the same complaint. The question most often omitted is what has already been applied, because a market cream, a traditional preparation, a bleaching agent, or a steroid obtained without a prescription can change the eruption before the clinician sees it, hiding the scale of psoriasis or making a fungal infection look like eczema. Where an applied substance is suspected, the finding that supports the diagnosis is a rash that settles when it is withdrawn.

Eczema, or atopic dermatitis, is the commonest inflammatory rash of childhood, recognized by its itch, its distribution, and its course rather than by any single lesion. The itch comes before the rash, and the family describes scratching that disturbs sleep and skin that feels dry and rough. In infancy the eruption favors the cheeks, the scalp, and the outer surfaces of the arms and legs, while in older children and adults it settles into the creases of the elbows and knees, the neck, and the backs of the hands, a shift that reflects how the child moves and what the child touches. The lesions begin as red, poorly defined patches with small papules and progress to dryness, scaling, thickening, and darkened skin lines where scratching has gone on for a long time. The course is one of flares and remissions, and the clinician should ask what precedes a flare, since heat, wool, soap, dust, stress, and infection all appear in different patients. A sudden worsening with crusting, weeping, and tenderness suggests that bacteria have entered damaged skin, and a widespread eruption with fever and blisters in a child with eczema may be a serious viral infection of the skin that needs urgent help.

Psoriasis is a disease of accelerated turnover of the skin, and it declares itself through well-defined plaques with a silvery scale on the outer surfaces of the elbows and knees, the scalp, the lower back, and the buttocks. The plaques are raised, sharply bordered, and often symmetrical, and the scale is thick and dry, so that scraping it away reveals small bleeding points beneath. The nails are part of the diagnosis, and pitting, thickening, separation of the nail from its bed, and yellow-brown discoloration are found in patients whose joints or scalp are involved. New lesions appear along a scratch or a surgical scar, the tendency of the disease to follow injury, and the center of an old plaque may clear and leave a ring easily mistaken for a fungal infection. Psoriasis is not only a skin disease, since a proportion of patients develop a painful, swollen joint disease that can damage the joints permanently if it goes unrecognized. The clinician should ask every patient with psoriasis about joint pain, morning stiffness, and swelling of the fingers and toes, and should examine the joints rather than accept a denial of symptoms.

Fungal infections of the skin are common everywhere, and they are recognized by their shape, their site, and their slow spread rather than by an itch alone. A ring-shaped lesion with a raised, scaly, active edge and a paler center is the classic appearance, and the diagnosis is supported when scale examined under the microscope shows the organism. On the feet the infection sits between the toes, where skin is moist and cracked, and on the soles, where it may look like dry, thickened skin easily mistaken for eczema. On the scalp it causes patches of hair loss with broken hairs and scaling, most often in children, in whom it must be treated properly because it can spread and leave permanent hair loss. In the groin and under the breasts it produces a red, itchy rash with a border more active at the edge, while the same areas may instead be infected by yeast, which tends to give a uniform red rash with satellite spots. The nails become thick, discolored, and crumbly, and nail disease is often the reason a skin infection keeps returning on the hands and feet; a topical steroid given without an antifungal can make the whole picture worse.

Bacterial infection of the skin ranges from a minor nuisance to a threat to life, and the boundary is crossed faster than most clinicians expect. Impetigo begins as small blisters or pustules that break and leave a thick, golden crust, most often around the nose and mouth of a child, and the fingers carry it to other parts of the body. Ecthyma is a deeper form that leaves a punched-out ulcer, and folliculitis is an infection of the hair follicles that appears as tender red papules and pustules in the beard, on the scalp, or on the limbs. Cellulitis is an infection of the deeper skin and fat that produces a warm, red, tender, swollen area with an indistinct border, often with fever and enlarged lymph nodes, and erysipelas is a more superficial form with a raised, sharply defined edge. The clinician should mark the edge of a spreading cellulitis and record the time, because the direction of change over hours is more reliable than the appearance at one visit; a red, swollen leg in a patient with diabetes or poor circulation is not automatically cellulitis, and pain out of proportion to the appearance with rapid worsening should shift the diagnosis.

The necrotizing infection of the deep tissues must be named early, because it destroys muscle and fat within hours while the surface may look deceptively mild. The pain is severe and continuous, far greater than the visible redness suggests, and it often begins before the skin changes at all. The skin then becomes dusky, gray, or blue, blisters of dark fluid appear, the area becomes numb, and a crackling sensation may be felt where gas has formed in the tissues. A falling blood pressure, confusion, or rapid breathing points to the whole-body response that follows. The finding that most strongly supports the diagnosis is the combination of disproportionate pain, rapid spread, and dusky skin, while a stable, well-demarcated, tender red area that improves over the next few hours argues against it. This is a surgical emergency as much as a medical one, and a delay spent on naming the organism is a delay in the only treatment that works.

Infestations are common, curable, and constantly missed, and each has a pattern that gives it away. Scabies produces an intense itch that is worse at night, a rash in the web spaces of the fingers, the wrists, the armpits, the waist, and the genitals, and small burrows that can often be seen with a magnifier and a good light. The itch is caused by an immune reaction rather than by the mite itself, so it may take weeks to appear after a first infestation and only days after a later one, and the whole household must be considered because the infestation spreads by close contact. Head lice produce itching of the scalp and visible eggs glued to the hair shafts, and body lice live in the seams of clothing and mark crowded living conditions. Insect bites produce crops of itchy papules in groups or in lines, and the distribution is often the clue, since bites on the ankles suggest fleas and bites under a waistband suggest a bed. A creeping, red, raised line that moves slowly across the foot is the track of a larva migrating through the skin, and the clinician who treats one patient without treating the household is likely to see the same eruption again.

A medicine can produce almost any rash, and a new eruption in a patient who has started a drug within the previous weeks should be treated as drug-related until proved otherwise. The commonest pattern is a red, widespread, blotchy rash that begins on the trunk and spreads outward, often with itching, and it appears some days after the drug was started rather than at once. Hives are raised, itchy, transient swellings that move from place to place within hours, and they may be part of a reaction that also involves the lips, the tongue, or the throat, which is the form that can obstruct the airway and must be treated as an emergency. A fixed drug eruption is a round, dusky patch that appears in the same place each time the drug is taken, and a reaction to light appears only on skin exposed to the sun. Some reactions come with fever, swollen lymph nodes, an enlarged liver or spleen, and a rise in a particular white cell in the blood, and that combination is serious, requiring the drug to be stopped and the patient managed in hospital. The clinician should ask about every substance, including herbal preparations and medicines prescribed elsewhere, and should record the drug, the day it was started, and the day the rash appeared.

The blistering drug reaction is the eruption that most often kills, and it begins with fever, burning or painful skin, and a feeling of illness before any rash is visible. The skin then develops dusky patches and flaccid blisters, and it may shear away with gentle pressure, leaving raw, weeping surfaces. The moist linings of the mouth, the eyes, and the genitals are involved in the severe forms, and the patient may be unable to eat or to open the eyes because of the pain. The extent of skin loss is used to judge severity, and a figure such as one tenth of the body surface offered as a teaching illustration must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the way the area is estimated and the line that defines the severe form vary between services and editions. The finding that makes stopping the suspected drug the right answer is a temporal link between the drug and the eruption together with the characteristic skin and mucosal changes, and any patient with fever, skin pain, and mucosal involvement needs immediate senior help and a transfer decided before the diagnosis is certain.

A rash that does not disappear when a clear glass is pressed firmly against it is a finding that changes the whole assessment, because it means blood has escaped into the skin. The commonest important cause is infection in the bloodstream, and a feverish, drowsy, cold, or painful patient with such a rash may deteriorate within hours. The rash may begin as a few small spots and spread quickly, and it is often found first on the legs, the buttocks, or the pressure areas, so a patient who is unwell must be examined completely rather than at the site of complaint. Not every non-blanching rash is infection, since a low platelet count, a disease of the blood vessels, a reaction to a medicine, and a bleeding disorder can all produce the same appearance, and the clinician should look for bleeding gums, nosebleeds, bruises without injury, and an enlarged spleen. The finding that most strongly supports infection is a patient who looks seriously ill with fever and a spreading rash, while a rash in a patient who looks well and has other evidence of a bleeding tendency supports another cause. When infection is possible the patient must be treated and helped at once, without waiting for the rash to declare itself further.

Skin cancer is the diagnosis that must not be missed among the many harmless lumps and spots that patients bring, and the single most useful clue is change. A lesion that is growing, that has become darker or more varied in color, that has an irregular border, that bleeds or itches without cause, or that has failed to heal over weeks deserves close examination and, where possible, a sample. The dark tumors are the most dangerous because they spread early, and they occur on the palms, the soles, and under the nails as well as on sun-exposed areas, so a dark lesion in a place never sunburned must not be dismissed. A figure such as 6 millimeters has often been taught as the size above which a dark lesion raises concern, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the size that matters depends on the site, the patient, and the edition of the advice being followed. The pale, pearly lump with tiny visible vessels, the scaly patch that will not heal, and the ulcer with a rolled edge are the other common skin cancers, and each is cured by removal when found early. A patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, a patient with albinism, and a patient with many moles or a family history of skin cancer are at higher risk and deserve particular care. The finding that confirms the diagnosis is tissue examined under the microscope, and until that is done the clinician should record the size, the site, the color, and the change that prompted the visit.

The skin often speaks for an organ the clinician cannot see. A persistent purple-red rash on the face across the cheeks and the bridge of the nose, especially with joint pain, hair loss, mouth ulcers, and fever, may be part of a disease of the connective tissue, and the clinician should examine the joints, the urine, and the blood. Painful red nodules on the shins, often with fever and joint pain, point to inflammation beneath the skin that may follow an infection, a bowel disease, or a medicine. A velvety, darkened thickening of the skin of the neck and the armpits is associated with insulin resistance and with some cancers, and a sudden appearance of many such areas should prompt a search for a hidden tumor. Yellow deposits around the eyes, a purple discoloration of the eyelids, and firm lumps in the skin may each signal a systemic disorder, and so may a rash that appears in the same place as a joint that is inflamed. The clinician who examines the skin with the rest of the body in mind, and who asks what else has changed, will find diagnoses that a skin-only approach would miss.

Certain patients require a different reading of the same skin. In a newborn, a rash may be a common and harmless adjustment of the skin or the first sign of a serious infection, and a baby who is unwell, feeding poorly, or has a rash that does not blanch should be assessed urgently. In an older patient the skin is thin and fragile, so a pressure injury can begin as a red area that does not fade, and the clinician must look at the heels, the sacrum, the hips, and the ears of every patient who cannot move independently. In a patient with darker skin the redness of inflammation is often seen as a purple, brown, or gray change rather than as red, so an eruption can be missed by an examiner who looks only for the familiar color, and the same is true of cyanosis, jaundice, and pallor. In pregnancy, an itchy eruption with blisters, or one with fever and systemic symptoms, must be assessed without delay because some rashes endanger the mother or the baby. In a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, a common infection can look unusual, spread widely, and fail to respond as expected, so the clinician should lower the threshold for seeking help; the same lesion means different things in a newborn, an older adult, and a patient with darker skin.

In a well-resourced hospital the same clinical method is extended with instruments and with help. A dermatoscope, which is a magnifier with a light, reveals the fine structures of a pigmented lesion and helps the clinician decide whether it needs to be sampled, and it is a tool for better description rather than a substitute for judgment. A scraping of scale examined with a simple stain confirms a fungal infection within minutes, a swab identifies bacteria, and a sample of skin examined under the microscope settles the diagnosis of a blistering disease or a tumor. A lamp that shines a particular kind of light can show some infections of the hair and the skin, and patch testing can name a contact allergen. A colleague who examines the patient, or a written description compared with the one already in the record, is often the most useful step when the diagnosis is uncertain, because two descriptions of the same rash are worth more than one. Blood tests and imaging may be needed when the skin finding is part of a systemic disease, and each is chosen because its result would change what is done next.

In a clinic with a torch, a magnifier, and no laboratory, the skin can still be assessed well, because the skin is the one organ that can be examined directly. The torch is held to the side so that the surface texture, the scale, and the raised edge cast shadows that make them visible, and the magnifier turns a burrow or a tiny vessel into something the clinician can describe. A wooden stick, a fingernail, and a clean glass are enough to test whether a rash blanches, and a tape measure or a piece of string makes the size and the spread of a lesion recordable from one visit to the next. The thermometer turns the impression of fever into something that can be recorded and compared, and the stethoscope and the hands complete the general examination that a rash with fever always demands. The clinician should write a description that another clinician could recognize, naming the lesion, its color, its border, its surface, its arrangement, and every part of the body involved, together with what has been applied and what else the patient reports. Where the diagnosis cannot be made, the honest record of an uncertain rash and a plan for review is better than a confident wrong name, because the name may travel with the patient and mislead the next clinician. The patient and the family should be told which changes should bring them back: a rash that spreads quickly, a fever, a rash that does not fade when pressed, pain out of proportion, blistering, or a lesion that grows or bleeds. Follow-up should be arranged rather than promised, and the day of review written down, because a patient asked to return only if things worsen may not be able to judge that.

To summarize, dermatology is pattern recognition disciplined by description, and the clinician who describes before naming will recognize more than the clinician who names first. The common diseases are separated by their distribution and their evolution: eczema in the creases with itch, psoriasis on the outer surfaces with silvery scale, fungal infection at the active edge, bacterial infection with warmth and crusting, infestations with itch that follows the household, and drug eruptions with a new medicine in the recent past. Four habits organize the assessment of any unfamiliar rash: describe the lesion precisely, compare one area and one stage with another, consider the whole patient, and ask what has been applied. The dangers are named at the bedside rather than deferred: the rash that does not blanch, the rash with mucosal involvement, the blistering drug reaction, the necrotizing infection whose pain exceeds its appearance, and the lesion that is a skin cancer. Every figure in this chapter, whether a size, an area, an interval, or a reference range, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. The same method works with a dermatoscope and a colleague and with a torch, a magnifier, and a careful note, and the writing down is part of the diagnosis rather than a chore after it. A patient whose rash worries the clinician deserves another look, a clear explanation, and a plan for what should bring them back.
