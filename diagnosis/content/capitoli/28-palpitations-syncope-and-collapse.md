# Chapter 28 — Palpitations, Syncope and Collapse

Palpitations and syncope lead the clinician down the same path, because both ask what the heart was doing when the patient felt or looked wrong, and whether the circulation failed briefly or is failing now. A palpitation is an unexpected awareness of the heartbeat, described by its rate, its regularity, and its duration. Syncope is a transient loss of consciousness from a fall in blood flow to the whole brain, with complete recovery, and a collapse is that same event seen by a witness, who may describe it differently from the patient. The lethal causes hide inside these ordinary words, and they are named as the symptom is presented rather than deferred. They are a tachyarrhythmia that has compromised the circulation, complete heart block, narrowing of the aortic valve, a clot in the pulmonary arteries, a ruptured aortic aneurysm, a pregnancy implanted outside the uterus, bleeding around the brain, and a hemorrhage from the gut. Each has a finding that would reveal it at the bedside, and that finding is given with the cause. This is educational material and cannot replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

The description the patient gives is the most powerful instrument for a palpitation, and it is available everywhere. The clinician asks what the heartbeat felt like, when it began, whether the start and the end were sudden or gradual, how long it lasted, and how often it has happened. A heartbeat that starts and stops in an instant usually points to a short circuit in the electrical system, while a rhythm that accelerates and fades over some seconds usually points to an irritable focus firing on its own. Regularity carries as much information as rate, and a single extra beat followed by a pause is usually benign in a healthy heart. The same sensation in a scarred heart may mark a dangerous tendency, so the clinician reads every description with the state of the heart in mind.

A fast and perfectly regular rhythm suggests a reentrant circuit, and its common forms differ by how narrow or wide the electrical wave looks. A fast and irregularly irregular rhythm, in which no two intervals are alike, suggests that the upper chambers are firing chaotically and the lower chambers are following at random. A rhythm that is slow when it should be fast, during exertion or fever, is a warning in itself. The clinician matches the description to a mechanism and then asks whether that mechanism is dangerous in this particular heart, because the same rhythm can be trivial in one patient and lethal in another.

Duration and circumstance change the meaning of the complaint and the plan that follows. A palpitation that ends quickly may need only an explanation and advice to return if it changes, while a rhythm that persists for a long stretch can exhaust the heart and lower the pressure even while the patient is still walking. The clinician asks what the patient was doing when it began, whether it starts on standing, on effort, on bending, after alcohol, after fever, or at rest, and whether anything reliably stops it. Palpitations that begin on standing and resolve on lying flat suggest a rhythm sensitive to posture or a fall in pressure when upright.

The features that accompany the palpitation show how much the circulation is suffering. Chest pain, breathlessness, sweating, impending faintness, confusion, or an actual loss of consciousness turn a nuisance into an emergency. The finding that reveals a tachyarrhythmia with hemodynamic compromise is not the rate alone but a fast rhythm with a falling pressure, cool clammy skin, an altered mental state, or fluid in the lungs, and its presence converts the next step from investigation into resuscitation. A pulse of 150 beats per minute may be tolerated by a young person with a healthy heart and may be enough to decompensate an older person with a weak ventricle, so the number cannot be read without the patient attached to it. Treat that rate as an illustration of the reasoning and confirm the figure your service treats as dangerous against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold is changed by age, by pregnancy, and by the health of the heart muscle.

The state of the heart underneath changes the probability that a palpitation or a faint is lethal. A previous heart attack, heart failure, valve disease, or surgery on the heart raises the risk that a rhythm disturbance will be poorly tolerated and that it signals a structural problem. The murmur of a narrowed aortic valve is the classic example, because a valve that cannot open fully limits the output of the heart during exertion and can cause a faint at the moment the patient most needs flow. The finding that reveals it is a harsh ejection murmur best heard at the upper right of the chest and conducted to the neck, with a pulse that rises slowly and weakly and a narrow pulse pressure. Aortic stenosis is one of the lethal causes named at the start, so it must be sought in every patient who faints on exertion, because that faint is the warning before a cardiac arrest.

Complete heart block is the other electrical emergency hidden in this symptom. Conduction between the upper and lower chambers fails, the lower chambers escape at their own slow rate, and the brain may be starved of flow without warning. The patient often describes a fall without a prodrome, sometimes with a brief convulsion as the brain is deprived, which is why the event is easily mislabeled as a seizure. The finding that reveals it is a pulse that is slow and does not rise when the patient is asked to exert, with a tracing that shows the upper and lower chambers beating independently. A rate below 40 beats per minute at rest is the kind of number that should draw attention, but confirm that cut-off against the current guideline and the local protocol, because a slow rate means different things in a trained athlete, an older person, and a patient taking a drug that slows conduction. When the block is symptomatic and persistent, the decision to implant a permanent pacing device is the right answer, while when it is transient and caused by a reversible trigger such as a drug or an electrolyte disturbance, that same decision is the wrong one until the trigger is corrected.

Syncope is approached as a question of mechanism, and the mechanisms are three. In cardiac syncope the heart fails to deliver enough blood, because the rhythm is too fast, too slow, or because a structural obstruction prevents the output. In reflex syncope a normal reflex fires at the wrong moment, as in the faint after pain, heat, emotion, prolonged standing, or coughing. In orthostatic syncope the pressure falls because the patient has risen and the circulation cannot compensate, whether from loss of fluid, from a drug, or from nerve damage. Placing the event in one of these groups narrows the investigations and the urgency, because the cardiac group carries the greatest danger. A fourth possibility is never forgotten, which is that the event was not syncope at all but a seizure, a low blood sugar, an intoxication, or a psychological attack.

Cardiac syncope has a signature that should stop the clinician's hand. It often arrives without warning, at exertion or while the patient is lying down, and recovery may be quick or may leave the patient pale and breathless. A family history of sudden death in a young relative, a known heart condition, or a previous heart attack makes this explanation far more likely. The finding that reveals it may be a murmur, an irregular pulse, a displaced and forceful heartbeat, a raised jugular venous pressure, or an abnormal tracing, and any of these changes the plan at once. Fainting during exertion must not be filed as a simple faint, because it points to a fixed obstruction or a dangerous rhythm that exertion has provoked. The greater the danger in a symptom, the more important it is that the examination be complete before a reassuring label is applied.

Reflex syncope behaves in almost the opposite way, and its complete pattern is the reassurance the clinician is looking for. There is usually a warning of warmth, nausea, a gray or green tinge to the vision, a roar in the ears, and a feeling of detachment before the patient slides down. The event happens while standing, often after a trigger such as the sight of blood, a painful procedure, a hot room, a long queue, or a bout of coughing, and the patient recovers quickly once horizontal. The finding that supports the diagnosis is the whole scene rather than one sign, and the absence of structural heart disease and of an abnormal tracing is what allows the clinician to rest on it. A story that does not fit this pattern neatly should not be given the label of a simple faint merely because the label is comfortable.

Orthostatic syncope is diagnosed at the bedside with a blood pressure cuff, a watch, and a willingness to repeat the measurement. The pressure is taken with the patient lying quietly and then again after standing, and the finding that matters is a fall in the systolic pressure together with the symptoms the patient recognizes. A fall of 20 millimeters of mercury or more in the systolic pressure after standing is often quoted as the threshold, but that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it is adjusted for age, for the baseline pressure, and for whether the patient has high blood pressure at rest. The pulse response is as informative as the pressure, since a rise in pulse suggests that the nerves are working and the problem is volume, while a pulse that fails to rise suggests a failure of the autonomic response. The causes to seek are dehydration, blood loss, prolonged bed rest, a drug that lowers the pressure or relaxes the vessels, and the nerve damage that accompanies diabetes and some other neurological disorders.

The history of the event is taken in three parts. Before the event the clinician wants the position, the activity, the warning, and the circumstances, because a faint on exertion and a faint after a hot bath are different diseases. During the event the clinician wants what the witnesses saw, because the patient has no memory to offer, and the color of the face, the pattern of breathing, the movements, and any injury are the data. After the event the clinician wants how quickly consciousness returned, whether there was confusion, and whether the same thing has happened before. The witness is interviewed deliberately about whether the eyes were open, whether the patient was pale, blue, or flushed, whether the breathing was quiet or absent, and whether the limbs stiffened or stayed limp. A short-lived event with a few jerks at the end may be a faint rather than a seizure, because a brain starved of blood can produce a brief convulsion, and the witness account is what separates the two.

The examination is focused and unhurried, and it begins with the patient lying comfortably at rest. The pulse is counted rather than estimated, and both wrists are felt because a difference between them may point to a problem in the aorta. The heart is listened to in a quiet room, with attention to the murmurs and the added sounds, and the jugular venous pressure is examined in the neck because a raised pressure points to a failing or obstructed heart. The pressure is measured in both arms and then on standing, the lungs are listened to for fluid, and the abdomen is palpated for a pulsatile expansile mass or a tender swelling. The skin is inspected for pallor, bruising, or the small red spots that suggest bleeding, and the legs are examined for swelling or for the tender cord of a clot in the deep veins.

A hemorrhage from the gut can present as a faint before any blood is seen by the patient. The finding that reveals it is a black, tarry stool, which the patient may describe only if asked about the color of the stool rather than the presence of blood, and which may be found only on a rectal examination. The patient may also report vomiting blood or coffee-ground material, and the pulse rises and the pressure falls before the blood count changes, because the body loses whole blood and the remaining fluid has not yet diluted the count. A normal blood count early in a major bleed is therefore no reassurance at all, and the clinician who waits for the laboratory may wait too long. The decision to treat as an emergency rests on the combination of a faint, a fast pulse, a fall in pressure on standing, and the stool, and it does not wait for anemia to appear.

A ruptured aortic aneurysm is another cause of collapse, and its window for treatment is short. The patient is often older, with a history of high blood pressure or smoking, and the pain may be felt in the abdomen, the flank, or the back. The finding that reveals it is a pulsatile and expansile mass in the upper abdomen, felt with both hands placed on either side, with a falling pressure and a rapid pulse. The examiner must palpate the aorta gently and must not delay the decision while trying to be certain, because the examination can be almost normal in the moments before the circulation fails. A patient with a known aneurysm and new back pain has a rupture until imaging or surgery proves otherwise, and the distance to a surgeon decides how fast the journey must begin.

A pregnancy implanted outside the uterus must be considered in every woman of childbearing age who faints with abdominal pain. The finding that reveals it is lower abdominal pain, often one-sided, with a missed or unusual period, a tender mass beside the uterus, pain when the cervix is moved, and the signs of internal bleeding with a rising pulse and a falling pressure. A pregnancy test changes the reasoning more than any other single step, and where no test exists the menstrual history and the signs of early pregnancy must carry the weight. The patient may have slight vaginal bleeding that she attributes to a period, and the clinician who accepts that explanation without asking about its timing may miss the diagnosis. A ruptured ectopic pregnancy is a surgical emergency, and the decision does not wait for imaging to confirm it.

Bleeding around the brain can also present as a collapse, and its warning may be a headache the patient remembers as the worst of his life. The classic story is a sudden severe headache, sometimes with vomiting, neck stiffness, and a brief loss of consciousness at the moment of the bleed. The finding that reveals it is neck stiffness on examination, with a headache of sudden onset and a patient who is drowsy or confused, and the diagnosis is confirmed by imaging or by examination of the fluid around the brain. The clinician must ask specifically about a sudden headache in any patient who collapsed, because the patient may remember only the fall. A normal examination does not exclude a small bleed, so a patient with a warning headache is treated as a possible bleed until the question is settled.

A clot in the pulmonary arteries can announce itself as a faint, particularly when it is large enough to obstruct the output of the heart. The patient may describe breathlessness, chest pain, or a sense of doom, and the examination may show a rapid pulse, a rapid breathing rate, a low oxygen level, and a leg swollen or tender from a clot in the deep veins. The finding that reveals it is the combination of a faint with breathlessness, a fast heart rate, and signs of a clot in the leg, in a patient who has been immobile, pregnant, recently operated on, or has had a previous clot. The pressure may be low and the neck veins raised, which points to a strain on the right side of the heart.

The electrocardiogram, or ECG, is the single most useful test in these symptoms, and it is read as a set of questions rather than as a picture. The clinician asks what the rhythm is, how fast or slow it is, whether conduction between the upper and lower chambers is normal, whether the intervals are prolonged, and whether there is evidence of a previous heart attack, of thickened muscle, or of an unstable blood supply. The tracing may be normal between episodes, and a normal tracing in a patient with a compelling story is not reassurance but a reason to pursue a recording during the symptoms. The finding that changes everything is often a short run of an abnormal rhythm captured while the patient felt the symptom, which is why the timing of the recording matters more than its number of leads. In a hospital the patient can be monitored continuously, and an ambulatory monitor can be worn while the patient goes about ordinary life, with longer recordings used when the episodes are rare.

In a well-resourced hospital the assessment moves quickly because the tools are at hand. The patient is attached to a monitor, a tracing is taken immediately, blood is drawn for the tests that could explain the event, and imaging such as computed tomography, or CT, and magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, is arranged for the brain, the lungs, the aorta, or the pelvis according to the working hypothesis. Monitoring may continue on a ward, and a rhythm nurse or a device clinic may review the recording, while the intensive care unit, or ICU, is involved when the circulation is unstable or the rhythm dangerous. A normal set of tests does not cancel a dangerous story, and the decision about observation and about the pace of further investigation is made on the clinical picture. Every number that returns from the laboratory or the monitor is read against the patient, and the reference ranges used to interpret them must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because those ranges are set differently in different laboratories and editions.

Where there is no monitor and no laboratory, the same reasoning is performed with a stethoscope, a torch, a watch, and a thermometer, and it is not a lesser medicine. The pulse is counted rather than estimated, and the rhythm is described in words, because an irregularity felt at the wrist is a finding even when no tracing exists. The heart is listened to with the patient both lying and standing, any murmur or added sound is noted, and the neck veins are inspected with the patient propped at an angle. The pressure is measured lying and standing, and the patient is watched during the change for the symptoms he described. The eyes are examined with the torch for pallor that might suggest anemia, the stool is checked for evidence of bleeding, and the abdomen is palpated for a mass or a tender swelling. The thermometer may reveal a fever that points to infection, and although a temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is treated as a fever in many settings, that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold is defined differently in different ages and populations.

When a single tracing is the only one that will ever be available, the clinician treats it as one frame of a moving picture. A normal tracing taken after the event says that the heart is not permanently in a dangerous rhythm, but it does not say what the rhythm was during the faint. The witness account then carries the weight the monitor would have carried, and the quality of that account depends entirely on the questions asked. The clinician also uses time as a substitute for equipment, repeating the pulse, the pressure, and the examination after the patient has stood, after a meal, and after exertion if it is safe. A patient whose story is dangerous but whose tests are normal is admitted or transferred rather than reassured, because the absence of evidence is not the absence of disease.

The decisions that follow the assessment concern place and pace rather than a particular drug. A patient with a dangerous rhythm and a compromised circulation needs immediate resuscitation, and the decision to treat belongs to the team that can support the airway, the breathing, and the circulation while the rhythm is corrected. A patient with a slow rhythm and symptoms that persist needs a permanent pacing decision, while the same patient with a transient cause needs that cause corrected first. A patient with a reflex faint and a complete, reassuring story needs explanation, advice about triggers, and a plan to return if the pattern changes. A patient with blood loss needs the source found and controlled, and the decision to replace blood depends on how much has been lost and how well the patient is compensating, not on a single number. The clinician names the decision and the finding that would justify it, and leaves the exact treatment to the protocol and the team that will carry it out.

Safety-netting protects the patient when the diagnosis is uncertain, and it matters especially here because the dangerous episodes are often over by the time the patient is seen. The patient is told what to watch for, which findings should bring him back at once, and where to go if the clinic is closed. The record should state whether the patient had a warning, whether the event occurred on exertion, whether there was a witness, and whether the pressure fell on standing, because those four facts decide much of the risk. A normal examination and a normal tracing do not exclude a dangerous rhythm that has not yet been captured, a small bleed around the brain, a clot that has not yet grown, or a leak from an aneurysm that has sealed itself for the moment. A record that says only that the patient felt faint leaves the next clinician with no way to judge the danger.

Two constructed illustrations show how one finding can reverse the order of a differential, and neither describes a real patient. Imagine an older man who faints while carrying shopping, who has no warning, and whose pulse is slow and does not rise when he is asked to walk, so the description alone already points to a cardiac cause and the tracing confirms that conduction has failed. Now imagine a young woman who faints in a warm queue after a long stand, who feels sick and warm beforehand, and whose pulse and pressure recover as soon as she lies down, so the scene fits a reflex faint and the absence of heart disease and of an abnormal tracing supports it. Change one detail in the second story, so that the faint happens while she sits at rest and there is no warning, and the reflex explanation loses its force. They are constructed examples built for teaching, and real patients are more variable and more surprising than any of them.

Distinguishing a faint from a seizure is one of the most consequential judgments in this chapter. A faint usually begins with a warning, is provoked by standing or by a trigger, lasts a short time, and ends with the patient oriented once the brain is perfused again. A seizure may begin without warning, may be followed by a slow return to full awareness, and is often accompanied by tongue biting on the side, incontinence, and a prolonged period of unresponsiveness. The two overlap, because a faint can produce a few jerks when the brain is briefly deprived of blood, and a seizure can be caused by a sudden fall in pressure. The witness account is the decisive evidence in most cases, and the clinician should record the exact sequence rather than the label the family has already applied.

To summarize, palpitations and syncope are read by matching the patient's description and the witness account to a mechanism, and by dividing the faint into a cardiac, a reflex, and an orthostatic cause, with the cardiac group carrying the greatest danger. The lethal possibilities are named as the symptom is described, among them a tachyarrhythmia with hemodynamic compromise, complete heart block, aortic stenosis, a pulmonary embolism, a ruptured aortic aneurysm, an ectopic pregnancy, a subarachnoid hemorrhage, and a gastrointestinal bleed, each with the finding that would reveal it. The examination of the heart, the pressure on standing, the pulse counted by hand, and the search for pallor, melena, and a tender abdomen are the same reasoning in a hospital full of monitors and in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer. Every rate, pressure, temperature, interval, threshold, and reference range in this chapter is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This chapter is educational material and cannot replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and no illustration in it describes a real patient. If the patient in front of you is deteriorating, the reasoning pauses and the patient is treated, because no diagnosis is worth the delay of someone who is becoming more ill.
