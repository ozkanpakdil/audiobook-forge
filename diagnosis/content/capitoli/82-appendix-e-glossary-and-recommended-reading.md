# Appendix E — Glossary and Recommended Reading

This appendix is a glossary and a spoken guide to further reading, meant to be returned to rather than heard straight through. It holds in one place the words this book has used, each defined as a clinician would define it for a student, so that a listener who has forgotten what a likelihood ratio means, or what the Vindicate framework organizes, can find the answer without searching a shelf. The entries are continuous prose rather than a list, because a list cannot be heard, and a definition is more useful when it carries the reason the term exists. This is educational material, it is not medical advice, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which a patient is seen. Where a term has a short form, the short form is written out rather than abbreviated, so that the voice reading this book pronounces the whole idea. A listener who uses these definitions to avoid a real assessment has misunderstood the book, because the words describe reasoning and never a decision that belongs to the clinician with the patient.

The vocabulary of reasoning comes first, because every later term depends on it. A diagnosis is the best current explanation of the patient's problem, held with a stated confidence and revised as information arrives. A differential diagnosis is the ranked list of explanations that could account for the findings, ordered by how likely each is and, more importantly, by how dangerous each would be if missed. Pretest probability is the estimate before a test is performed, built from prevalence, age and sex, and the history and examination rather than from intuition alone. Sensitivity describes how often a test is positive when disease is present and specificity how often it is negative when disease is absent, so a highly sensitive test helps rule a diagnosis out while a highly specific test helps rule it in. A likelihood ratio expresses how much a result should move confidence, and the positive predictive value depends on how common the disease is in the population tested, which is why a test can mislead in a setting unlike the one where it was studied. The Vindicate framework is a structured prompt for building a differential diagnosis, walking the clinician through categories such as vascular, infectious, neoplastic, degenerative, iatrogenic, congenital, autoimmune, traumatic, endocrine and metabolic causes, so that no family of disease is forgotten in the rush toward the first plausible answer.

The terms of the clinical encounter form the next group. An open question invites the patient to tell the story in the patient's own words, while a closed question seeks one fact, and beginning with the open form shows what the patient thinks matters before the field narrows. The Oldcarts framework guides the history of the present illness through onset, location, duration, character, aggravating and relieving factors, radiation, timing, and severity, so that a symptom is described in full rather than in fragments. The Socrates framework applies the same discipline to pain, asking about site, onset, character, radiation, associations, timing, and the factors that ease or worsen it. A review of systems is the deliberate sweep through organs the patient has not mentioned, and it exists because a patient who came about a cough may not think to mention the weight that has fallen away. Soap notes are the four-part structure of documentation, in which the subjective account, the objective findings, the assessment, and the plan are written in that order, and the structure is a memory aid and not a substitute for thought.

The language of the physical examination is the most concrete. A symptom is what the patient reports, while a sign is what the examiner finds, and a reported symptom depends on the patient's words while a sign depends on the examiner's skill. A pathognomonic finding establishes a diagnosis by itself, and such findings are rare enough that reasoning resting on a single one deserves suspicion. Clubbing is a change in the fingertips and nails that suggests longstanding disease of the lungs, the heart, or the gut, pitting edema is swelling that keeps a dent after pressure from a finger, and cyanosis is the blue color of skin that carries too little oxygen. Jugular venous pressure is the height of the pulsation in the vein of the neck, a bedside estimate of the pressure in the right side of the heart. An apex beat is the point where the heartbeat is felt most strongly, and its position speaks of the size of the heart, while a murmur is a sound of turbulent flow through a valve or a narrowed vessel, described by its timing, its site, and where it travels. Lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, and splenomegaly are enlargement of the lymph nodes, the liver, and the spleen, and each directs the examiner to the system to examine next.

Vital signs and the scores built from them deserve particular care, because a score only counts findings the clinician has already gathered. The quick sequential organ failure assessment is a short bedside check for the patient whose infection may be severe, counting a change in mental state, a breathing rate that has climbed, and a pressure that has fallen, and its items and trigger point must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Systemic inflammatory response syndrome is an older description of the body's widespread response to infection, defined by changes in temperature, pulse, breathing, and the white cell count, and it is a reminder of what to measure rather than a diagnosis. The Glasgow coma scale records consciousness by testing the response of the eyes, the voice, and the movement to command and to pain, and its figures and trigger point differ between editions and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. An early warning score gathers routine observations, gives each a weight, and produces a figure that a nurse or junior clinician can act on, but the weights belong to the service that designed the chart, so verify them locally. A red flag is a finding that raises the chance of a serious cause enough to change the plan at once, and it is acted upon before it is explained. Triage sorts patients by urgency rather than arrival, because the order in which patients are seen should follow danger.

The laboratory vocabulary is where the greatest caution is required, because a number is meaningless without the range and the population it belongs to. A reference range is the span of results found in a defined group of people believed to be well, and it describes that group rather than a universal truth. A laboratory might report a hemoglobin range of about 12 to 16 grams per deciliter and a sodium range of 135 to 145 millimoles per liter, but these only illustrate the kind of number a report contains, and the range belongs to the laboratory and the population it serves, so read the local limits rather than carrying these figures elsewhere. A cut-off is the value at which a measurement is declared abnormal or a decision is triggered, a choice made for a purpose rather than a fact of nature. A blood pressure of 140 over 90 millimeters of mercury is often taught as the line above which high pressure is diagnosed, but that line is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it differs between countries, editions, and the ways a measurement is taken. A false positive declares disease when none is present and a false negative misses disease that is there, and the harm of each depends on what the missed diagnosis would have done. A culture tries to grow an organism so that it can be named and its vulnerabilities tested, a polymerase chain reaction detects its genetic material directly, and a rapid test gives an answer at the bedside quickly at some cost in accuracy.

Imaging and the tests that support diagnosis have their own small vocabulary. A chest radiograph is the plain image of the chest, answering questions about the lungs, the outline of the heart, and the space around the lungs, although it can look almost normal early in a serious illness. Computed tomography, known as CT, builds cross-sectional images from many angles, and magnetic resonance imaging, known as MRI, uses a magnetic field to show soft tissue in detail, and each has limits and a cost and should be chosen because a question has already been framed. Ultrasound uses sound to show structure and movement at the bedside, and an echocardiogram is an ultrasound of the heart. An electrocardiogram, known as ECG, records the electrical activity of the heart and is repeated rather than trusted once, since a single tracing is a moment that has passed. Angiography shows the inside of blood vessels after a contrast material is introduced, a biopsy removes tissue for examination under the microscope, and a lumbar puncture takes fluid from around the spinal cord, performed only once the questions of safety have been answered.

The names of diseases form the largest group, each defined here by the process it describes. Infection is the presence and multiplication of an organism in the body, while inflammation is the response of tissues to injury or infection, and the two often travel together without being the same thing. Sepsis is organ failure caused by the body's own response to infection, so it is the response rather than the organism that threatens the patient. Shock is a state in which the circulation fails to deliver enough oxygen to the tissues, recognized by a falling pressure, a rapid pulse, cool or mottled skin, a change in consciousness, and a shrinking passage of urine rather than by any single figure. A hemorrhage is bleeding heavy enough to matter, and anemia is a shortage of red cells or of the hemoglobin that carries oxygen. A tumor is a growth that has escaped the usual controls on its division, a metastasis is a deposit of that growth elsewhere, leukemia is a cancer of the blood-forming cells, a lymphoma is a cancer of the lymph nodes, and meningitis is inflammation of the membranes around the brain and spinal cord. A stroke is the sudden loss of brain function from a blocked or bleeding vessel, and a transient ischemic attack is the same loss of function that resolves, which is a warning rather than a reassurance.

A group of terms belongs to the chest and the circulation, where a missed diagnosis most often kills. Angina is chest discomfort that comes when the heart's demand for blood exceeds what a narrowed artery can supply, and a myocardial infarction is death of heart muscle from a blocked artery. Heart failure is the state in which the heart cannot meet the body's needs, either because the muscle is weak or because it cannot relax and fill, and it is a syndrome rather than a single disease. An arrhythmia is any disorder of the rate, the rhythm, or the order of the heartbeat, and atrial fibrillation is the irregular rhythm in which the upper chambers quiver instead of contracting, so that clots can form and travel. A deep vein thrombosis is a clot in a deep vein, usually of the leg, and a pulmonary embolism is that clot after it has broken away and lodged in the artery of a lung. Pneumonia is infection of the lung tissue, a pleural effusion is fluid in the space around the lung, and a pneumothorax is air in that space, which can collapse the lung. Dyspnea is the patient's experience of breathlessness, while a raised breathing rate is a sign the examiner counts and records.

Other terms describe the whole patient and appear throughout the book. Edema is swelling from fluid in the tissues, ascites is fluid within the abdominal cavity, and jaundice is the yellow color of the skin and eyes that appears when a substance called bilirubin accumulates. Pallor is an unusual paleness that may reflect anemia or poor circulation, and mottling is the patchy discoloration often seen with a failing circulation. Diarrhea is stool more frequent or more liquid than the patient's normal, and constipation is stool that is infrequent or difficult. A seizure is a sudden abnormal discharge of the brain's electrical activity that may produce movement, altered awareness, or both, and syncope is a transient loss of consciousness from a fall in blood flow to the brain. Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, common in older patients and in those whose infection, medicine, or metabolic state has gone wrong. Fatigue is a lack of energy disproportionate to effort and anorexia is the loss of appetite, and neither should be dismissed as vague, because both are often the first expression of serious disease.

The words that describe how often disease occurs, and in whom, decide how a finding should be weighed. Prevalence is the proportion of a population with a disease at a given moment, while incidence is the number of new cases over a period, so a common chronic disease has high prevalence and small incidence. A risk factor is associated with a higher chance of disease without necessarily causing it, and treating an association as a cause can send the clinician down the wrong path. Absolute risk is the chance of an outcome in a group, relative risk compares that chance with another group, and a relative risk that sounds alarming may rest on a very small absolute difference. Anchoring is the tendency to fix on the first explanation, premature closure is the decision to stop searching once a plausible answer is found, and confirmation bias is the habit of noticing findings that support the favored diagnosis while overlooking those that do not. Availability bias favors the diagnoses that come most easily to mind, and base rate neglect ignores how common a disease is in the population. Shared decision making combines the clinician's knowledge with the patient's values, informed consent follows a full explanation of what is proposed and its uncertainties, and safety netting names what should bring a patient back, to whom, and over what interval. A constructed illustration is an example invented for teaching and described as such, never a real patient and never a portrait of an identifiable case.

The recommended reading is a spoken guide to kinds of text rather than a list of citations. For the foundations of reasoning, the useful works explain how clinicians actually think, including books on judgment under uncertainty and on the psychology of error. For the clinical encounter and the physical examination, the enduring texts are the manuals of bedside method, which describe how a maneuver is performed and what a normal and an abnormal finding feel like, and these reward repeated reading. For the symptom-based chapters, the useful sources are the large general texts of medicine that organize disease by presentation as well as by system, alongside the local guidelines that reflect the diseases a service actually sees. For recognizing the sick patient, the texts of emergency and critical care are the ones to consult, together with the local protocols for resuscitation and for early warning of deterioration. For the system-based chapters and for special populations, the standard specialty texts are the place to go, and the pediatric, obstetric, and geriatric volumes reward the effort because disease behaves differently at the extremes of age. For investigations and decision tools, the useful reading explains what a test measures and what its limits are, and the original description of a score shows the population in which it was derived and what it was never meant to do. For practice and professional development, the literature of case-based learning, reflective practice, and clinical audit extends these habits, and above all the current guideline of the service remains the text that states what is expected there.

To summarize, this appendix has gathered the words of the book in one place, defining each term in the sentence that names it, so that the Vindicate framework, the Oldcarts framework, the quick sequential organ failure assessment, the Glasgow coma scale, and the ordinary language of the bedside can be found again without searching. The vocabulary of reasoning came first, because diagnosis is an estimate of probability rather than a label, and the terms of the history, the examination, the laboratory, the image, and the disease itself followed, each with the reason it earns its place. The reading that was recommended is a guide to kinds of text rather than a list of titles, and it sends the listener back to the current guideline of the service rather than to a shelf of old editions. Every figure in this appendix, whether a reference range, a cut-off, a pressure, a temperature, a rate, or a duration, is an illustration of the kind of number a report may contain and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries and editions. This appendix, like the whole book, is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and no definition here should be used to avoid looking at the patient. The glossary is a way of returning to the reasoning, not a way around it, and the clinician who is uncertain should say so, ask for help, and see the patient again.
