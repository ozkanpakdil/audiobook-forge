# Chapter 44 — Deterioration and Early Warning

Deterioration is rarely a single dramatic event; it is a slope, and the task is to notice the tilt before the patient reaches the bottom. The reasoning here is temporal rather than anatomical, because the question is not what disease the patient has but which way the patient is traveling and how fast. A patient who looks stable may be compensating for a process that is already winning, and the signs that announce the loss of that compensation are quiet and easy to explain away. They are almost always present for hours before the crisis, so the clinician usually has time, provided the time is used. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, not a protocol, and not a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local protocol of your own service.

Several lethal causes of deterioration must be named before the ordinary discussion begins, each with the finding that reveals it. Sepsis, organ failure caused by the response to infection, announces itself through a rising breathing rate, a falling pressure, new confusion, and skin that has turned mottled or cool, and the earliest clue is often the breathing rather than the temperature. Major hemorrhage after trauma or surgery declares itself through a pulse that climbs while the pressure holds, then pallor, restlessness, thirst, and a shrinking passage of urine. Bleeding inside the skull after a head injury reveals itself through a falling level of consciousness, a worsening headache, vomiting, and one pupil larger than the other. Severe pancreatitis and the postoperative patient both deteriorate quietly, and in each the first warning is often a change in breathing, mental state, or color rather than pain.

The body defends its vital functions through compensation, and compensation is why deterioration is so easy to miss. As the circulation fails, the heart rate rises, the vessels of the skin and gut narrow to preserve flow to the brain and kidney, and the breathing rate climbs to correct the acid that accumulates. The patient may therefore hold an acceptable pressure, keep a warm skin, and continue to walk and talk while the reserve behind that appearance is spent. Compensation is a loan, and deterioration is the moment the loan is called in; the skill is to recognize the borrowing before the default. A clinician who waits for the pressure to fall is waiting for the last stage of a process that began hours earlier.

The respiratory rate is the earliest and most neglected warning sign, neglected because it is boring to count. Pain, anxiety, and fever all raise the rate, so a rapid rate is easily attributed to something benign; the error is to do so without asking whether the rise is new. A rate that has climbed from the patient's own baseline over successive observations carries far more than a single figure. A rate above 20 breaths per minute is often used as the point at which concern begins, but that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted boundary shifts with age, pregnancy, and the edition of the reference in use. Counting for a full minute, with the patient unaware, remains the cheapest and most sensitive test in medicine.

A change in mental state is the second quiet herald, and it is most often blamed on age, exhaustion, or the hour of the night. New confusion, drowsiness, agitation, or a sudden inability to follow a conversation may be the first evidence that the brain is short of oxygen, blood, or the right chemical environment. In an older patient, an infection, a low blood sugar, a stroke, or a reaction to a medicine may present as confusion alone, with no fever and no pain. The clinician must establish the patient's usual mental state from the patient, the family, or the record, because a change can only be recognized against a baseline. A patient who was lucid this morning and is vague this evening has deteriorated, whatever the cause proves to be.

Urine output is the window through which the clinician watches the kidney, and the kidney often complains first when the circulation is inadequate. Output that shrinks over successive hours suggests that blood flow to the kidney has fallen, whether from fluid loss, a failing heart, or the widened vessels of infection. A patient who has passed no urine for several hours, or whose output has halved, is telling the clinician something no pressure reading can. A commonly quoted figure is a urine output below half a milliliter for each kilogram of body weight in each hour, but that threshold is an illustration and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since it varies with age, body size, and the definition used by the service. In a clinic without a catheter the question is simpler and just as useful: when did the patient last pass urine, how much, and has that changed?

The color of the patient can be read from the end of the bed and changes before the numbers do. Pallor, a gray or dusky tinge, mottling of the knees and elbows, and coolness of the hands and feet all speak of blood diverted from the skin. The examiner presses a fingertip on the sternum or knee for a few seconds and watches the color return; a delay beyond about 3 seconds is often taught as poor perfusion, but that figure is illustrative and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because both the method and the boundary differ between services. The lips, tongue, and conjunctivae should be examined with a torch, because cyanosis, jaundice, and severe anemia each carry a different meaning. A patient whose color has changed since the last observation has deteriorated, even when the temperature, pulse, and pressure are unchanged.

The oxygen saturation is another number that can mislead, whether displayed on a monitor or inferred from color. A reading that looks acceptable does not prove that the patient is ventilating well, because it describes the oxygen carried in the blood and says nothing about the removal of carbon dioxide. The reading is also unreliable when the hands are cold, when the patient is moving, when the nail is painted, and in patients with darker skin, in whom the device may overestimate the true value. The clinician should treat saturation as one clue among several and return to the breathing rate and the work of breathing, which need no device. Where a value is used as a trigger for action, that trigger must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because practice differs between services and devices.

The person who knows the patient best often detects deterioration first, and that observation deserves to be treated as data. A relative who says the patient is not himself, that the color is wrong, or that something feels different is reporting a comparison the clinician, meeting the patient, cannot make. That intuition is not a diagnosis, but it is a sensitive alarm, and the response is to re-examine rather than reassure. The clinician should ask what exactly has changed, when, and what the patient was like yesterday, then look again with those answers in mind. A family's concern dismissed as anxiety is a missed observation, and the record should show it was heard.

A single normal value is the most seductive reassurance in medicine, and it is why warning systems exist. An acceptable pressure, a settling temperature, a falling pulse, or a laboratory result that looks unremarkable can each quiet the clinician while the patient continues to worsen. The pressure is the clearest example, because it is the last vital sign to change and may stay normal until the reserve is nearly gone. The temperature is another, because a serious infection can produce a normal or low temperature, especially in the very old, the very young, and the patient whose immune defenses are suppressed. The remedy is not to distrust every number but to read each as part of a sequence, asking what it was an hour ago and which way it is moving.

Early warning scores were designed to formalize this reasoning, and the reasoning, not the arithmetic, is what the listener should carry. The insight is that several mildly abnormal signs, taken together, predict deterioration far better than any one alone. A score assembles the breathing rate, the saturation, the temperature, the pressure, the pulse, and the level of consciousness, gives each a weight, and produces a number that rises as the patient drifts. The value lies less in the figure than in the obligation it creates: a rising score demands that a named person attend, assess, and decide. A score does not diagnose and does not replace the examination, and it can falsely reassure when the signs are abnormal in a direction it does not capture.

The score is a rule derived from a population, and like every rule it carries assumptions the clinician must know. It was built and validated in particular patients, often on hospital wards, and it may behave differently in pregnancy, in a child, and in the patient whose usual pressure or saturation is already abnormal. The quick sequential organ failure assessment, which asks about the breathing rate, the mental state, and the pressure, formalizes a similar logic and carries the same limits. A patient with long-standing lung disease may live at values that would alarm a healthy person, so deterioration means a change from the personal baseline rather than a return to the textbook. Those usual values should be recorded, and wherever fixed figures define the acceptable range, they must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the range differs with age, pregnancy, and chronic disease.

The patient who is compensating is the patient in greatest danger, and the compensation is invisible unless deliberately sought. The young, the fit, and the patient with good reserve can hold the pressure, keep the mind clear, and continue to walk and talk while the circulation fails underneath. The signs are subtle: a resting pulse faster than the patient admits to noticing, a breathing rate that is up, a cool periphery with a warm trunk, and an anxiety out of proportion to the situation. The clinician should compare the patient with the patient's own earlier state rather than with a general impression of how ill a person ought to look. When the reserve runs out, the change can be abrupt and irreversible within minutes, and the pressure that finally falls is the last event rather than the first.

Improvement and exhaustion can look alike, and telling them apart is among the hardest judgments in acute care. A patient whose pulse has fallen and who seems calmer may be recovering, or may be tiring toward failure, and the distinguishing question is what the other signs are doing. If the breathing rate is still high, the skin still cool, and the urine still scanty while the pulse has come down, the fall may reflect exhaustion rather than recovery. A patient who was restless and is now quiet, or who fought the oxygen mask and now accepts it without protest, may be losing the struggle rather than winning it. The clinician should read the work of breathing and the level of consciousness beside the pulse, because improvement should appear in the whole picture and not in one number.

Certain situations carry a known risk of deterioration, and sepsis is the first among them. Sepsis is organ failure caused by the response to infection, and a patient can move from looking unwell to critically ill within hours. The findings that demand action are a breathing rate that has climbed, a changed mental state, a pressure below the accepted threshold for that patient, mottled or cold skin, and a falling urine output. A normal temperature does not exclude it, and the older patient, the patient receiving treatment that suppresses the immune response, and the patient with diabetes may show little fever at all. A suspected infection should be assessed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the thresholds and definitions differ between services and editions, and the decision to treat rests on the clinical picture rather than a single result.

Hemorrhage is the second expected cause, and its danger is that the visible loss understates the hidden one. Bleeding into the chest, abdomen, pelvis, or gut may leave little external evidence, and the patient may look surprisingly well until compensation fails. The early findings are a rising pulse, a narrowing pulse pressure, restlessness, thirst, and pallor, with the pressure falling only late. Bleeding into the gut is often announced by vomiting blood or passing black stool, but a slow loss may show only as weakness, breathlessness on effort, and a falling hemoglobin. In a patient after trauma or surgery, or in a woman with abdominal pain and a missed period, bleeding should be assumed until excluded, because the revealing sign is often the pulse rather than any single test. Any figure defining the rate of rise that demands action must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since those boundaries are drawn differently in different settings.

A head injury is the third expected situation, and it is dangerous because the patient may be lucid for a period before worsening. Bleeding inside the skull raises the pressure within it, producing a brief loss of consciousness, a lucid interval, and then a worsening headache, vomiting, and drowsiness. The finding that reveals it is a pupil larger than the other and sluggish to react, with a rising pressure and a slowing pulse, and that combination needs immediate senior help and imaging. The Glasgow coma scale is the standard way of recording consciousness, and the reasoning it formalizes is the response to voice, to pain, and to command; its exact contents and the figure that should cause alarm must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the version in use varies between services and editions. Older age, alcohol, and a medicine that thins the blood each lower the threshold for observation and imaging.

Severe pancreatitis is the fourth situation, and its early appearance can badly understate its course. The pain is often severe and boring, reaching to the back and easing when the patient leans forward, with vomiting that does not relieve it. The danger lies not only in the pancreas but in the fluid lost into the tissues, the falling circulating volume, and the distant organ failure that follows, so a patient who seems stable may deteriorate over the next day. The findings that matter are a rising pulse, a climbing breathing rate, a falling urine output, and a pressure drifting down, with bruising around the umbilicus or flanks in the most severe cases. Scoring systems exist to predict severity, and their thresholds must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since they were derived in particular populations and their boundaries differ between editions.

The postoperative patient is the fifth situation, and surgery itself supplies a plausible explanation for almost every warning sign. Pain, nausea, a raised pulse, and a rapid breathing rate are all expected after an operation, and that expectation is what makes deterioration easy to excuse. The clinician must ask which findings are proportionate to the procedure and the time since it was performed, and which have appeared or worsened in a way the operation does not explain. The treatable causes include bleeding, infection, a clot in the lung, an airway problem, a collection of pus, and the effects of the anesthetic and the medicines given for pain, and each shows itself through a different part of the assessment. A patient whose breathing rate climbs, whose mental state changes, or whose wound or drain changes character should be reassessed at once, and the figures defining the acceptable range after surgery must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because they vary with the operation and the service.

Pain can be a herald of deterioration as well as a symptom to be relieved, and a new pain deserves an explanation rather than only an analgesic. Sudden severe pain in the chest, back, abdomen, or head may mark a clot in the lung, a dissection of the aorta, a perforated organ, or bleeding inside the skull, and each can be present while the vital signs still look acceptable. Pain that is severe beyond the appearance of the patient is a warning in its own right and should prompt a fuller assessment. The patient who cannot report pain, because of sedation, dementia, a language barrier, or critical illness, must be watched through behavior, posture, and the signs the body shows without words.

The setting changes the tools but not the reasoning, and the contrast is sharpest in how observations are gathered. On a monitored ward the pulse, pressure, saturation, and heart rhythm are displayed continuously, and alarms announce a change at once. That abundance has its own hazard, because a clinician can become a reader of screens rather than an examiner of patients, and no monitor shows the color of the skin, the work of breathing, or the confusion the family noticed. Where observations are written by hand every 4 hours, the record between those times is a blank, and the patient may deteriorate unseen. The interval of 4 hours is a convention rather than a law and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the appropriate frequency differs with the patient's condition, the service, and the resources available. The clinician who increases the frequency when worried is using time itself as a diagnostic instrument.

In a well-resourced hospital, deterioration is anticipated through a system rather than the memory of one clinician. The observations are charted and scored, a rising score triggers a call to a designated responder, and the laboratory and imaging service answer questions within minutes or hours. The arterial blood gas, the lactate, the blood count, and the chest radiograph each add a piece to the picture, while ultrasound and CT can find the collection of blood or pus the examination suspected. The senior clinician who attends should ask not only what is wrong but what is changing, and should set an explicit plan for the next observation and the trigger for calling again. The machinery never replaces standing at the bedside, and the clinician who does both catches the deterioration the monitor shows only as a number.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is carried by the hands and the watch. The breathing rate is counted for a full minute, the pulse is felt and counted, and the temperature is written down with the time beside it. The torch shows the color of the lips and tongue, the size of the pupils, the moisture of the skin, and the rash that would otherwise be missed, while the stethoscope reveals the breathing and heart sounds a monitor would have displayed. The urine is collected and measured where possible, and the patient is asked when urine was last passed. The clinic's disadvantage is the absence of tests; its advantage is continuity, because the same clinician may see the patient again in an hour and compare rather than guess.

Deterioration is often missed at the joints of care rather than at the bedside, and those joints are the handover and the escalation. At night the observations are less frequent, the laboratory slower, and the senior help further away, so a change noticed at noon may be recorded only as a note that the patient slept badly. The clinician on duty should visit the patients flagged during the day, read the trend rather than the last set of numbers, and ask the nursing staff which patient worries them most. A request for help with no named recipient and no expected time of arrival is not an escalation but a hope. The behavior of the team is part of the system, and a hierarchy that discourages the junior voice disables it; a nurse who has watched the patient all shift often notices the change first.

Trajectory is the central concept of this chapter, and it is measured rather than remembered. Each set of observations should be documented with its time, so that the direction of travel is visible to the next reader. A patient whose breathing rate has risen twice, whose urine has fallen, and whose mental state has shifted is deteriorating, even when the latest pressure is normal and the temperature has settled. Two observations separated by an interval are worth more than one perfect examination, and the interval should be chosen according to risk rather than routine. Where the patient is stable a longer interval is reasonable, and where the patient worries the clinician it should shorten; the frequency chosen must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the recommended interval differs with the condition, the setting, and the staffing available.

The treatment decision in deterioration turns on the same logic as the diagnosis, and it belongs to the clinician who knows the patient. Treatment is the right answer when the patient looks seriously ill, when the signs of organ failure are present, when the trajectory is clearly downward despite initial measures, or when a treatable cause such as infection or bleeding is strongly suspected and waiting would cost more than acting. Treatment is the wrong answer when a clear alternative explanation accounts for the findings, when the patient is stable and the diagnosis can be completed safely first, and when treating before the samples are taken would destroy the evidence the diagnosis depends on. Naming the finding that would justify or forbid treatment is more useful than naming a medicine, because the reasoning travels between settings even when the drugs do not. No drug, no dose, and no regimen appear anywhere in this chapter, and that omission is deliberate: the decision, not the prescription, is what this book teaches.

A constructed illustration may fix these habits, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than a portrait of any real person. Imagine an older man a few days after an operation, described as comfortable, whose temperature is normal and whose pressure is unchanged from the morning. The nurse mentions that he seems vague, that his breathing is faster than yesterday, and that he has passed very little urine, and the clinician who listens counts the rate, looks at the color of the skin, and finds it mottled at the knees. The picture is one of a patient who is compensating and losing, and the response is not a new medicine but a full reassessment, samples, help, and a shorter interval before the next observation. Now imagine the same findings in a young woman with a fever and a cough, where the breathing rate is explained by pain and the confusion by a sleepless night, and where the safe answer is still to look again in an hour. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment.

When a patient is not admitted, the early warning reasoning follows the patient home as specific advice. The patient and family should be told which changes must bring them back, how quickly, and where to go at that hour, in words they can repeat rather than a general instruction to return if worse. The signs worth naming are a breathing rate the patient can feel is too fast, a new confusion or drowsiness, a color that has changed, a passage of urine that has stopped or nearly stopped, a fever that will not settle, and a pain that is worsening. A follow-up visit arranged in advance is worth more than a promise, because many patients cannot judge their own deterioration and will wait for permission. The record should show what was said, because safety netting that is not documented cannot be reviewed.

Every figure in this chapter, whether a breathing rate, a pressure, a temperature, a urine volume, an interval between observations, or a score, is an illustration of reasoning rather than a rule to apply unchanged. Such numbers are drawn from particular populations, editions, and laboratories, and they differ between countries and services, so each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it influences care. The clinician who understands why the breathing rate rises, why the urine falls, and why the confusion appears can work without any score at all, and can use a score well when one is available. That understanding is what this book is for, and it is what remains when the chart, the monitor, and the laboratory are taken away.

To summarize, deterioration is a slope rather than an event, and it is recognized by the direction of travel rather than by a single reading. The quiet heralds are a rising breathing rate, a new confusion, a shrinking passage of urine, a change in color, and the relative who says that something is wrong. The lethal causes, among them sepsis, major hemorrhage, bleeding inside the skull, severe pancreatitis, and the complications of surgery, are named at the bedside with the finding that reveals each. A single normal value reassures too easily, the compensating patient is in the most danger, and improvement must be distinguished from exhaustion by looking at the whole picture. Early warning scores formalize several mildly abnormal signs, and their trigger points must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because they differ between countries, editions, and services. The same vigilance is possible on a monitored ward and in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, because the reasoning, not the machinery, detects the patient who is slipping. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient who worries the clinician is one to be reassessed, discussed with a senior colleague, and watched more closely, never one to be reassured and left.
