# Chapter 47 — Gastrointestinal and Hepatic Disease

The gut and the liver announce themselves through patterns rather than single symptoms, and this chapter follows those patterns: the obstruction of a tube, the inflammation of a wall, the loss of continuity, the failure of a factory, and the rise in pressure when blood cannot pass freely through the liver. The same ache beneath the breastbone may belong to the stomach, the pancreas, the gallbladder, or the heart, and the same yellowing of the eyes may be the fading trace of a mild infection or the first sign of a liver losing its function. The clinician's work is to decide which pattern the story fits, and then to ask which dangerous member of that pattern could produce the same picture. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is no substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.

A short list of lethal possibilities belongs at the front, each paired with the finding that would reveal it. A perforated peptic ulcer declares itself through sudden severe pain with a rigid, motionless abdomen, and the loss of the normal dullness over the liver is the clue that gas has escaped beneath the diaphragm. A variceal hemorrhage appears in a patient who already carries the marks of chronic liver disease, who vomits a large volume of fresh blood and then turns pale, sweaty, and faint. Ascending cholangitis, which is infection behind a blocked bile duct, shows itself as fever with shivering, yellowing of the skin and eyes, and pain in the right upper abdomen. Pancreatic necrosis is betrayed not by the first hours of pain but by a patient who fails to improve, in whom organ failure persists and fever develops, and fulminant hepatic failure is revealed by a drowsy or confused patient, a liver that can no longer be felt as it shrinks, and blood that will not clot.

Disease of the gut and the liver is often advanced before it is noticed, partly because of its nerve supply and partly because of the reserve of the liver. The organs of the abdomen are supplied by nerves that sense stretching and contraction rather than precise location, so an early tumor, an early ulcer, or an early scar produces a discomfort the patient cannot place. The liver continues to work while much of it is being replaced by scar, which is why a patient may feel entirely well while the disease is already established. The first sign is therefore often not pain but a change in what the body can do, whether the failure to clear a yellow pigment, the failure to clot blood, or the accumulation of fluid in the abdomen.

Peptic disease begins where the lining of the stomach or the duodenum is injured faster than it can repair itself, and the two injuries that matter most are infection by the bacterium Helicobacter pylori and the repeated use of medicines that reduce the protection of the stomach. The injury is usually painless until it is deep, so a patient may carry an ulcer for a long time and remember only a period of indigestion, and the pain characteristically comes when the stomach is empty and eases with food. The same disease can produce no pain at all and declare itself by bleeding, which is why an unexplained anemia in an older patient is a reason to think of the stomach. The clinician should ask about painkillers taken for joints or headaches, because many patients do not consider these to be medicines. The finding that would make ulcer disease unlikely is a pain clearly provoked by food and relieved by fasting, or a picture the history explains better as something else.

The two complications that convert peptic disease into an emergency are bleeding and perforation, and each has its own bedside signature. Bleeding may be small and chronic and show itself only as a falling blood count, or it may be sudden and large, with vomited blood or black, tarry stool and the signs of a circulation short of volume. Perforation produces the moment the patient remembers exactly, a severe pain that starts in the upper abdomen and spreads, and an abdomen that is rigid and still. The finding that reveals perforation most clearly is the rigid abdomen together with the loss of the normal dullness over the liver, since gas beneath the diaphragm changes the note obtained when that area is tapped. The decision that follows is surgical rather than medical, and the finding that would make that decision wrong is another cause such as pancreatitis or a clot in the mesenteric vessels.

Acute hepatitis is inflammation of the liver that develops over days, and its cause is usually one of the viruses named by letters, most often hepatitis A, hepatitis B, or hepatitis E. The illness begins like an ordinary infection, with fever, aching, nausea, and poor appetite, and the yellowing of the skin and eyes appears afterwards, often as the patient begins to feel better. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is often used as the line above which fever is recorded, but that figure is only an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted value changes with the route of measurement and the edition of the reference. Hepatitis A and hepatitis E are usually spread by contaminated water and food and usually resolve, while hepatitis B can pass through blood, sexual contact, and from a mother to her child, and it may become persistent.

The danger hidden inside acute hepatitis is fulminant hepatic failure, in which the liver loses its function faster than it can be repaired. The earliest sign is often a change in behavior or sleep rather than pain, and a patient who becomes drowsy, irritable, or difficult to rouse during a bout of hepatitis must be treated as an emergency. The breath may take on a sweet odor, the liver that was enlarged may become impossible to feel as it shrinks, and the blood stops clotting, so the patient bleeds from the gums, the nose, or the gut. A coarse flapping tremor may appear when the hands are held out, and the Glasgow coma scale records the level of consciousness through the response to voice, to pain, and to command; its contents and the figure that should cause alarm must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the version in use varies between services. The decision then is transfer to a unit that can support the failing organs, unless another cause such as low sugar or an infection of the brain explains the drowsiness.

Chronic hepatitis is the form that matters most for the clinician in a clinic, because it is quiet and because it ends in cirrhosis and in cancer of the liver. The boundary between acute and chronic is usually drawn at 6 months, and that interval is an illustration of the reasoning which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the definition shifts between services and editions. Hepatitis B becomes persistent most often in those infected very young, and it can be passed from a mother to her newborn, which is why the test belongs in the care of every pregnant woman wherever it can be offered. The patient usually has no symptoms at all, and testing is offered to anyone from a region where the virus is common, to a person who has injected drugs, and to a person with infection by the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV.

Cirrhosis is the end of a long process in which working liver tissue is replaced by scar, and its signs on examination follow directly from what the liver can no longer do. The liver stops clearing a pigment, so the eyes and skin may yellow, and it cannot make the proteins that hold fluid inside the vessels, so the legs swell and the abdomen fills. The pressure in the vein that drains the gut rises, and blood finds other routes back to the heart, which produces swollen veins at the lower end of the esophagus, dilated veins around the navel, and an enlarged spleen. The skin of the upper trunk may carry small red vessels with fine legs, the palms may be red, and the patient may bruise easily because the clotting factors are no longer made. Hormonal changes appear as loss of body hair, enlargement of the breast tissue in men, and shrinkage of the testicles, and the clinician who finds these signs knows that a jaundiced patient has chronic disease rather than a passing infection.

Decompensation is the moment when a compensated cirrhosis tips into failure, and it is recognized by four events that may arrive in any order. Fluid collects in the abdomen, and the examination finds a rounded, tense belly, shifting dullness when the patient turns, and a fluid thrill that confirms the fluid is free rather than contained. The brain is affected by substances the liver no longer clears, and the patient becomes forgetful, drowsy, or confused, sometimes with the flapping tremor described earlier. The swollen veins in the esophagus may burst, and the patient bleeds from the mouth or passes black stool. The fluid in the abdomen may itself become infected, which produces fever, pain, and a worsening of the general state, and this is easily missed because the signs are mild, so each of these events demands a decision rather than a period of waiting.

A variceal hemorrhage is the bleed that kills most quickly, and it deserves its own reasoning. Long-standing liver disease raises the pressure in the vein that drains the gut, and that pressure is carried backward into the veins of the esophagus and the upper stomach, which swell and become thin-walled. When one of them bursts, the patient vomits a large volume of blood, the pressure falls, and the liver that caused the problem is often no longer able to make the factors that clot the blood. The findings that reveal it are the marks of chronic liver disease, an enlarged spleen, free fluid in the abdomen, a history of heavy alcohol use or long-standing viral hepatitis, and a previous episode of bleeding. Where endoscopy is available the airway and the circulation are secured first and the esophagus is examined as soon as the patient is stable, and where it is not, the whole decision is to begin the journey to a place that has endoscopy and blood, immediately rather than after a second look.

Ascending cholangitis is an infection that climbs the bile ducts behind an obstruction, and it is dangerous because it can move from a fever to a failure of the circulation within hours. The classical picture is fever with shivering, yellowing of the skin and eyes, and pain in the right upper abdomen, and the patient may become confused or drowsy while the pressure falls. The finding that reveals the obstruction most reliably is a dilated duct system on ultrasound, and where that is available it should be sought at once. The decision is to relieve the obstruction and to treat the infection, and the finding that would make treatment alone the wrong answer is a duct that is completely blocked, because the pressure must be released for the treatment to work.

Acute pancreatitis is inflammation of a gland that lies behind the stomach, and it is usually caused by a stone passing through the bile duct or by alcohol, though many cases have no identified cause. The pain is steady, severe, and boring, it sits in the upper abdomen, and it often travels to the back and eases when the patient leans forward, while vomiting does not relieve it. The abdomen may be only mildly tender at first, which is one of the traps of the disease, because the pain is far greater than the examination suggests. The finding that would make pancreatitis unlikely is a pain that is clearly colicky and comes in waves, or an examination that finds another explanation such as a perforated ulcer or a blocked mesenteric vessel.

The severity of pancreatitis is judged at the bedside by what the disease is doing to the rest of the body rather than by the level of the enzymes. Mild disease settles within days, while severe disease is marked by organ failure that does not correct, and the clinician watches the pressure, the breathing, the urine output, and the level of consciousness to detect it. A systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the point at which the circulation must be considered compromised, and that figure is an illustration which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the pressure that matters depends on the patient's usual value, on age, and on any medicine being taken. A failure that persists beyond the first 2 days is a different illness from one that corrects when the circulation is supported, and that interval must also be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the definitions used by different services do not agree. The appearance of fever, a rising pulse, or a fall in the oxygen level of the blood suggests that part of the gland has died, which is pancreatic necrosis, and imaging confirms it, but a patient who seems to improve and then worsens is the one most likely to be developing infected dead tissue.

Appendicitis is the commonest surgical emergency of the abdomen, and its diagnostic value lies in the movement of the pain. The earliest pain is a vague ache around the navel, caused by the appendix stretching before it is inflamed enough to touch the wall, and it is often accompanied by loss of appetite, nausea, and a mild fever. Over some hours the pain moves to the right lower abdomen, becomes sharp and well localized, and is made worse by movement, by coughing, and by the jarring of a heel drop. The examination then finds tenderness at a point in the right lower quadrant, guarding of the wall, and pain in that place when the left side of the abdomen is pressed, which is the sign that the inflammation has reached the peritoneum.

Several dangerous conditions imitate appendicitis, and they must be considered before the diagnosis is settled. In any woman of childbearing age, a pregnancy outside the uterus produces pain, fainting, and the signs of internal bleeding, and a pregnancy test is the single step that changes the reasoning more than any other. Torsion of an ovary, an infection of the kidney, a stone in the ureter, and a twisted piece of bowel each produce pain in the same region, and the clinician who examines the urine, the flank, and the hernial openings will not be caught by surprise. In children the lymph nodes inside the abdomen can inflame after a viral illness and produce an almost identical picture, which is why repeated examination over hours is the safest course when the child is stable. In older adults the appendix may lie behind the colon or low in the pelvis, so the pain and the tenderness appear elsewhere, and in pregnancy the growing uterus pushes the appendix upward and the diagnosis is easier to miss.

Inflammatory bowel disease is a group of conditions in which the immune system attacks the lining of the gut, and the two main forms are ulcerative colitis, which affects the colon continuously from the rectum, and Crohn disease, which can affect any part of the gut in patches and can burrow through the wall. Ulcerative colitis produces bloody diarrhea, urgency, cramping pain before the stool, and a feeling of incomplete emptying, and the attacks come and go over months and years. Crohn disease produces pain, diarrhea that may not contain blood, weight loss, fever, and a tender mass in the right lower abdomen, and because the disease reaches through the wall it can form tunnels to other organs, narrow the bowel, or open onto the skin. The clinician should ask about blood in the stool, weight, night symptoms, and family history, and should not settle on irritable bowel when any of these are present.

What makes inflammatory bowel disease worth its own reasoning is that it speaks through organs far from the gut. The joints may swell and hurt, especially the large joints of the legs and arms, and pain in the lower back that is worse at rest and improves with movement suggests inflammation of the spine rather than a mechanical strain. The skin may show tender red nodules on the shins or painful ulcers that do not heal, and the eyes may become red, painful, and sensitive to light, which is a reason to ask about vision directly because the patient may not connect it with the bowel. The mouth may carry ulcers, and the liver and bile ducts may develop a chronic inflammation that appears as an abnormal liver test long before any symptom. A blood count may fall because of ongoing loss of blood and of inflammation, so the patient may be anemic without any visible bleeding, and the joint, skin, and eye problems often follow the activity of the bowel while the liver and spine problems run their own course.

The complications of inflammatory bowel disease are the reason the diagnosis matters, and the most feared is a sudden widening of the colon with loss of its muscle tone, which produces a distended, tender abdomen, fever, a fast pulse, and a patient who looks toxic. A narrowing of the bowel produces colicky pain, distension, and vomiting, and a tunnel between the bowel and the bladder, the vagina, or the skin produces a discharge that the patient may find hard to describe. Bleeding can be heavy, and long-standing disease of the colon raises the risk of cancer, which is why the duration and the extent of the disease are recorded with care. The decision to treat the inflammation is the right answer when there is clear evidence of active disease in the bowel, when the complications are absent, and when infection has been excluded. It is the wrong answer when the patient has a fever and bloody diarrhea that may be an infection rather than a flare, because suppressing the immune response in that situation can be dangerous, and the stool test together with the story of travel and recent treatment is what decides between the two.

The history of a digestive complaint is taken with a discipline that leaves nothing to memory, and the Oldcarts framework is a useful way to keep the account complete. The clinician asks about the site, the way the symptom began, its character, what makes it better or worse, and what accompanies it, and then asks the patient to compare this episode with any earlier one. The bowel habit belongs in the same conversation, including the frequency, the consistency, the color, any blood, any mucus, and any change in what the patient considers normal. Medicines are asked for by name and by purpose, with particular attention to painkillers, and alcohol and tobacco are recorded in the patient's own units rather than dismissed. Travel, water, food, sexual history, injections, and family history of cancer or of bowel disease complete a picture that no examination can replace.

The examination begins before the hand touches the abdomen, with the general appearance, the color of the skin and eyes, the state of hydration, and the alertness of the patient. The hands are examined for redness of the palms, the nails for clubbing or a change in shape, and the eyes for yellowing, because each is a visible sign of disease that has been present for some time. The abdomen is then inspected, listened to, percussed, and finally palpated, with the most painful area left until the end, and the hernial openings, the groins, and the umbilicus are sought in every patient. The liver is measured by its span, the spleen is sought as a dullness that moves with breathing, and free fluid is detected by shifting dullness and by a thrill. A rectal examination is performed when there is bleeding or an unexplained picture, and the testicles are examined in every man or boy with abdominal pain, because torsion can present as abdominal pain alone.

In a hospital the laboratory and the imaging are asked specific questions, and the reasoning that directs them is the reasoning used at the bedside. The pattern of the liver tests is more informative than any single value, because a rise dominated by the enzymes released from liver cells points toward hepatitis, while a rise dominated by the enzymes of the bile ducts points toward obstruction. Ultrasound answers whether the bile ducts are dilated, whether the liver is irregular, whether the spleen is enlarged, and whether fluid is present, and it is the first image to obtain in most patients with jaundice. Computed tomography, or CT, and magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answer particular questions about the pancreas, the ducts, and masses that ultrasound cannot reach, while endoscopy looks directly at the lining of the esophagus, the stomach, and the colon and can take a sample.

Where no laboratory and no imaging exist, the diagnostic task narrows to one decision: can this patient be watched here, or must this patient travel. The clinic has three instruments that never fail, which are the history, the repeated examination, and the observation of the patient over time, and they are used more thoroughly rather than less. A patient with a soft abdomen, stable vital signs, a pain that is settling, and a plan for review within hours may be watched, provided the patient can return and the clinician writes down what should prompt a return sooner. A patient with a rigid abdomen, a falling pressure, a rising pulse, vomiting of blood, a pain severe beyond the appearance of the abdomen, or a fever with yellowing and confusion must travel, and that decision should be made early rather than after a second examination confirms what the first already suggested. The clinician should not be reassured by a normal temperature in an older or immune-suppressed patient, because the response may be blunted while the disease advances, and pain relief may make the abdomen look calmer without changing the disease behind it.

A constructed illustration may fix these habits, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a man of about 50 who has drunk heavily for many years, who arrives with vomiting of fresh blood and black stool, and whose palms are red and whose abdomen is distended with fluid. Those details are the marks of chronic liver disease, and the source of the blood must be treated as variceal until endoscopy shows otherwise. Now imagine a woman of about 25 with pain that began around the navel and has moved to the right lower abdomen, whose pulse is 110 beats per minute and whose temperature is 38 degrees Celsius, and each of those figures must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the values that should trigger action differ with age, fitness, pregnancy, and the definitions in use. The first patient needs a unit with endoscopy, blood, and a surgeon, and the second needs a surgeon's opinion before the picture becomes obvious, and neither story should be applied to a living person without a full assessment.

The record of a digestive illness should show the thinking and not only the findings, including what was considered, what was excluded, and what would change the plan at the next review. The clinician should write down the time of each examination and what changed since the last, because the trajectory of an abdominal illness is often the most important single piece of evidence. Safety netting belongs in the record and in the conversation, since the patient must be told which symptoms should bring him back, how quickly, and where to go if the clinic is closed. The signs worth naming are a pain that becomes severe or generalized, vomiting of blood or black stool, a fever with yellowing, a swelling of the abdomen, a new confusion, and the inability to pass stool or gas.

To summarize, gastrointestinal and hepatic disease is read by pattern: the obstruction of a tube, the inflammation of a wall, the loss of continuity, the failure of the liver as a factory, and the rise in pressure that follows when blood cannot pass through it. The dangerous possibilities are named where the disease is described, among them a perforated ulcer, a variceal hemorrhage, ascending cholangitis, pancreatic necrosis, and fulminant hepatic failure, and each is paired with the finding that reveals it. Peptic disease, acute and chronic hepatitis, cirrhosis with its stigmata, pancreatitis, appendicitis, and inflammatory bowel disease with its manifestations far from the gut are all approached in the same way, from the history and the examination toward a ranked list that weighs both likelihood and danger. The same reasoning serves in a hospital where endoscopy, imaging, and surgery are at hand and in a clinic where the recognition of the abdomen that must travel is the whole diagnostic task. Every number in this chapter, whether a temperature, a pressure, an interval, a score, or a laboratory value, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. The clinician who examines again, records the change, and asks for help early will serve the patient better than the one who waits for the picture to become unmistakable.
