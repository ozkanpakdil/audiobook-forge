# Chapter 22 — Chest Pain

Chest pain forces a clinician to reason in parallel, because the history and the hunt for the lethal causes must run together, and the diagnosis cannot wait for the electrocardiogram. Most people with chest pain have a benign and self-limiting cause, while a few have a condition that will kill them within hours unless it is recognized at the first contact. Six causes deserve to be named at once, each with the finding that would reveal it. Acute coronary syndrome causes ongoing pressure or heaviness in the chest, often with sweating, nausea, or a sense of impending death, and sometimes with a fall in blood pressure or sudden breathlessness instead of pain. Aortic dissection causes abrupt, severe, tearing pain that often travels to the back between the shoulder blades, and it may produce a difference in pulse or pressure between the arms. Pulmonary embolism may appear as pleuritic pain with sudden breathlessness, or as a collapse with a raised jugular venous pressure and a swollen, tender leg.

Tension pneumothorax must be recognized by the hands and the eyes, because it can kill before any image is taken. It causes sudden one-sided pain and breathlessness, absent breath sounds and a hollow note on that side, a falling pressure, and distended neck veins as trapped air pushes the trachea away. Esophageal rupture is rare and easily dismissed, and it typically follows forceful vomiting, causing severe lower chest or upper abdominal pain with air under the skin of the neck. Pericarditis with tamponade causes pain that changes with posture and a friction rub, then a falling pressure, a rising jugular venous pressure, and muffled heart sounds as fluid compresses the heart. These six are named first because their treatment cannot wait, and because any of them may be present in a patient who looks comfortable. Nothing here replaces training, supervision, clinical judgment, or your own protocol, and every case in this chapter is a constructed illustration rather than a real patient.

Once the dangers are excluded or managed, the causes are best organized by mechanism, because that tells the clinician what each symptom ought to look like. Cardiac pain arises when heart muscle demands more oxygen than its vessels deliver, or when the pericardium, the aorta, or the heart wall is directly involved. Pulmonary pain arises from the pleura, the pulmonary vessels, or the airways, and it is usually altered by breathing. Gastrointestinal pain arises from the esophagus, the stomach, or the biliary tree, and it relates to swallowing, meals, or body position. Musculoskeletal pain arises from the chest wall, the ribs, the costal cartilages, or the spine, and it is usually reproduced by movement or by pressing the spot. A benign explanation never removes a lethal one, because the two coexist more often than teaching suggests.

The history carries most of the diagnosis in chest pain, because the discriminating features all lie in the story. The Socrates framework is a memory aid that asks about the site, the onset, the character, the radiation, the associations, the timing, and the factors that make the pain better or worse. It should be heard as a habit of completeness rather than a form to be filled in. Character is the first discriminator, and patients describe pressure, tightness, burning, tearing, stabbing, or dull. Site helps less than hoped, because the heart, the esophagus, and the stomach all send pain to the middle of the chest, and a patient may point with one finger or with a closed fist. Radiation is the second discriminator, and pain traveling to the jaw, throat, either arm, or upper abdomen moves the story toward the heart. Duration is the third, and it separates pain lasting seconds from pain lasting minutes and pain lasting hours, which are three different problems.

Triggers and relief are the remaining discriminators, and patients usually describe them accurately. Pain that arrives with exertion and leaves with rest, or with a treatment that dilates the coronary vessels, points to the heart, while pain following a meal or relieved by food or an acid-suppressing treatment points to the esophagus or stomach. Pain worse on deep breathing, coughing, or lying flat points to the pleura or pericardium, and pain worse on twisting the chest points to the wall. Pain reproduced exactly by pressing one tender spot is reassuring but not decisive, because a strained muscle and a heart attack can occur in the same person on the same day. The pattern suggested by these answers is a hypothesis, not a verdict, and the clinician should name what would make it wrong and then look for it.

The textbook pattern is not the pattern many patients produce, and waiting for the classical description misses the dangerous cases. Older people, people with diabetes, and people whose immune response is suppressed often feel myocardial infarction weakly or not at all, arriving instead with breathlessness, fatigue, confusion, or a sudden loss of consciousness. Women more often describe aching, burning, or pressure between the shoulder blades rather than crushing central pain. People who have recently had surgery, who are immobile, who are pregnant, or who have had a clot in the leg are at higher risk of a clot in the lung, and their pain is too easily attributed to the operation or the pregnancy. Severity is a poor guide, because a dissecting aorta, a torn esophagus, and a small heart attack may all be called the worst pain of a lifetime while a large one is called mild indigestion. The clinician should weigh the patient's risk more heavily than the drama of the description, and never let a good story about indigestion close the search for the six dangers.

The examination begins during the history, because color, breathing, and posture are findings in their own right. The clinician measures the pulse, the blood pressure in both arms, the respiratory rate, the temperature, and the oxygen saturation where a device exists, and looks for pallor, sweating, and distress. A fast, irregular, or slow pulse, a low or unequal pressure between the arms, and a rising respiratory rate are more informative than the pain score. The neck is inspected, where a raised jugular venous pressure suggests right heart strain or fluid compressing the heart, and the skin is examined for pallor, cyanosis, or rash. Whether the patient can speak in full sentences and lies flat comfortably measures how well the heart and lungs are coping. A pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury illustrates the reasoning but must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the figure shifts with age, pregnancy, and longstanding high blood pressure.

The cardiovascular examination answers four bedside questions. The first is whether there are signs of heart failure, which appear as a raised jugular venous pressure, crackles at the lung bases, and swollen ankles, and which make an ischemic cause much more likely. The second is whether there is a new murmur, because aortic regurgitation with severe chest pain suggests dissection, while a murmur with fever suggests infection on a valve. The third is whether the pulses are equal, since a difference between the arms or between arm and leg supports the same diagnosis. The fourth is whether the heart sounds are muffled and the pressure falls on inspiration, which with a raised jugular venous pressure points to fluid compressing the heart. The clinician also listens for a friction rub, which may come and go and may vanish when the patient holds his breath. A normal cardiovascular examination excludes none of the dangers, and the trajectory of the patient decides the next step.

The chest examination looks for asymmetry, because comparing one side with the other is the quickest way to find a collection of air or fluid. The clinician inspects for unequal movement with breathing, feels for tenderness, and percusses both sides from the top to the base. Hyperresonance with absent breath sounds on one side and a trachea pushed away is tension pneumothorax, a clinical diagnosis that must not wait for a radiograph. Dullness with reduced breath sounds at a base suggests fluid from infection, tumor, or esophageal tear, and crackles in one area suggest pneumonia or collapsed lung. A pleural rub points to inflammation that may come from infection, a clot, or a tumor. The abdomen is examined too, because pain from the stomach, esophagus, and biliary tree is felt in the chest, and upper abdominal tenderness may reveal the cause.

The legs are examined in every patient with chest pain, because a clot in the deep veins of the leg is the commonest source of a clot traveling to the lung. The clinician looks for swelling, warmth, redness, and calf tenderness, and measures both calves at the same level with a tape if one exists. A difference of 3 centimeters is often taught as significant, but that figure illustrates the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold varies with build and technique. Many patients with a clot in the lung have no leg sign at all, so a normal leg changes very little. Brown discoloration, hardened skin, and old swelling raise the probability of recurrent clot. Where the story suggests a clot, the leg examination supports the diagnosis but cannot rule it out.

The probability that chest pain is cardiac depends on age, sex, and risk factors more than on the pain itself, and this is the most important idea in the chapter. High blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, raised cholesterol, a previous heart attack, and a close relative with early heart disease all raise the probability, and the more that are present the more the balance shifts. A previous clot, recent surgery, immobility, active cancer, pregnancy, and treatment with estrogen raise the probability of a clot in the lung. A recent viral illness, a connective tissue disease, and kidney failure raise the probability of pericardial inflammation. Structured scores formalize this reasoning by combining the history, the tracing, the age, and the risk factors, and the listener should understand what they formalize rather than memorize their contents. Any score used must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the rules were derived in particular populations and their thresholds differ between countries and editions.

The electrocardiogram is the first test wherever it exists, and it should be recorded quickly, but it must be read with the patient rather than instead of him. A tracing taken during pain is far more informative than one taken afterwards. Elevation of the segment between the Q and T waves in a group of leads suggests dying muscle, depression of that segment or inversion of the T wave suggests starved muscle, and widespread depression with elevation in one lead may indicate dissection involving the coronary openings. A normal tracing does not exclude a heart attack, because changes take time to appear, and a single normal result must never be used to send a patient away. A bundle branch block, a pacemaker, or a previous infarction makes the tracing hard to read, and the clinician then needs the old tracing and a second opinion. The electrocardiogram is powerful, but its silence proves little.

The blood marker of heart muscle damage, troponin, is the second instrument in a well-resourced setting, and its logic is the logic of timing. It rises only after injury, so an early single measurement may be normal while disease is present, and the test is repeated over some hours before it is trusted. A high value means muscle has been damaged, but not why, because pericarditis, a clot in the lung, kidney failure, and a fast rhythm can all raise it. The laboratory reports its own reference range, and that range must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because values differ between laboratories, assays, and editions. A rising or falling pattern is more meaningful than a single figure, and the interval between samples must be checked locally rather than assumed. If no possible result would change the plan, the test should not be ordered.

Imaging follows when it can answer a question the history and examination have left open. A chest radiograph may show a widened mediastinum in dissection, air in the neck tissues in esophageal rupture, fluid beside the lung, or pneumonia, but it may be entirely normal in a clot, an early dissection, and a heart attack. Computed tomography, or CT, with contrast is definitive for dissection and for a clot in the lung, and it also shows an esophageal tear and pleural fluid. An ultrasound probe at the bedside can show fluid around the heart, a dilated right heart, and air in the chest while the patient is still being examined. Magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answers particular questions when other images are equivocal and is rarely first in an emergency. The clinician should decide what he expects the image to show before it is taken, so an unexpected result is recognized as a surprise.

Treatment appears here only as a decision, because this is a book about diagnosis and never about what to give or how much. In a heart attack, the decision to open the blocked vessel rests on continuing pain, the tracing, and the rise of the muscle marker, and it is wrong when the pain is clearly reproduced by the chest wall and the tracing is normal and unchanged. In dissection, the decision to operate rests on demonstrating the tear and its branches, and it is wrong when the picture is explained by a clot or an esophageal tear, which require different management. In a clot in the lung, the decision to treat rests on the probability before the test, the result, and the bleeding risk, and it is wrong when a definitive image has excluded the clot or the bleeding risk is overwhelming. In tension pneumothorax, the decision to release the trapped air is made on clinical findings alone and must not wait for a radiograph. None of these decisions should be delayed while a more certain answer is sought.

Several common and mostly benign causes deserve description, because recognizing them allows the dangerous ones to be set aside without anxiety. Costochondritis causes tenderness over the cartilages where the ribs meet the sternum, with sharp pain worse on movement and deep breathing. A strained muscle causes pain after unaccustomed effort that is worse with movement and reproduced by pressing or stretching. A cracked rib causes pain sharply worse on deep breathing and on pressing the spot, common after a fall or a vigorous cough, but the same story in an older person after a minor fall should raise the question of weakened bone. Herpes zoster begins as burning one-sided pain before any rash, and the rash later follows a single nerve and does not cross the midline. Pain referred from the spine causes a band of discomfort around the chest that is worse on movement and twisting. Panic can produce real pain with breathlessness and a rapid pulse, but it is a diagnosis reached only after the dangers have been considered.

The gastrointestinal causes deserve their own account, because they are common and two of them are dangerous. Reflux of acid produces burning behind the breastbone that is worse after meals and on lying flat and relieved by sitting up or by an acid-suppressing treatment, and it can mimic cardiac pain closely. Spasm of the esophagus produces severe pain in waves that may be relieved by a treatment that relaxes smooth muscle, and it can occur at rest. Inflammation of the gallbladder produces pain in the upper abdomen and right chest after a fatty meal, sometimes traveling to the right shoulder, with tenderness below the right rib margin. Inflammation of the pancreas produces severe upper abdominal pain traveling to the back and eased by sitting forward, usually with vomiting. Esophageal rupture is the dangerous one already described, and perforation of a peptic ulcer causes sudden severe upper abdominal and lower chest pain with a rigid abdomen. The decision to treat a gastrointestinal cause is right when the story fits and no danger is present, and wrong when it explains away pain that has the features of the heart.

The pulmonary causes are organized by the structure that is inflamed, which tells the clinician what to look for. Infection of the lung causes pain when the overlying pleura is involved, with fever, cough, and sputum, and examination may find crackles, dullness, and a rub. A tumor causes dull, persistent pain, often with weight loss, a changed cough, or blood in the sputum, and it requires imaging and time. A collapsed lung without tension causes sudden pain and breathlessness but lacks the falling pressure and shifted trachea that make the tension form so dangerous. Inflammation of the pleura without infection can follow a viral illness or a connective tissue disease and produces a rub with pain on deep breathing. A clot in the lung remains the one to keep in front of the mind, because its pain may be small, its signs absent, and its course a sudden collapse. A clear chest does not make a clot unlikely.

Dissection of the aorta deserves its own paragraph, because it is the cause most often missed and its treatment window is narrowest. The pain is usually abrupt and at its worst from the first moment, often described as tearing or ripping rather than pressure. It commonly travels as the tear extends, from the front of the chest to the back, to the abdomen, or to the legs. The revealing finding may be a difference in pressure or pulse between the arms, a new murmur of aortic regurgitation, a stroke, a lost leg pulse, or fluid around the heart, and any of these in the right story is enough to act. A chest radiograph may show a widened mediastinum, but a normal radiograph does not exclude the diagnosis, and the definitive test is a contrast study of the aorta. The clinician should measure the pressure in both arms in every patient with severe chest pain, because the difference is easy to find and easy to forget.

Inflammation of the pericardium and the fluid that can compress the heart deserve their own account, because they are common, they are treatable, and the second stage is lethal. The pain is often sharp, worse on deep breathing and lying flat, eased by sitting forward, and it may travel to the shoulder or neck. The finding is a friction rub, a scratching sound heard with the stethoscope that may be present one moment and absent the next. The tracing may show depression of the segment between the P and Q waves, which differs from the pattern of a heart attack, but the tracing alone must not be trusted. When fluid collects quickly enough to compress the heart, the pressure falls, the jugular venous pressure rises, the heart sounds become muffled, and the arterial pressure falls further on breathing in than it should. That combination is an emergency, and the decision to remove the fluid is made clinically and confirmed by bedside ultrasound.

In a hospital with a laboratory and imaging, the assessment takes a definite shape. The patient is monitored, an electrocardiogram is recorded within minutes, access to a vein is obtained, and the blood marker of muscle damage is sent with the other tests the story requires. A chest radiograph follows, and a contrast study of the aorta, an ultrasound of the heart, or a study of the lung vessels is arranged according to the leading hypothesis. The patient with continuing pain and a tracing suggesting injury is treated as an emergency and moved directly to where the blocked vessel can be opened, without waiting for the blood result. The patient with a normal tracing, a normal marker, and pain clearly reproduced by the chest wall may still need observation, because an early normal result proves very little. Discharge rests on the whole picture, the reliability of follow-up, and the patient's ability to return, rather than on one test. Any cut-off or interval must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because timings and thresholds differ between hospitals and editions.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is performed with fewer instruments, and the decision changes from which test to order to whether the patient can travel and by what means. The clinician takes the same history, examines the same system, counts the pulse and the breathing, measures the temperature, and looks at the color of the skin and the neck veins. A thermometer turns a vague sense of illness into a number that can be followed, and a torch allows the throat and skin to be examined properly. The pressure may be measured with a cuff if one exists, and the pulse in both arms should be compared with the fingers even when no cuff is available, because a difference between the arms needs no instrument at all. When a dangerous cause is suspected, the decision is not whether to treat but where the patient must go, how quickly, and whether the journey is safe. If transport is slow or unsafe, the clinician should say so, arrange the best available means, and give clear instructions about what to do if the pain worsens on the way.

The limits of a clinic without imaging must be stated plainly, because pretending otherwise harms patients. A normal chest examination cannot exclude a clot in the lung, a normal electrocardiogram cannot exclude an early heart attack, and a normal chest radiograph cannot exclude a dissection of the aorta. The clinician should therefore decide on probability and danger rather than on a normal finding, and treat a normal finding as a reason to keep thinking rather than to relax. Safety netting is the practical form of this honesty, and it means telling the patient which symptoms should bring him back, how soon, and where to go, in words he can repeat to his family. A follow-up appointment, a named person to contact, and a written plan reduce the chance that a patient with an evolving emergency is lost. Where no safe plan can be made, the patient needs a place with more tools, and the referral should be made rather than delayed.

Certain groups deserve a lower threshold for action, and the clinician should know who they are. People who have just had an operation, who are confined to bed, who have active cancer, who are pregnant or have recently given birth, and who have had a clot before are all at higher risk of a clot in the lung, and their symptoms deserve attention even when the examination is unremarkable. People with a connective tissue disease, a known aneurysm, or a family history of sudden death at a young age are at higher risk of aortic dissection. People who use cocaine or other stimulant drugs risk both coronary spasm and dissection, and the history should include that question in a way the patient can answer. People with kidney failure are at higher risk of pericardial inflammation, and people with a weakened immune system may have infection without the usual signs. The absence of these risks should not dismiss a story that otherwise fits.

To summarize, chest pain is assessed by running the history and the search for the lethal causes at the same time, with acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, esophageal rupture, and pericarditis with tamponade named and sought before anything else is settled. The causes are organized by mechanism, and the discriminating features are the character, the site, the radiation, the duration, the triggers, and the relief, together with the risk factors that change the probability before any test is ordered. The examination measures the pulse and the pressure in both arms, inspects the neck veins and the chest, listens for a rub or a murmur, and feels the legs for a clot, because these are the findings that reveal the dangers at the bedside. Every number in this chapter, whether a pressure, a rate, a temperature, a width, or an interval, is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This recording is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and whether the setting is a hospital with every resource or a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same questions order the reasoning and the same obligation remains, to find the dangerous cause early, to decide whether the patient can safely travel, and to explain honestly what remains uncertain.
