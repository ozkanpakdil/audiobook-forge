# Chapter 42 — Acute Abdomen, Trauma and Hemorrhage

The abdomen that has been injured and the abdomen that has not share a single emergency, and the first question in both is the same: is this patient bleeding inside, and if so, where. A spleen that has ruptured, a liver that has torn, a hemorrhage behind the peritoneum, a bowel that has perforated, a pelvis that has broken, a pregnancy that has burst outside the uterus, and an aneurysm that is leaking all announce themselves through that question before they announce themselves through any name. The reasoning of this chapter is therefore organized by the search for hidden blood and for peritoneal irritation rather than by a catalog of diagnoses, because the patient who is bleeding is treated before the cause of the bleeding is settled. The history and the examination are the instruments, and in this subject they are more powerful than any single test, because a reassuring result obtained early can be falsely reassuring while the patient is still losing volume. This chapter is educational material; it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. Nothing in it should be applied to a real person without a full assessment by the clinician who is actually with that person.

A short list of lethal possibilities deserves to be named at once, each with the finding that would betray it. A ruptured spleen produces pain and tenderness in the left upper abdomen, and often a pain felt at the tip of the left shoulder when blood irritates the diaphragm beneath it. A laceration of the liver tells the same story on the right side, with right upper abdominal tenderness and pain referred to the tip of the right shoulder. A hemorrhage into the space behind the peritoneum may produce almost no abdominal signs at all, and the clue is a patient who looks far worse than the soft abdomen suggests, sometimes with a faint discoloration of the flank. A perforated bowel produces a sudden severe pain and a board-like rigidity of the abdominal wall, with bowel sounds that have fallen quiet. A fractured pelvis can bleed heavily into that same hidden space, and the findings are instability when the pelvis is gently compressed, blood at the urinary opening, and swelling or bruising in the perineum. A pregnancy that has ruptured outside the uterus produces lower abdominal pain, a missed or unusual period, and the signs of internal bleeding in a woman of childbearing age. A leaking aneurysm of the abdominal aorta produces pain that reaches the back together with a pulsatile, expansile mass in the upper abdomen.

The mechanism of injury sets the probability before the patient is touched, and it is worth as much as any single sign. A collision at speed, a fall from a height, a pedestrian struck by a vehicle, a rollover, and an ejection from the vehicle all raise the likelihood of injury to solid organs and to the great vessels. The damage to the vehicle is part of the mechanism, because a deformed steering wheel or a cracked dashboard tells the clinician where the body met the machine. Whether a restraint was used changes what is likely, and a bruise running across the abdomen from a seat belt is a finding that should be looked for and recorded rather than dismissed as an incidental mark. The mechanism also has limits, because an older patient whose blood is thinned by medicine, or a patient whose spleen is enlarged by infection, can hemorrhage after an injury that would leave a healthy young person almost untouched. The mechanism raises or lowers suspicion, and it never makes or excludes a diagnosis on its own. A clinician who records the mechanism carefully, and then examines the patient rather than the story, has used it correctly.

The assessment of the injured patient follows a fixed order that exists to find the threats to life in the sequence in which they kill. The clinician first checks whether the airway is open and protected, then whether breathing is adequate on both sides of the chest, then whether the circulation is failing, then the level of consciousness, and finally exposes the whole body to look for the injuries that clothing conceals. The abdomen is examined during the circulatory step and then again at the end, because it is one of the places where bleeding hides without any external wound. The same sequence is used at the roadside, in a district clinic, and in a hospital, and only the tools that accompany each step change. A finding at any step is addressed before the next step is completed, because a patient who cannot breathe will not benefit from a perfect abdominal examination. The person who follows the order under pressure is the one who finds the quiet injuries.

Internal bleeding announces itself through the circulation before it announces itself through the abdomen, and the earliest signs are easy to miss. The pulse rises, the skin becomes cool and clammy, the extremities grow pale or mottled, and the passage of urine shrinks before the pressure does. A young and fit patient can hold the pressure at a deceptively normal level while losing a large volume, and then collapse suddenly when that compensation is exhausted, so a single normal pressure is never reassurance. A pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the point at which shock must be assumed, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the value that matters depends on the patient's usual pressure and on age and pregnancy. A pulse of 110 beats per minute deserves the same caution, and the accepted rate at which a patient is said to be tachycardic varies with age, fitness, and the reference in use, so verify it against the guideline and the protocol that govern your own service. The clinician should compare the patient with the patient an hour earlier rather than with a number written on a chart. A patient who is quietly deteriorating is the patient who is bleeding.

The abdomen that has not been injured presents the same emergency under a different story, and the first question is unchanged. Peritonitis, which is inflammation of the lining of the abdominal cavity, produces pain on the slightest movement, a tightening of the abdominal wall that the patient cannot relax on request, and pain when the examining hand is lifted away. Internal bleeding without trauma is less obvious, and its causes are few enough to be held in mind as a short list: a pregnancy outside the uterus, an aneurysm that is leaking, an ovary that has bled, a spleen enlarged by infection and then ruptured, and a tumor that has eroded a vessel. Each of these can produce a patient who looks pale and anxious, whose pulse has climbed, and whose abdomen is only vaguely tender at the first examination. The clinician who asks about the last period, about previous aneurysms, and about any recent illness has already begun the diagnosis before touching the abdomen. A soft abdomen in a patient who looks unwell is a reason to examine again rather than a reason to relax.

A pregnancy that has implanted outside the uterus is the diagnosis most often missed in a woman of childbearing age with abdominal pain, and it can kill within hours of rupture. The classical story is a missed or unusual period, pain in the lower abdomen that may begin on one side, a small amount of vaginal bleeding, and a fainting episode or a sense of dizziness on standing. The pain at the tip of the shoulder appears when blood has reached the diaphragm, and it is a sign that the bleeding has become significant rather than trivial. On examination the lower abdomen is tender, and moving the cervix produces pain that the patient feels deep inside, so the pelvic examination is performed with consent and a chaperone. The test that settles the question is a pregnancy test, and in any woman of childbearing age with lower abdominal pain it should be performed before any other explanation is accepted. A woman whose pressure is falling, or who has fainted, must travel immediately to a place where surgery is possible. The decision to operate is made on the clinical picture rather than on an imaging result that would take longer than the patient has.

A leaking aneurysm of the abdominal aorta is the second hidden hemorrhage that must be recognized at the bedside. It occurs most often in older people, particularly in men who have smoked, and the pain is typically felt in the abdomen, the back, or the flank, and may be described as boring or tearing. The finding that reveals it is a mass in the upper abdomen that expands with each pulse, and the examiner should feel for it gently and deliberately in any older patient with new abdominal or back pain. The patient may also have a falling pressure, a rapid pulse, and a fainting episode, and the combination of back pain, a pulsatile mass, and circulatory collapse is the picture that demands an immediate call for surgical help. The clinician should not delay that call in order to obtain a scan in a patient who is unstable, because the examination has already answered the question that matters most. Where the patient is stable, imaging may be used to define the extent of the disease, but it is arranged alongside the surgical decision and never in place of it.

A perforated bowel is the third emergency that presents without any external injury, and its signature is the sudden onset of severe pain together with a rigid abdominal wall. The patient often describes a moment when something gave way, and thereafter the pain is constant and made worse by the slightest movement, by coughing, or by the vibration of a footstep. The abdomen is board-like and quiet, and the normal dullness over the liver may be lost when free gas has escaped beneath the diaphragm. A chest radiograph taken with the patient upright may show that gas, but the film can be normal early in the illness, so a normal image does not exclude the diagnosis. The clinician must also keep in mind the conditions that imitate it, among them inflammation of the pancreas, which produces severe pain but a less rigid abdomen, and a stone in the kidney, which produces waves of pain without peritoneal irritation. The decision in perforation is surgical and it is urgent, because the longer the contamination continues the more dangerous the operation becomes.

A fractured pelvis is both an injury and a source of hidden hemorrhage, and the two facts must be held together. The pelvis is a ring, and a break in one part is usually accompanied by a break or a ligament injury in another, so instability is the finding that matters more than any single crack. The clinician compresses the pelvis gently and once, watching the patient's face, because repeated or forceful compression can dislodge a clot and restart the bleeding. Other findings that point to a pelvic injury are blood at the urinary opening, an inability to pass urine, bruising in the perineum or the scrotum, and a difference in the height of the legs. A rectal or vaginal examination may reveal a bone fragment, and it is performed when the mechanism or the findings suggest that the rectum or the vagina has been injured. Bleeding from a fractured pelvis collects behind the peritoneum, where it cannot be seen or felt by the hand, so the abdomen may remain soft while the patient loses a large volume. The finding that would make the diagnosis wrong is a second source of bleeding that fully explains the picture, and the search for that source continues even when the pelvis is clearly broken.

The abdominal examination in the injured patient is performed in the same fixed order as in any other patient, but the gentleness matters more because the abdominal wall is often guarding in defense. Inspection looks for distension, for bruising, for a seat belt mark, and for any wound, and the clinician should look at the back, the flanks, and the perineum as well as the front. Auscultation is performed before palpation, and the finding of quiet or absent bowel sounds supports the suspicion of peritoneal irritation, although normal sounds do not exclude it. Percussion is used gently to detect tenderness and to look for the loss of liver dullness that suggests free gas. Palpation begins away from the region that hurts and ends at the place the patient identifies as the worst, so that the patient does not tense the whole abdomen before the examination has begun. Involuntary guarding is a tightening that the patient cannot control, and rigidity is that guarding taken to a board-like extreme, so both are signs of peritoneal irritation rather than of a particular organ.

When consciousness is impaired, the patient's account of pain cannot be used, and the clinician must rely on physical findings that do not depend on speech. Distension that appears or increases, tenderness produced by the gentlest palpation, guarding that persists as the hand is lifted, and rigidity all remain interpretable. A change in the pattern of breathing, a falling pressure, a rising pulse, cool and clammy skin, and a shrinking passage of urine are signs that the body offers without words. The level of consciousness is itself a measured observation, and the Glasgow coma scale is the common way of recording it; a score of 8 or less is widely used as a point of concern, but that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the scale, its contents, and the figure that should prompt action are stated differently in different services and editions. A patient who cannot report pain must be examined more often rather than less, because the absence of a complaint is not the absence of injury. The clinician should record the findings and the time of each examination, so that a change becomes visible even when the patient cannot describe it.

In a hospital the examination is used to direct the tools, and each tool answers a question that the clinician has already formed. A bedside ultrasound examination that looks for free fluid in the abdomen and around the heart can be performed while the patient is still being examined, and it changes the pace of everything that follows when fluid is found. Computed tomography, or CT, defines injury to the spleen, the liver, the kidneys, and the pancreas, and it shows bleeding behind the peritoneum that the hand cannot reach, but it is used only when the patient is stable enough to tolerate the journey and the time. A chest radiograph is taken to look for free gas under the diaphragm, for fractured ribs, and for an injury to the diaphragm itself, and a radiograph of the pelvis is taken when the mechanism suggests a break. A pregnancy test is performed in every woman of childbearing age, because the answer changes the entire plan. The clinician who says what each test is expected to show before it is ordered will recognize a surprise for what it is, and will know when a normal result contradicts the examination rather than confirming it.

Where there is no scanner and no operating room, the same reasoning is compressed into three decisions: whether the patient can be kept safely here, how the patient should be moved, and how the bleeding that can be seen should be controlled. The clinic's equipment of a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer is enough to count the pulse and the breathing, to look at the skin and the eyes, and to measure a temperature of 38 degrees Celsius or more if fever is present, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the meaning of a temperature differs with the site of measurement and the edition of the reference being used. The torch makes the mouth, the ears, the skin, the perineum, and the urinary opening visible, and those are the places where a hidden injury is found. A watch allows the pulse and the breathing to be counted rather than estimated, and the count repeated after an interval such as 1 hour, which is offered as an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the safe interval between observations differs with the patient and the setting. A patient who is pale, restless, and becoming drowsy after an injury must begin the journey at once, even when the abdomen still feels soft and the story sounds reassuring.

Control of external bleeding is the one intervention that the clinic can perform well, and it is done with the hands before anything else. Direct pressure on the wound, applied firmly and held long enough for the clot to form, stops most bleeding, and the pressure is maintained rather than lifted repeatedly to look underneath. A wound that continues to bleed despite pressure can be packed firmly with clean material, and the packing is left in place while the patient is moved rather than removed for inspection. A limb wound that cannot be controlled by these measures may require a constriction band placed above the wound, and when one is used the time of its application is recorded and the limb is not left unwatched, because the tissue beyond the band is at risk. The pelvis, which can bleed into the hidden space behind the peritoneum, is steadied with a binder or with a sheet tied firmly around it, and the legs are kept together rather than splayed. Bleeding into the abdomen itself cannot be controlled from outside, and the only treatment for it is surgery, which is why the patient must travel rather than wait.

The way a patient is positioned and handled during transfer is part of the treatment and part of the diagnosis. The injured patient is laid flat and kept warm, because cold worsens bleeding and interferes with the body's ability to compensate for it. Nothing is given by mouth, because the patient may need an operation and because vomiting can block the airway in a person who is drowsy. A fractured limb is splinted before the journey, and the splint is checked so that it does not press on the skin or restrict the circulation. A patient with a suspected pelvic injury is moved as a single unit, with the pelvis supported from below, and a patient with an abdominal injury is moved gently, because jolting can restart bleeding and worsen pain. The receiving hospital should be told what is known before the patient arrives, including the mechanism, the findings, the trend of the pulse and the pressure, and what has already been done.

Not every injury is accidental, and the pattern of injury sometimes contains the diagnosis that the mechanism does not explain. An injury that does not fit the story, a delay in seeking help that is difficult to account for, and bruises or fractures of clearly different ages should raise the possibility of an assault or of abuse, whether the patient is a child, an older person, or a dependent adult. The clinician's duty in that situation is to protect the patient, to examine and document carefully, and to involve the person or the service responsible for safeguarding, rather than to confront the family in a way that may place the patient at further risk. The same care applies to a patient whose injuries suggest self-harm, because the physical emergency and the mental emergency are treated together. Documentation matters especially here, because a precise record of what was seen, in the patient's own words where possible, may later protect the patient as much as any treatment. These are uncomfortable thoughts, and they belong in the first assessment rather than after the third visit.

Certain patients deserve a lower threshold for concern because their bodies hide bleeding or compensate badly. A child has a smaller volume of blood and can maintain a normal pressure until the loss is severe, then deteriorate very quickly, so a pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury has a different meaning in a child, and the figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol and against the reference range for that child's age. An older patient may be taking a medicine that thins the blood or a medicine that blunts the heart's response, so a serious injury can produce few signs and a soft abdomen. A patient who is pregnant has organs displaced and an abdominal wall stretched, so pain and tenderness are shifted and muted, and the safety of both the woman and the unborn child must be weighed in every decision. A patient whose spleen is enlarged by a blood disorder or by an infection such as infectious mononucleosis can rupture it with very little force. In all of these people the decision rests on the repeated examination and on the trend rather than on any single set of findings.

The single most useful diagnostic instrument in this chapter is the repetition of the examination, performed by the same clinician and recorded in a form that allows comparison. An abdomen that is soft and only vaguely tender at the first look may become rigid and silent an hour later, and that change is the diagnosis arriving. The findings worth recording at each review are the level of consciousness, the pulse, the breathing, the pressure, the color and warmth of the skin, the passage of urine, and the abdominal signs themselves. Where the patient is observed rather than operated upon, the plan should state the interval between observations and what would end the period of watching, so that a deterioration is not met with a decision to wait again. The clinician should also record what was considered and what was set aside, because the next reader must be able to see the reasoning and not only the conclusion. Observation is not the absence of a decision; it is a decision with conditions attached, and those conditions must be written down where the next clinician can find them.

A constructed illustration may make the reasoning concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a man of middle age who was the driver in a collision and who now complains of pain in the left upper abdomen and at the tip of the left shoulder, whose pulse has risen and whose skin is cool, and in whom the abdomen is only mildly tender; the shoulder tip pain sends the clinician back to the spleen, and the journey to the operating room begins. Now imagine a young woman with lower abdominal pain, a period that is late, and a fainting episode, in whom a pregnancy test is performed before any other explanation is considered, and whose lower abdomen is tender with pain on moving the cervix. None of these people is real, and none should be read as a portrait. Each is constructed only to show how one examination finding can reorder the whole differential and change the plan within a minute.

A patient who is sent home after an injury must be given a plan that is specific enough to follow, and the clinician must be able to justify the decision to send that patient at all. The patient and the family should be told which signs should bring them back, among them increasing pain, a swollen or hardening abdomen, vomiting, a fever, dizziness on standing, and any new confusion, and they should be told how quickly to return and where to go. The record should show the examination findings, the trend of the observations, the mechanism, the reasoning that led to the plan, and the advice that was given, because a note that contains only a conclusion cannot be reviewed by anyone else. Where the patient is a child or an older person, the person who will watch them at home should hear the same advice directly rather than receive it secondhand. Follow-up should be arranged rather than promised, because many patients cannot judge their own condition and may not seek help until the illness is advanced. To summarize, the abdomen after injury and the abdomen without injury ask the same first question, which is whether the patient is bleeding inside and where that blood is collecting. The lethal causes are named at the bedside together with the finding that reveals each one: a ruptured spleen with pain at the tip of the left shoulder, a torn liver with right upper abdominal tenderness, a hemorrhage behind the peritoneum with a patient who looks worse than the abdomen suggests, a perforated bowel with a rigid and silent abdomen, a fractured pelvis with instability and blood at the urinary opening, a pregnancy ruptured outside the uterus with a late period and fainting, and a leaking aneurysm with a pulsatile expansile mass. When consciousness is impaired the clinician relies on distension, guarding, rigidity, the color of the skin, and the trend of the pulse and the pressure, and the examination is repeated because the change is the diagnosis. The same reasoning serves in a hospital with a scanner and an operating room and in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, where the decisions are whether to transport, how to position, and how to control the bleeding that can be seen. Every score, pressure, temperature, rate, volume, and interval named in this chapter is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories.
