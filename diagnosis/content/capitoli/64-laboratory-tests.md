# Chapter 64 — Laboratory Tests

The laboratory answers questions, but it cannot ask them, and a result that arrives without a question attached is only a number looking for a meaning. A test is worth sending when the clinician has already decided what each possible answer would change, because a result that changes nothing has spent money, time, and the patient's confidence for no return. The reasoning of laboratory medicine is therefore the reasoning of the bedside carried one step further: a probability is formed from the history and the examination, a test is chosen that can move that probability, and the new result is read against the patient rather than against the sheet of paper it is printed on. This chapter treats the laboratory as an extension of clinical reasoning and never as a substitute for it, and it is educational material that does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

Four dangerous results deserve to be named before any comfortable reading of a panel begins, because each announces itself through a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. A low glucose is the one laboratory value that can imitate a stroke, a seizure, or drunkenness and can be put right at once, and the finding that betrays it is a patient whose consciousness is altered, who is sweating and shaky and hungry, and whose confusion lifts when the sugar is restored. A rising creatinine speaks of a kidney that is failing, often before the patient feels anything at all, and the finding that reveals it is a shrinking passage of urine together with swelling, breathlessness, or a pressure that has begun to climb. A critical potassium, whether high or low, can stop the heart without warning, and the finding that reveals it is muscle weakness, an irregular pulse, or the tall peaked waves and the widening of the tracing on the electrocardiogram, or ECG. A positive culture in a patient who looks entirely well is the fourth, and it is dangerous not because the patient is ill but because the result invites treatment that may be unnecessary and harmful when the growth is contamination or quiet colonization rather than infection.

A test never creates a diagnosis by itself; it moves a probability from where the history and the examination left it to somewhere new, and the size of that move is what makes the test worth doing. An abnormal value may appear in a person who has no disease at all, because reference ranges are built from a chosen population and a share of healthy people will always fall outside them by chance alone. A normal value may appear in a person who is gravely ill, because a test taken too early, a disease confined to one place, or a result that fluctuates from hour to hour can all leave the paper innocent while the patient is not. The clinician who holds both possibilities in mind reads every result as a change in belief rather than as a verdict.

Every test with a continuous value, such as a glucose, a potassium, or a count of cells, must have a line drawn somewhere to separate the ordinary from the alarming, and wherever that line is drawn something is gained and something is lost. Move the line toward the healthy and the test catches more of the sick, but it also labels more healthy people as abnormal and sends them for further tests that may harm them. This is why a test used to confirm a suspicion that is already strong should be chosen differently from a test used to set aside a suspicion that is faint, and why the same result means different things in a young athlete and in a frail older patient. The exact position of such a line is a local decision, and any figure quoted in this chapter is an illustration of the reasoning rather than a rule to be carried between hospitals.

The complete blood count is the most frequently ordered test in medicine, and it repays being read part by part rather than as a single verdict. The hemoglobin concentration and the hematocrit describe how much oxygen-carrying capacity the blood holds, while the red cell count, the mean corpuscular volume, and the mean corpuscular hemoglobin describe the size and the color of the cells themselves. A low hemoglobin with small pale cells points toward iron deficiency, a loss of blood that has gone on quietly, or a diet that lacks the mineral, while a low hemoglobin with large cells points toward a lack of vitamin B twelve or folate, or toward alcohol, thyroid disease, or a marrow that is being crowded by something else. A hemoglobin of 7 grams per deciliter is often quoted as a level at which transfusion is considered, but that figure is offered here only to show the shape of the reasoning, and it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line depends on the speed of the fall, the symptoms, the heart, and the blood supply of the service.

The white cell count and its differential are read for the direction and the kind of change rather than for the total alone. A count that rises with a majority of neutrophils usually accompanies a bacterial infection, a tissue injury, a burn, or the use of steroids, while a count that rises with lymphocytes accompanies many viral illnesses and some bacterial ones. A fall in all the cell lines together is a finding that must be explained rather than watched, and a very high white cell count with immature forms, especially when the patient has bone pain, bruising, recurring infections, or an enlarged spleen, raises the possibility of leukemia, which is a cancer of the blood-forming tissue. A low white cell count may mean a marrow that is suppressed by disease, by infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, or by a medicine, and such a patient may carry a serious infection with few signs at all.

The platelet count is the part of the count that most directly changes what the clinician is willing to do next, because platelets are the first patch on a torn vessel. A falling count produces bruising without injury, small bleeding spots in the skin, bleeding from the gums or the nose, and, when the count is very low, bleeding into the gut or the brain. A platelet count below 50,000 per microliter is often described as the point at which bleeding becomes a real concern, but the number is a teaching illustration rather than a universal line and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the risk depends on why the count fell, whether there is infection or fever, and whether the patient is already bleeding. A low count may come from a marrow that is not making platelets, from a spleen that is holding them, from an immune attack on them, from sepsis, or from a medicine, and the decision about whether to treat or to watch belongs to the clinician who can see the whole picture. A raised count is more often a reaction to inflammation, bleeding, or iron deficiency than a disease in its own right, and it is followed rather than treated.

The chemistry panel is a set of questions about water, salts, sugar, and the work of the kidneys, and each of its parts is read against the patient rather than against the printed range. The sodium describes the balance of water in the body more than it describes salt itself, so a low sodium may reflect a heart that is failing, a liver that is scarred, a kidney that is leaking, a hormone disturbance, or a medicine that holds water back. A sodium below 125 millimoles per liter is often named as a level at which confusion and seizures become likely, and that figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the danger lies in how quickly the value fell and in the patient's own brain, not in the number alone. The potassium is the electrolyte that most often kills quietly, since both a high and a low value can disturb the rhythm of the heart, and the causes range from kidney failure and medicines to vomiting, diarrhea, and the breakdown of tissue after an injury.

Calcium and magnesium are measured less often and are missed more often, and both can produce dramatic symptoms at the bedside. A low calcium causes tingling around the mouth and in the hands, cramps, spasm of the muscles, and sometimes a seizure, and the examiner may bring it out by tapping the facial nerve in front of the ear or by inflating a pressure cuff on the arm and watching the hand curl. Magnesium is the quiet partner of calcium and of potassium, and a low magnesium will keep a low potassium and a low calcium from correcting until it is found, which is why the clinician who cannot explain a stubborn electrolyte problem should measure it. None of these values carries a fixed meaning apart from the patient, and the reference range issued by the laboratory must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the method of measurement changes the number that is reported.

The tests of kidney function, the urea and the creatinine, are among the most misunderstood results on any panel, because they describe a relationship between the blood and the kidney rather than a fixed property of either. The creatinine depends on how much muscle the patient carries, so a small elderly woman and a large young man can have the same value while their kidneys are working very differently. The urea is influenced by the diet, by bleeding into the gut, by steroids, and by the state of hydration, so it is read alongside the creatinine rather than on its own. An estimate of the filtering work of the kidney, usually called the estimated glomerular filtration rate, is calculated from the creatinine with age and sex, and it too is an estimate rather than a measurement, so it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local laboratory before it is used to make a decision. A creatinine that has risen sharply from the patient's own previous value, or a passage of urine that has fallen away, is a finding to act on today, because a kidney that is injured can often be saved if the cause is found early and lost if it is watched.

The liver tests are a group of measurements that answer different questions, and reading them as one result called liver function is the most common mistake made with them. The bilirubin describes the pigment that accumulates when red cells are broken down faster than the liver can process, when the liver cells are injured, or when the flow of bile is blocked, and the color of the urine helps to separate these possibilities at the bedside. The enzymes, among them alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and gamma-glutamyl transferase, are released when cells are damaged or when the bile passages are irritated, and they also come from muscle and from bone, so a raised value is not automatically a liver problem. The albumin and the prothrombin time, reported as the international normalized ratio, describe what the liver is still making, and they are the tests that speak of function rather than of injury. A patient whose bilirubin is climbing while the albumin falls and the clotting time lengthens, especially with drowsiness, bleeding, or a swollen abdomen, may be in liver failure, and that patient needs urgent help rather than an outpatient appointment.

The glucose is the value that can change a patient's condition fastest in either direction, and it is measured in every patient whose consciousness is altered. A low glucose is treated at once on the clinical picture when a reliable measurement supports it, because waiting for a laboratory result while a patient's brain is starved can leave damage that no later result can undo. The finding that would make that decision wrong is a reading from a meter that has not been checked against the patient, or a value that does not fit the picture, so the measurement is repeated and the clinical state is watched while it is repeated. A high glucose with a dry mouth, a great passage of urine, deep rapid breathing, and a fruity odor on the breath suggests ketoacidosis, and the finding of ketones in the urine or in the blood supports it. Both extremes are read with the potassium, because the treatment of a very high glucose moves potassium into the cells and can uncover a dangerous fall.

The inflammatory markers, among them the C-reactive protein, the erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and procalcitonin, are often treated as if they named a disease, and they do not. Each of them rises with inflammation from any cause, including infection, injury, surgery, cancer, and diseases of the connective tissue, and each can be normal in a patient who is seriously ill, particularly early in an infection or when the infection is walled off in one place. A normal marker therefore never sets aside a diagnosis that the history and the examination support, and a raised marker never makes a diagnosis on its own. What these tests do well is follow a trajectory: a value that falls over days in a patient who is improving says something useful, and a value that climbs in a patient who is not tells the clinician to look again. The number must always be read with the patient in front of the clinician, and its reference range must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local laboratory, because the assays and the units differ between services.

The urinalysis is the cheapest window on the inside of the body, and in a clinic with no laboratory it is often the only one. The strip is dipped in a fresh sample of urine and read at the time stated by its maker, because a strip read late will show colors that were never there, and the sample should be collected in the middle of the stream after the opening has been cleaned where that is possible. The strip reports protein, blood, glucose, ketones, nitrite, and the enzyme released by white cells, and each of these asks a different question. Blood in the urine may come from an infection, a stone, an injury, a tumor, or a glomerulus that is inflamed, and a patient with visible blood in the urine needs that bleeding explained rather than covered with an antibiotic and a follow-up that never comes.

The microscopy of the urine adds what the strip cannot see, and it is worth the few minutes it takes wherever a microscope exists. White cells and bacteria together point to infection, red cells of an odd shape point to bleeding from the glomerulus, and casts, which are little molds of the kidney tubes, may show that the disease lies inside the kidney itself. Contamination is the most common explanation for an unexpected result, especially in women, in the elderly, and in any sample that was not collected with care, so a positive result in a patient without symptoms is confirmed before it is treated. A positive nitrite together with fever, pain in the flank, vomiting, and tenderness over the kidney is a different matter, because it suggests infection of the kidney itself, which can lead to infection of the bloodstream and must be treated promptly rather than watched.

Culture is the test that names the organism, and its value depends on when and how the sample was taken far more than on the laboratory that grows it. A sample taken before any treatment gives the best chance of naming the cause, while a sample taken afterwards may grow nothing and leave the clinician guessing. Blood cultures deserve particular care: the skin must be prepared properly, the bottles must be filled with enough blood, and more than one set should be taken from different sites, because a single set that grows an organism living on the skin may be telling the clinician about the skin rather than about the blood. The interval between sets is chosen to catch a release of organisms into the bloodstream rather than to suit the ward routine, and a patient who is seriously ill is treated first while the samples are drawn rather than after.

A positive culture in a patient who looks well is the result that most often leads to unnecessary treatment, and it deserves a deliberate pause. An organism grown from the urine of a patient with no urinary symptoms, from the sputum of a patient with a long-standing cough, or from a wound that is healing quietly may be colonizing rather than invading, and treating it does not help the patient while it selects resistant organisms and exposes the patient to the harm of the medicine. The decision that makes treatment right is a growth of a likely pathogen together with signs that fit it, such as fever, local pain, pus, or a rise in the inflammatory markers, and the decision that makes treatment wrong is a growth in a patient whose story and examination point elsewhere. Where the result is doubtful, the sample is repeated and the patient is examined again rather than treated on the strength of a single report.

Some diagnoses require a test that looks for the organism itself rather than for the body's response to it, and tuberculosis is the classic example. The organism can be seen under the microscope in stained sputum, grown in culture over weeks, or detected by a method that copies its genetic material, known as the polymerase chain reaction, and each approach has its own strength and its own delay. A negative smear does not exclude the disease, particularly in a patient with HIV infection or in disease outside the lung, so the decision to treat is often made on the clinical picture, the chest radiograph, and the judgment of the clinician rather than on a single laboratory report. Antibody tests describe the body's response and can be positive from an old infection or negative in an early one, so they are read with the timing of the illness and with the local prevalence of the disease. Every one of these results is interpreted against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the tests available and the ways they are used differ between countries and between editions.

In a well-resourced hospital the danger is not too few tests but too many, and the reasoning that keeps them useful is the same reasoning used in the clinic. A panel ordered by habit produces numbers that no one has decided how to use, and an unexpected result then generates another test, and then another, while the patient waits and the original question is forgotten. The clinician who asks what would be done differently for each possible answer will send fewer tests and learn more from them, and will know which sample needs to be taken before treatment begins. Repeated measurement is part of the reasoning rather than a failure of the first test, because a value that is changing tells a story that a single value cannot, and the direction of change is often more useful than the value itself. Where the laboratory reports a value that is dangerous, the report should reach a clinician who can act on it, and every service should have a way of making that happen.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the laboratory may be a single strip, a small meter, or nothing at all, and the skill is to choose the one test that changes what happens next. A glucose measurement on a patient with altered consciousness changes everything, because a low value is corrected at once and a normal value sends the reasoning elsewhere. A urine strip in a patient with urinary symptoms or with fever and flank pain may confirm an infection that must be treated or may show blood that must be explained. A test for malaria in a febrile patient who has been in a region where it is transmitted is worth more than any other single test in that setting, and a test for HIV in a patient with weight loss, recurrent infection, or a rash that fits no other pattern opens the door to care. A pregnancy test in a woman of childbearing age with abdominal pain or bleeding changes the list of dangerous causes immediately, and it costs almost nothing.

Where no test exists at all, the reasoning does not become weaker; it becomes the only tool, and it is used with more care rather than less. The history is taken again and more slowly, the examination is repeated at intervals, and the observations are written down so that a trend can be seen. The torch makes the throat, the ears, the eyes, the skin, and the perineum visible, the thermometer turns the impression of fever into something that can be compared, and a watch turns the pulse and the breathing into measurements rather than guesses. The clinician who records these observations honestly, and who says aloud what is uncertain, has done the most important part of what a laboratory would have done.

A laboratory result is only as good as the sample that reached the laboratory, and many wrong results begin long before the machine is switched on. A sample taken from the wrong patient, labeled with the wrong name, left in the sun, delayed for hours, or drawn from an arm that was squeezed for too long can produce a value that sends the reasoning in the wrong direction. Hemolysis, which is the breaking of red cells during collection, releases potassium and other substances into the fluid and can create a critical value that the patient does not have. A result that does not fit the patient should be questioned before it is acted on, and the first step is to repeat it rather than to treat it. The clinician who writes the time of collection and the treatment already given beside the request helps the laboratory and helps the next person who reads the chart.

A constructed illustration may make the place of the laboratory concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a man brought in drowsy and sweating, whose speech is slurred and whose wife says he has been drinking, and whose glucose is found to be low; the reasoning that mattered was not the label but the measurement, and it was made in the first minutes. Now imagine a woman with a long-standing cough whose sputum grows an organism, and who has no fever, no weight loss, and no change in her cough; the finding that makes treatment the right answer would be a change in her symptoms together with the growth, and the finding that makes it wrong is a growth that repeats unchanged while she remains well. Neither story is real, and neither should be applied to a real patient without a full assessment. What the two share is the habit that makes laboratory medicine safe: the result is placed beside the patient, and the reasoning decides.

To summarize, the laboratory extends the reasoning of the bedside and never replaces it, and a test earns its place only when the clinician has decided in advance what each possible answer would change. The count is read part by part; the chemistry is read against the patient rather than against the printed range; the inflammatory markers are used to follow a trajectory and never to name a disease; the liver and kidney tests are interpreted with the age, the muscle, the diet, and the medicines of the patient in mind; the urinalysis is the cheapest window on the body and the one most easily misread; and cultures are taken before treatment and interpreted with the possibility of contamination firmly in view. The dangerous results are named where they appear, among them a low glucose, a rising creatinine, a critical potassium, and a positive culture in a patient who looks well, and each is met with the finding that would confirm or set aside its meaning. Every score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, and duration mentioned in this chapter is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A result is a question asked of the patient, and the clinician who keeps the patient in view will read it correctly more often than the one who reads only the number.
