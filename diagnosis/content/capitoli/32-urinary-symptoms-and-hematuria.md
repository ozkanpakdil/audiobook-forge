# Chapter 32 — Urinary Symptoms and Hematuria

Urinary symptoms describe a tract rather than a disease, and the reasoning they invite is a question of place and of mechanism. A patient who burns when passing urine, a patient who cannot pass urine at all, and a patient whose urine has turned the color of tea may share nothing except the organ that produced the complaint. The first task is therefore to locate the problem along a line that runs from the two kidneys, down the ureters, into the bladder, through the prostate in the man, and out along the urethra. The second task is to decide whether the blood, if blood is present, has come from the filtering tissue of the kidney or from the plumbing below it, because those two answers send the patient in different directions. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.

Three dangers deserve to be named before the ordinary reasoning begins, because each is revealed by a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. An infection that sits behind an obstruction can destroy the kidney and spill into the bloodstream, and the finding that betrays it is a feverish patient with a rigor, a tender loin, a palpable mass, or a history of stones. A patient in acute retention may be in severe pain with a distended bladder, and the danger is not the discomfort but the pressure that climbs back to the kidneys, revealed by a palpable bladder with a falling passage of urine and a blood test that is no longer normal. Visible blood in the urine is a malignancy until it has been investigated and set aside, and the finding that reveals it is a tumor seen when the inside of the bladder is inspected or when the kidneys are imaged.

The urinary tract is best pictured as a tree of tubes with a reservoir at the base, and the symptoms name the level at which the trouble lies. Pain that begins in the loin and travels around the flank toward the groin belongs above the bladder, in the kidney or the ureter, and it is felt as a deep ache or as waves of colic. Frequency, urgency, and pain in the lower abdomen belong to the bladder, and burning at the moment of passing urine belongs to the bladder or to the urethra. In the man, hesitancy, a stream that starts slowly and stops weakly, terminal dribbling, and a sense of incomplete emptying belong to the prostate and the bladder outlet. Incontinence describes a failure of storage or of control and must be classified before it is explained, because the same wet patient may have a full bladder, an irritable bladder, a weak sphincter, or a body that cannot reach a toilet in time.

The history of a urinary complaint is taken with the Oldcarts framework, which keeps the account complete without turning it into an interrogation. The clinician asks when the symptom began, how it has behaved since, what makes it better or worse, and what the patient has already tried. The color of the urine is asked about explicitly and in the patient's own words, because red, pink, smoky, tea-colored, and the dark brown of cola describe different mechanisms, and because a patient may not volunteer a change that seems embarrassing or unimportant. The timing of the blood within the stream matters, since blood at the very start points to the urethra, blood throughout the stream points higher, and blood only at the end points to the bladder neck or the prostate. Fever, rigors, weight loss, night sweats, a change in bowel habit, a previous stone, a previous catheter, and every medicine the patient takes belong in the same account, because each of them changes the list of likely causes.

Frequency means the bladder empties too often, and it means little until the volume passed each time is known. A patient who passes small amounts many times has an irritable or a reduced bladder, while a patient who passes large amounts often has a problem of volume rather than of storage, and the two suggest entirely different causes, among them diabetes and a kidney that cannot concentrate the urine. Dysuria, the pain or burning on passing urine, is most often caused by infection, but it also accompanies stones, tumors, and the inflammation of the bladder that follows pelvic treatment. Urgency is the sudden and compelling need to pass urine, and when it comes with frequency and pain it makes infection likely, while when it stands alone in an older person it may be the only sign of a bladder that has become unstable. Nocturia, the need to wake and pass urine at night, is a symptom of the aging bladder, of heart failure, of diabetes, and of a prostate that does not empty, and it deserves the same careful questioning as any pain.

A urinary infection is the most common explanation for frequency, urgency, and dysuria in an otherwise healthy young woman, and the reasoning changes when the infection moves above the bladder or meets a blocked system. Infection of the bladder produces pain, frequency, and a cloudy or offensive urine, and it rarely produces fever. Infection of the kidney produces fever, a rigor, pain in the loin, vomiting, and tenderness when the kidney is pressed or struck, and this is a different illness with a different tempo. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is often taught as the line above which fever is present, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold shifts with the route of measurement, the age of the patient, and the edition being consulted. The features that move infection up the list are pregnancy, a stone, a catheter, diabetes, a suppressed immune defense, a congenital abnormality of the tract, because each makes the infection more likely to spread or to recur.

A stone in the ureter produces one of the most distinctive pains in medicine, and it also produces one of the most dangerous mimics. The pain of colic begins in the loin and moves downward and forward toward the groin, comes in waves that make the patient restless, and is often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and blood in the urine that may be visible only under the microscope. A stone larger than 5 millimeters is less likely to pass on its own, but that size is offered only as an illustration and should be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the measurement that matters depends on where the stone sits and on the patient. An aneurysm of the abdominal aorta that begins to leak can produce pain in the flank or the loin that is mistaken for colic, and the findings that betray it are a pulsatile mass, a falling pressure, and a pain that does not match the urinary story. The clinician who assumes a stone in an older patient with a first episode of this pain has made a lethal assumption.

Voiding symptoms in the man describe the outflow, and the examination that answers them is the rectal examination. A prostate that is enlarged, smooth, and firm is the ordinary enlargement of age, and it explains hesitancy, a weak stream, terminal dribbling, and a bladder that does not empty. A prostate that is hard, irregular, or fixed, with a nodule that feels like a pebble within the gland, raises the possibility of cancer, and the finding that confirms it is a sample of tissue taken from the gland. The blood test that measures a prostate protein is a common source of confusion, because it is raised by enlargement, by infection, by recent catheterization, and by cancer alike, so a single raised value does not diagnose the disease. The clinician must therefore treat the test as one piece of evidence, set its interpretation beside the current guideline and the local protocol, and place it beside the rectal findings and the symptom story before drawing a conclusion.

Acute retention is a patient who wants to pass urine and cannot, and it is a urological emergency recognized by the symptom and the examination rather than by any test. The lower abdomen is uncomfortable and full, the bladder is palpable and dull when tapped, and the patient may be agitated, sweating, and in obvious distress. Chronic retention has a quieter face, and it may present with overflow, in which the patient dribbles constantly and the bladder is never empty, or with a gradual failure of the kidneys discovered only when the blood is tested. A pulse of 110 beats per minute in a blocked and infected system is a warning rather than a diagnosis, and that rate must be read against the current guideline and the local protocol, because what is fast for one patient is ordinary for another. The finding that changes the urgency of either picture is a kidney swollen by back pressure, a blood test that is no longer normal, a high potassium, or signs of infection behind the obstruction, because these mean the system is both blocked and threatened.

Incontinence is not one condition but several, and the history separates them with a few simple observations. Stress incontinence is the leakage of a small amount of urine on coughing, laughing, lifting, or sneezing, and it points to a sphincter or a pelvic floor that has lost its support. Urge incontinence is the loss of urine that follows a sudden and overwhelming desire to pass it, and it points to a bladder that contracts without warning. Overflow incontinence is the constant dribbling of a bladder that is always full, and it points to obstruction or to a bladder that has lost its nerve supply. A clinician who assumes that an older person wets the bed because of age, and who does not examine the abdomen, the perineum, and the nervous system, will miss the retained bladder, the pelvic mass, and the spinal cord problem that would have changed the management completely.

The most dangerous cause of a change in bladder control is compression of the spinal cord or of the nerves that leave its lower end, and it must be sought in any patient who suddenly cannot pass urine or becomes incontinent. The findings that reveal it are pain in the back, weakness or numbness in both legs, and a loss of sensation around the saddle area between the legs, together with a bladder that is painless and full. The loss of the ability to pass urine may come before the weakness, which is why it is never dismissed as a urinary problem alone. This is a surgical emergency because the damage becomes permanent with time, and the patient examined early is the one who may recover. A clinician who treats such a patient for a urinary infection while the legs are growing weak has misread the whole problem.

Blood in the urine is the finding that most changes the reasoning, and it must first be confirmed and then classified. Visible blood is blood the patient or the clinician can see, and non-visible blood is blood found only when the sample is tested. Before any of this is accepted, the clinician should establish that the color really is blood, because beets, blackberries, and certain medicines can color the urine red or brown without any bleeding at all. In women, blood may come from the menstrual flow, and a sample collected during a period can mislead the whole assessment. A dipstick that reports blood is not a diagnosis of bleeding, and a positive result must be confirmed by looking at the urine under the microscope before a patient is sent down a long and anxious path.

Once blood is confirmed, the next question is whether it came through the filter or from the plumbing, and several clues separate the two. Glomerular bleeding tends to make the urine brown, smoky, or the color of tea rather than bright red, and it usually does not produce clots. It is commonly accompanied by protein in the urine, by red cells that are deformed under the microscope, and by casts, which are small plugs of material that form in the tubules of the kidney. The patient may also have swelling around the eyes in the morning, a raised pressure, a rash, joint pains, or a recent sore throat or skin infection, and any of these points to the kidney. Urological bleeding is more often bright red, may contain clots, and may come with colic, obstruction, or the symptoms of a bladder infection. Inflammation of the filtering tissue is a serious diagnosis, and one form of it can destroy kidney function quickly, so a patient with these features needs a specialist and often a hospital.

Malignancy of the kidney, the bladder, or the prostate can all present with blood in the urine, and the cruel feature of that presentation is how well the patient often looks. A tumor of the bladder typically causes visible blood that comes and goes and is not painful, and the bleeding may stop for a time and seem to have resolved. A tumor of the kidney may cause blood, pain in the loin, and a mass that can be felt, but any one of the three may be the only sign. Cancer of the prostate may announce itself through a hard gland on rectal examination rather than through blood, and blood may never appear at all. The findings that reveal these tumors are the direct inspection of the bladder through a thin telescope, the imaging of the kidneys, and the sample of tissue, and none of them can be replaced by a normal dipstick result. The rule that must be spoken plainly is that visible blood in the urine is treated as a malignancy until the appropriate investigation has shown that it is not.

Blood that is found only on testing is a different problem, and its meaning depends heavily on the patient. In a young person after vigorous exercise, during an infection, or at the time of a period, it may mean nothing, and a repeat test once the provoking factor has passed is the sensible first step. In an older person, in a smoker, in someone who has worked with certain industrial chemicals, and in a patient with a history of stones or of pelvic treatment, the same finding may be the first evidence of a tumor. The decision is not whether blood is present but who should be investigated and how, and that decision turns on age, on risk factors, and on whether the blood persists when the test is repeated. The share of such patients who turn out to have a serious cause varies widely between populations, so any figure quoted for it belongs to the current guideline and the local protocol, because the clinics being compared are never quite the same.

Pain in the loin is not always the kidney, and the structures that share the neighborhood must be remembered. A stone produces waves of pain and blood, an infection produces fever and tenderness, and a tumor produces a dull ache with weight loss and sometimes blood. A clot that blocks the artery of the kidney produces sudden severe pain in the loin with blood in the urine, and it is easily mistaken for a stone; the patient often has a rhythm disturbance of the heart or disease of its vessels, and the diagnosis is made by imaging the artery. The leaking aneurysm, a problem in the spine, in the muscle wall of the back, or in the lower ribs can all be felt in the same place. The clinician who examines the back, the abdomen, the pulses in the groin, and the urine before settling on a kidney stone will avoid the mistakes that these shared territories create.

Obstruction of the urinary tract is a problem of pressure, and it can exist without pain or infection. In the man it is usually the prostate; in the woman it may be a pelvic mass, a prolapse, or a bladder that has lost its nerve supply; in the child it may be a congenital narrowing present since birth. The kidney under pressure loses its ability to filter, and the loss is silent until the blood is tested or until the patient becomes unwell with an infection. The finding that identifies it is an empty bladder after the patient has tried to pass urine, or a kidney that is swollen when it is imaged, together with a blood chemistry that shows what has accumulated. The clinician who treats the infection and never asks whether the system is draining will meet the same patient again with the same problem, or with a worse one.

Any patient with a catheter in the bladder has a foreign body in the tract, and the tube changes the meaning of every symptom and every test. A catheter is a common source of infection in hospital, and the longer it stays the more likely it is to produce one; the finding that suggests it is fever with tenderness over the kidney or a cloudy urine in a patient who was previously stable. A catheter can also obstruct, leak around its side, or produce bleeding from the lining of the bladder, and the clinician must check that it is draining rather than assume that it is. In a patient with a catheter who becomes confused or unwell, the tube belongs in the assessment of the source, and the decision about whether to change it is a clinical one. The clinician should also ask whether the catheter is still needed, because the best prevention is removal of a tube no longer doing useful work.

Several groups deserve a higher level of caution because the ordinary signs are altered in them. A child with a urinary infection may have fever alone, or vomiting, or simply a failure to thrive, and a young child needs a urine sample obtained properly rather than a diagnosis made on the smell of a diaper. A pregnant woman may carry bacteria in the urine without symptoms, and the risk to her and to the pregnancy is high enough that the finding is sought deliberately and treated, and the exact criteria belong to the local guideline and the obstetric protocol. An older person may present with confusion, a fall, or a refusal to eat rather than with pain or frequency, and the bladder may be full without complaint. A patient whose immune defenses are suppressed may have a severe infection with almost no fever, so a low threshold for investigation is the right posture rather than a reassuring wait.

Urethral discharge, discomfort on passing urine, and a new sexual partner belong to a different list, and the sexually transmitted infections must be named rather than avoided. Infection with gonorrhea, with chlamydia, with trichomonas, and with the viruses that cause genital herpes produce burning on passing urine and a discharge, and they may be mistaken for an ordinary bladder infection. The finding that reveals them is the organism identified in a sample from the urethra or the cervix, or in the first part of the urine, and a patient whose symptoms persist after a bladder infection has been treated deserves that search. The examination of the genitals, the skin, and the lymph nodes in the groin belongs in the assessment, and the patient should be offered testing for HIV and for other infections at the same visit. The clinician who treats the symptom and does not name the infection leaves the patient and the partner exposed to the same problem again.

In a clinic that has a stethoscope, a torch, a thermometer, and a dipstick, the urinary patient can still be sorted with skill. The history and the examination carry most of the information, and the dipstick adds a few crucial facts: whether blood is present, whether protein is present, whether the urine is concentrated, and whether infection is likely. The clinician examines the abdomen for a palpable bladder or a mass, the loin for tenderness, the genitalia for discharge or ulceration, and the nervous system for the weakness and the saddle numbness that would mean compression of the cord. A urine sample should be collected cleanly and inspected by eye for color and clarity, and the dipstick should be read within the time its maker specifies, since a result read too late is worthless. Where no laboratory exists, the decision is not a diagnosis but a direction, and the finding that forces the journey to a hospital is a retained bladder, a fever with loin pain, or visible blood in the urine.

In a hospital the same reasoning is extended with tools that answer specific questions rather than with more guesswork. A urine sample is examined under the microscope and sent for culture, so that the organism is named and its sensitivity is known, and the culture is the finding that turns a guess into a decision. Ultrasound shows whether the kidneys are swollen, whether a stone is blocking the flow, and whether the bladder empties, and it is available at the bedside in many services. Computed tomography, or CT, answers questions about stones and tumors that ultrasound cannot, and magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, is used when the soft tissues of the tract and their surroundings must be seen in detail. A cystoscopy lets the clinician look directly at the lining of the bladder, which is the only way to exclude the small tumor that a scan may not show, and the urology team on call is the ally who turns a suspicion into a plan.

The decision about treatment is part of the diagnostic process, and in the urinary tract it is often delicate. Treatment for an infection is the right answer when the picture fits, when the patient is stable, and when the sample that will identify the organism has been taken first, because a culture taken after treatment has begun may never name the cause. Treatment is the wrong answer when the system is obstructed, because medicine alone cannot reach an infected kidney that is under pressure, and the finding that should change the plan is a swollen kidney, a full bladder, or a patient who is not improving. Treating blood in the urine without a diagnosis is also the wrong answer, because stopping the bleeding does not remove the tumor that caused it, and the finding that makes treatment appropriate is a cause that has been identified and named. Drainage and the relief of an obstruction are diagnostic acts as well as therapeutic ones, because the patient who improves once the pressure is relieved has confirmed the mechanism.

Two constructed illustrations may make the pattern concrete, and both are teaching devices rather than portraits of real people. The first is a young woman with burning on passing urine, frequency, and a cloudy sample, who has no fever and no pain in the loin, and in whom the decision is to treat a bladder infection on the clinical picture and to explain which features would move the problem higher. The second is an older man with a single episode of painless visible blood in the urine, no fever, and an unremarkable examination, in whom the correct next step is referral for direct inspection of the bladder rather than a repeat test and a reassuring word. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment. What the two share is that the visible symptom, burning in one and blood in the other, was placed beside the danger it might represent before the plan was made.

A clinician who sends a urinary patient home must say what should bring that patient back, and must write down what was found and what was considered. The patient should be told to return if the pain in the loin worsens, if a fever or a rigor appears, if the urine becomes visibly bloody, if the passage of urine stops or slows, or if vomiting prevents fluids from staying down. A repeat test arranged for a patient with blood found only on testing is worth more than a promise to return if things change, because the finding may clear and then mean nothing, or persist and then mean everything. The record should show the color of the urine as the patient described it, the timing of the blood within the stream, the state of the bladder after voiding, and the reasons why a more serious cause was or was not pursued. The most useful sentence in the note is often the one that names what remains uncertain and what has been arranged to settle it.

To summarize, urinary symptoms are organized first by where they arise along the tract and second by whether the blood comes from the filter or from the plumbing, and those two questions decide most of what follows. The dangers are named at the bedside and not deferred: the infected system that is obstructed, the retention that is quietly damaging the kidneys, the compression of the cord that presents as a bladder problem, and the visible blood that is a malignancy until it has been investigated. The history, taken with the Oldcarts framework, and the examination, which includes the abdomen, the loin, the genitalia, the back, and the nervous system, carry more of the answer than any single test. Every figure in this chapter, whether a temperature, a rate, a size, or an interval, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient who cannot pass urine, who is feverish with a tender loin, or who is losing blood visibly is helped first and investigated at the same time, because the diagnosis can wait and the patient cannot.
