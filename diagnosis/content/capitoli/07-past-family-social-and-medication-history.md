# Chapter 7 — Past, Family, Social and Medication History

The first half of the history explains why this patient has come today; the second half explains why this patient might have this illness at all. Past illnesses and operations, the family pattern, work and its exposures, travel, animals, the sexual history, alcohol, tobacco, allergies, and the medicines actually taken set probabilities that no examination can supply. A constructed illustration shows the safety at stake. A patient who takes an anticoagulant and reports a headache may be bleeding inside the head, and the findings that reveal it are a new weakness, a change in speech, or a fall in the level of consciousness, none of which can be read without the medication history.

This part of the history is the one most often skipped, and the reason is structural rather than lazy. The presenting complaint is vivid, the examination has a visible end, and the past history sits where fatigue is greatest. A patient asked what he was in hospital for, what was removed, and what the doctor said will tell a story, while a form produces a blank. A previous event then changes the probability of a future one in three ways: a disease may recur, as a second heart attack or a second episode of a mood disorder; a disease may leave a residue that is itself the new problem, as a damaged valve leaves the patient vulnerable to infection of the valve; and the treatment of a past disease may cause the present complaint, as surgery leaves adhesions that can obstruct the bowel.

The past history should be opened with questions about events rather than diagnoses. Asking whether the patient has ever been in hospital, and what for, reaches further than asking about medical problems, because patients do not count a past admission or a broken bone as a medical problem. When a diagnosis is named, the clinician should ask how it was discovered, since labels are often assigned without explanation and then carried for years. A label of ulcer, of asthma, or of anxiety may have been a guess never tested.

What matters about a past illness is its severity, its treatment, and its ending. An admission to the intensive care unit, or ICU, marks a serious event even when the diagnosis has been forgotten. A transfusion in the past raises questions about blood-borne infections, and the tests available then were not the tests available now. A childhood illness that kept the patient in bed for weeks may have been rheumatic fever, which can leave a damaged valve, or tuberculosis, which can reactivate.

Operations deserve careful reconstruction, because a scar is a document. Was anything found that the patient was not told about, and did the wound heal slowly? A previous abdominal operation makes adhesions a leading explanation for colicky pain with vomiting and a distended abdomen, and removal of the spleen raises the lifetime risk of overwhelming infection. The clinician should look at the abdomen while listening, because the scars often correct the account.

A history of tuberculosis is among the most important items in the interview, because the disease can reactivate years later, particularly when immunity falls, as in untreated infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, or HIV. The findings that should raise suspicion are a cough lasting 3 weeks, weight loss, night sweats, or blood in the sputum. The interval that should prompt investigation is not the same everywhere, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than trusting a remembered figure. Household contact with a person who had tuberculosis should also be asked about, and childhood illnesses such as measles and chickenpox are usually remembered well enough to date.

The family history is not a form to be filled with yes and no; it is a pattern drawn in words. The questions that generate it are the age at which each relative became ill, the age at which each relative died, and the cause of death as the family explained it. A condition appearing at an unusually young age in a close relative carries more weight than the same condition late in a distant one. Consanguinity should be asked about plainly, because it raises the probability of recessive disease in a way that nothing else does.

The pattern that most changes management is sudden death in a young relative, because some inherited conditions that cause it can be found in the living. A young person who died suddenly, a relative who drowned unexpectedly, or a family story of a death in the night may point to an inherited disease of the heart muscle or of the heart's electrical system. The findings that reveal it are fainting during exertion, a murmur, an abnormal electrocardiogram, or ECG, and a family tree that repeats the pattern. Such a history is not a curiosity, because the risk may extend to the patient and to the patient's children.

Cancer in the family shifts the differential, but only when its details are known. Cancer of the bowel or of the womb in a close relative at a young age, or cancer of the breast or ovary in several relatives, raises the probability of an inherited susceptibility. Cancer of the lung in a relative who smoked carries less weight than the same cancer in a relative who did not, because the exposure explains the pattern. The clinician should ask about the site rather than the word cancer, since many families use the word loosely.

Family history also reaches conditions whose inheritance is diffuse rather than tidy, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke, asthma, autoimmune disease, mental illness and kidney disease, each of which raises the prior probability in the patient. A patient with a strong family history of diabetes who presents with thirst and weight loss is a different diagnostic problem from the same patient without that history. It must also allow the patient to say that he or she does not know, because adoption, migration, estrangement, and early death are common, and a question that assumes a full family tree produces invented answers or silence. Some families conceal mental illness, suicide, or a death from infection because of stigma, so the clinician should ask without moral weight and without surprise.

Occupation is asked about to identify what the patient has breathed, touched, and carried home. A builder may have worked with asbestos, a farmer with pesticides, a painter with solvents, and a miner with silica, so the clinician should ask what the work involved, which dusts or fumes were present, what protective equipment was used, and whether the patient came home in work clothes. The dangerous cause this uncovers is malignant disease of the lining of the lung, which can appear many years after exposure to asbestos, and the findings that reveal it are a pleural effusion, a persistent cough, or a shadow on a chest radiograph. An interval of 10 years or more between exposure and disease is common, and such intervals differ between exposures, populations, and editions, so they must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before they are used to include or exclude anything.

Occupational disease often declares itself by its pattern rather than its presence. Asthma that improves on days away from work and returns on return to work is a different diagnosis from asthma that is constant, and the same is true of a rash on the hands that clears during a holiday. The clinician should ask what the symptoms do at the weekend, during leave, and after a change of job. A patient whose breathlessness began soon after starting a new process at work deserves that question, and the answer is almost never volunteered.

Work also exposes the clinician, and the history of exposures is the moment to think about protection. A patient with a cough, a rash, or a wound may transmit an infection to the person examining, and personal protective equipment, or PPE, belongs to the diagnostic encounter rather than to administration. The history should include needlestick injuries, contact with blood, work in laboratories, and the care of people who are unwell. Where a needlestick has occurred, the questions about the source and the timing belong to the local protocol, which must be consulted rather than reconstructed from memory.

Travel and residence are asked about because geography changes the list of possible diseases more than any single symptom does. Where has the patient lived, where has the patient traveled, and when did the patient return? A fever in a patient who has returned from a region where malaria is transmitted is a different emergency from a fever in a patient who has not left the city, and the finding that reveals malaria is the parasite seen on a blood film, or a rapid test where no microscope is available. The clinician who attributes such a fever to a cold without asking about travel has removed the most dangerous diagnosis from the list.

Residence matters beyond travel, because where a person lives determines the water, the air, the vectors, and the infections that circulate. A patient who lives near standing water, in a crowded dwelling, or where tuberculosis is common carries a different set of risks. The clinician should ask how many people share the home, whether there is running water, and whether anyone else in the household has been ill. Clusters of illness in a household are a clue that no individual examination can provide.

Recent travel also changes the probable cause of a fever and the probable resistance of an infection. A patient who has been in a hospital abroad may carry organisms resistant to the medicines available at home, and a patient who has been in a region where a virus that causes bleeding is transmitted may present with a temperature of 38 degrees Celsius or higher, a rash, and bleeding from the gums. What counts as a fever is not identical across services and ages, so treat the figure as an illustration of reasoning and check the definition used in your own setting against the current guideline and the local protocol. The findings to look for are bleeding out of proportion to any injury, a rash of small spots that do not fade under pressure, and a low platelet count where a laboratory exists.

Animal contact is sometimes decisive. The clinician should ask about pets, livestock, rodents, bats, birds, reptiles, and ticks, and about bites, scratches, and contact with animal fluids. Rabies is the dangerous possibility, and it is almost always fatal once symptoms begin, so the finding that must be sought is a bite or scratch from an animal, however trivial it seemed, together with agitation, difficulty swallowing, or an aversion to water. Other animal-linked infections are common in farming communities and are easily missed, and contact with sheep and goats can transmit brucellosis, which presents with a fever that comes and goes, sweats, and joint pain. Contact with cattle, rodents, or flood water can transmit leptospirosis, which causes fever, muscle pain, and red eyes and can progress to jaundice and kidney failure, while cats that hunt can transmit a bacterium that causes swollen lymph nodes.

The sexual history is a diagnostic instrument, and it is treated as a moral one only by mistake. It establishes exposure to infections that change the whole picture, identifies a pregnancy that may not yet be known, and opens the way for the patient to disclose violence or coercion. The clinician should ask it of every adult patient rather than of those who fit a stereotype, because assumptions about who is at risk are wrong often enough to be dangerous. The finding that should prompt a sexual history in a patient who has not volunteered one is a symptom that does not fit the usual pattern, such as weight loss, a prolonged fever, oral thrush, a rash, or a lymph node that persists. HIV remains the infection that most often changes the meaning of the other findings.

Asking well requires privacy, an explanation, and a route from the general to the specific. The clinician should say why the questions are asked, promise only what can honestly be promised about confidentiality, and begin with questions that are easy to answer. Whether the patient is sexually active, whether there are partners of one sex or both, whether there has been a new partner, and whether there has been discomfort or discharge open the subject without demanding a confession. Euphemism should be avoided, because it tells the patient that the subject is shameful, and a tone of judgment should be avoided for the same reason.

The sexual history changes the differential in several directions at once. A young patient with lower abdominal pain and fever may have an infection of the pelvic organs, and the same pain with a missed period and fainting may be a pregnancy outside the womb, a lethal emergency whose revealing findings are abdominal tenderness, a positive pregnancy test where one is available, and signs of internal bleeding. Syphilis can imitate almost any disease and may be found when the only sign is a painless ulcer or a rash on the palms. An infection of the cervix may cause no symptoms while it damages fertility, and infection with the human papillomavirus may lead to cancer of the cervix years later.

The sexual history is also where violence and coercion most often surface, and the clinician must be prepared for what the answer may bring. A patient whose injuries do not match the account, whose partner answers for them, or who is reluctant to be examined alone may be living with violence at home. The clinician should ask directly and privately, without a companion in the room, and should not press for a disclosure the patient is not ready to make. Where a disclosure comes, the response is governed by local law, local protocol, and the patient's safety, and the clinician should know those rules in advance.

Alcohol is asked about because it causes disease directly, because its withdrawal can be lethal, and because it interacts with almost everything else in the history, so the clinician should ask about the pattern rather than the total: whether the patient drinks most days, whether drinking has increased, and whether anyone has expressed concern. The dangerous complications are bleeding from veins in the food pipe, a seizure during withdrawal, confusion with shaking, liver damage, and injuries the patient does not remember. The finding that reveals advanced liver disease is jaundice, bruising, a firm liver, or fluid in the abdomen, and the finding that reveals withdrawal is a coarse tremor, sweating, and a rising pulse. A pulse of 110 beats per minute in this setting is suggestive rather than diagnostic, and the rate that should alarm you depends on the patient's age, fitness, and other illness, so it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Asking about alcohol without accusation is a skill that can be learned. A question about how much the patient drinks in a usual week invites a defensive answer, whereas a question about the occasions and the pattern invites a description. The clinician should normalise the subject by asking it of everyone and should avoid reacting with either approval or alarm. Where the patient minimizes, the clinician should not argue, because argument ends the history, and the subject should be returned to later through the effects of drinking on sleep, work, and family.

Tobacco changes the probability of cancer, of disease of the heart and blood vessels, of lung disease, and of poor wound healing. The clinician should ask whether the patient smokes, has smoked, or has stopped, what form the tobacco takes, and whether others smoke in the home. The details that matter most are the current pattern and the total years of exposure, because risk accumulates with both. The finding that should raise the possibility of lung cancer is blood in the sputum, a cough that has changed, weight loss, or a shadow on a chest radiograph in a patient with a smoking history.

Other substances complete the picture, and they are asked about in the same matter-of-fact way. Cannabis, opioids, stimulants, inhalants, khat, and medicines taken without a prescription all have diagnostic consequences, from infections introduced by injection to abnormal heart rhythms, from sedation to agitation. The dangerous consequence most often missed is infection of the heart valves in a person who injects drugs, and the findings that reveal it are a new murmur, a fever, and signs of material carried in the blood to the lungs or the brain. The clinician should ask about the route of use, the sharing of equipment, and the last time the substance was used, because withdrawal can mimic many other conditions.

Withdrawal is both a diagnosis and a trap. A patient who is agitated, sweating, and tremulous a day or two after the last drink may be diagnosed as anxious or infected, when the true explanation is withdrawal and the risk of a seizure. A patient who is drowsy after a period of heavy use may be withdrawing from a sedative or may have taken too much, and the two demand different actions. The clinician should ask when the substance was last used and whether the patient has ever had a seizure or confusion during a previous attempt to stop, because a falling level of consciousness, which the Glasgow coma scale can describe, is always a reason to stop the interview and act.

The clinician should ask what the patient reacted to, what the reaction was, how soon after the exposure it began, and how it was treated, because those details separate a true immune reaction from a side effect, an intolerance, or a coincidence. A rash appearing after a medicine is a different problem from swelling of the lips within minutes of eating a food, and only one predicts a life-threatening reaction. A severe allergic reaction is the lethal possibility, and the findings that reveal it are swelling of the tongue or throat, wheeze, a widespread rash, a systolic pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury or lower, and a history of a similar reaction. That pressure is an illustration only, because the level that defines shock varies with age and with the patient's usual pressure, so it must be read against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The allergy label carries consequences far beyond the note where it is written. A patient recorded as allergic to an antibiotic may be denied the most effective treatment and given a broader or more toxic alternative, and a patient recorded as allergic to a pain medicine may be left with untreated pain. The clinician should record the specific drug, the reaction, the timing, and the route, and should question a label with no detail behind it, because a history of intolerance such as nausea is not the same as allergy. The finding that would make treatment with the drug the right answer is a careful assessment that the original reaction was not immune, and the finding that would make it wrong is a documented reaction of the severe kind.

The medicines the patient actually takes are often different from the medicines on the list, and the difference is where errors live. The clinician should ask the patient to bring every container, including over-the-counter medicines and traditional remedies as well as the ones prescribed long ago, and should open each rather than trust the label. For every medicine the clinician should establish who prescribed it, what it is for, whether it is still taken, and what happens when a dose is missed. The most important question is often the one never asked, which is what has been stopped and why, because a medicine abandoned for a side effect, a cost, or a belief that it was no longer needed is the key to the present complaint.

Adherence is probed without accusation, because accusation produces concealment. The clinician should begin from the assumption that missing doses is normal and should ask about the last few days rather than the general habit. Questions about cost, transport to the pharmacy, the number of medicines, and difficulty opening containers will find more truth than a question about whether the patient takes the treatment properly. Where a medicine must reach a certain level to work, the finding that it is not being taken is the disease that remains uncontrolled, and the level that defines control belongs to the current guideline and the local protocol, because it differs between services and populations.

A medicine can cause the very symptom that brought the patient in. A cough beginning after a medicine for blood pressure, a rash after a new prescription, a fall in mood, a tremor, or confusion can all be adverse effects, and the timing of the start is the clue. The clinician should establish when each medicine was started in relation to the symptom and should consider whether stopping it, under supervision, changes the picture. The reasoning is the same as for any hypothesis: predict what should happen if the medicine is responsible, then observe whether it does, and never change treatment casually, since the decision to stop, switch, or continue belongs to the clinician who knows the patient.

In a well-resourced hospital the record usually arrives before the patient does, and this advantage carries its own danger. The previous notes contain a diagnosis, a list of medicines, and a set of labels, and the clinician who reads them before meeting the patient may adopt the old hypothesis without testing it. The record should be read with the patient present, so that each entry can be confirmed, questioned, or corrected. A label of anxiety written years ago can hide disease of the heart or thyroid, and a drug allergy recorded without detail can steer treatment away from the best option for years.

The hospital also allows sources that disagree to be reconciled. The patient's account, the family's account, the referral letter, the pharmacy record, and the old notes can be laid side by side, and the differences between them are often more informative than their agreements. Where accounts conflict, the clinician should not assume that the patient is unreliable, because the family may be remembering a different episode and the letter may describe an earlier stage. A family member can supply dates the patient has forgotten, but the patient remains the person whose consent governs what is discussed.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a thermometer, and a torch, the patient is the record, and the whole of the file is what the clinician writes at the end of the visit. Nothing can be checked against a previous result, so the history must be taken more carefully rather than less, and the patient's recollection becomes the only source for the past. The clinician should write the medicines using the patient's own description, including the color of the tablet and the shape of the container, because the name may be unknown or misremembered. A torn prescription or an old card is worth more than any memory, so the patient should be asked to bring the containers and any documents to the next visit, and should be told why.

The clinician in that setting must believe the patient, while remembering that belief is not certainty. A patient who says the illness began after a specific event, or that a food or medicine caused it, is usually reporting a real observation even when the explanation is wrong, and the clinician's task is to keep the observation and to test the explanation. Because there is no laboratory to fall back on, the plan must include a clear safety net of what to watch for, where to go if it appears, and when to return. Time is limited everywhere, so the second half of the history must be prioritized rather than omitted, and the questions that earn their place are the ones whose answers would change what the clinician does: a medicine that could be causing the symptom, an exposure that points to a dangerous infection, a family pattern that demands an electrocardiogram, a sexual history that opens the possibility of an emergency. A history that is incomplete but directed is safer than one that is complete but shapeless.

The whole of this chapter can be carried in a single habit: for every fact the patient offers about the past, ask what it does to the probability of the diseases now being considered. A past illness, a family death, a job, a journey, an animal, a partner, a drink, a cigarette, a remedy, or a bottle of tablets is not background, because each moves the ranking of the differential. The clinician who asks these questions only to complete a form will record the answers and learn nothing, while the clinician who asks them as hypotheses will find the differential rearranging itself during the interview. What is written down should show that rearrangement, which is why Soap notes remain a useful habit for keeping the subjective, the objective, the assessment, and the plan in a form another person can follow.

To summarize, the past, the family, the work, the journey, the animals, the sexual history, the substances, the allergies, and the medicines actually taken set the probabilities for everything that follows. They are asked not to complete a record but to change the differential, and each answer should be carried into the examination as a hypothesis to be tested. The dangerous possibilities named here, from bleeding in the head in a patient who takes an anticoagulant, to malaria in a returning traveller, to bleeding from the veins of the food pipe, are found by asking before they are found by examining. This chapter is educational material; it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or local protocol, and no patient described here is real. Where the patient is deteriorating, the interview stops and the patient is treated, because the safest history is the one that leaves time to act.
