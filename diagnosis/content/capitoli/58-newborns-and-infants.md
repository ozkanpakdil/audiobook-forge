# Chapter 58 — Newborns and Infants

The newborn brings no account of himself, so the whole history must come from the mother, the birth, and the body in front of the clinician. He cannot say where it hurts, cannot describe a change over days, and cannot cooperate with an examination that depends on answers. The reasoning rests instead on observation, on what happened before and around the birth, and on a few signs that mean serious illness. This chapter treats the newborn and the young infant as one patient with a language of his own. That language is spoken through the cry, the tone, the feeding, the temperature, the breathing, the color, the weight, and the jaundice. The first task is not to name a disease but to decide whether the baby is well, unwell in a way that can be watched, or unwell in a way that will kill him before morning. The material is educational and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the baby is seen.

Several lethal possibilities must be named before any comfortable explanation is entertained, because each is revealed by a finding sought in the first minutes. A baby who feeds poorly, feels cold, breathes with effort, and has become floppy may have an infection of the bloodstream. The alarm is a baby who is not behaving as he did yesterday, not any single number on the thermometer. A baby who collapses after feeding, pale and mottled, with pulses that cannot be felt in the groin, may have a congenital heart lesion. The weak groin pulse is the finding, and the lesion declares itself as the vessel that kept the circulation open begins to close. A baby who vomits green fluid and has a swollen abdomen may have a bowel obstruction, and a twist of the gut on its own blood supply is lethal. A baby who was well and then refuses feeds and becomes drowsy may have a metabolic disease, and a jaundice that is early or persistent must not wait for the next visit.

The history of a newborn is the history of his mother and of the pregnancy, and it is taken as carefully as any adult history. The clinician asks about infections during pregnancy, among them syphilis, HIV, hepatitis, and infection with group B streptococcus. Diabetes, high blood pressure, medicines, traditional remedies, smoking, alcohol, and drugs belong in the same conversation. The birth supplies its own account: how labor began, how long the membranes were ruptured, whether the fluid was stained with meconium, whether the mother had a fever, and whether instruments were used. The baby's story since birth follows: how he fed in the first hours, whether he has passed urine and meconium, how often he feeds now, and whether his color has changed. The family history matters more here than in adult medicine, since inherited diseases of the blood, of metabolism, and of the heart may declare themselves in the first days.

The Apgar score formalizes the baby's condition in the first minutes after birth, and it is a starting point for observation rather than a diagnosis. It records five things the examiner can see and feel: the color, the heart rate, the response to a mild stimulus, the muscle tone, and the effort of breathing. The reasoning it organizes is simply whether the baby is making the transition to life outside the womb. The score is usually recorded at 1 minute and again at 5 minutes, and those intervals are illustrations that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since practice differs between units and editions. A low score directs the clinician to help the baby breathe and to keep him warm, and it says nothing by itself about the cause or the future. The score must never be used to diagnose asphyxia or to predict how a child will develop, because it was designed for neither purpose.

The signs that must be recognized without any history are few, and they are learned by looking at the baby rather than at a monitor. A baby who is unusually quiet, who does not wake for feeds, who lies with his limbs extended rather than drawn up, or who feels limp when lifted is not merely sleepy. The work of breathing is the most visible signal: a rising rate, a grunt at the end of each breath, flaring of the nostrils, and drawing in of the ribs all mean the baby is using accessory muscles. Color is read in good light on the lips and tongue rather than on the hands and feet, because the extremities of a newborn are often blue while the center is well. The mother's own impression that something is wrong is a clinical finding, and it should be recorded and taken seriously rather than set aside.

The newborn loses heat quickly and defends his temperature poorly, so the thermometer measures two dangers at once. An ordinary temperature lies somewhere between 36.5 and 37.5 degrees Celsius when measured by an appropriate method. That range is a teaching illustration which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted values differ with the site of measurement and the age of the baby. A low temperature is often the first sign of serious infection, and it is missed more easily than a fever because the baby simply feels cool and is assumed to be cold. A raised temperature may mean infection, but it may also mean the baby is overwrapped or the room is hot, so the measurement is repeated after a layer is removed. Every temperature is read with the behavior, the feeding, and the breathing, since a number alone decides nothing.

The breathing of a newborn is counted over a full minute while the baby is calm, because the rate changes with crying and feeding. A rate of 40 to 60 breaths per minute is often quoted as the ordinary range. That figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the range shifts with age, sleep, and the protocol in use. A rate above the accepted line, sustained over repeated counts, is a sign of respiratory difficulty or of metabolic compensation. Grunting, flaring, and drawing in of the chest wall show that the baby is working to keep the lungs open, and any of them in a baby who feeds poorly is serious. Pauses in breathing, especially pauses long enough to change the color or the tone, are a danger sign rather than the irregular breathing of a healthy newborn.

Infection of the bloodstream, called sepsis in this age group, is the great imitator of the newborn period. It must be considered in every baby who is not behaving normally, because the immature defenses produce vague and general signs rather than a local focus. The classic picture is a baby who feeds less, sleeps more, feels cold, breathes faster, and has a temperature that is unstable in either direction. Early infection, acquired around birth, is suggested by maternal fever, prolonged rupture of the membranes, prematurity, and organisms carried in the birth canal. Infection appearing after the first days is suggested by a line or device in the body, by poor hygiene around the umbilicus, or by an outbreak in the unit. The finding that reveals it is not one sign but the combination, together with a blood culture drawn before treatment begins, and the decision to treat on the clinical picture is usually right when the baby looks unwell and the history carries risk.

Some congenital heart lesions are silent until a vessel called the ductus arteriosus begins to close, and then the baby collapses. This vessel keeps blood flowing between the great arteries before birth, and it normally narrows in the first days and weeks of life. The interval over which it closes is offered as an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the timing differs between babies and descriptions. A baby whose circulation depends on that vessel may feed and appear well at first, then become pale, mottled, sweaty, and difficult to rouse, with a racing heart and a pressure that cannot be maintained. The finding that reveals a narrowing of the aorta is weak or absent pulses in the groin while the pulses in the arms are strong. A murmur may be present, but its absence proves nothing, and a screening measurement of oxygen saturation before discharge looks for exactly this possibility.

Color is examined in good light at the center of the body, because the hands and feet of a healthy newborn are often blue and this peripheral blue is not central cyanosis. Central cyanosis, seen on the lips, the tongue, and the inside of the mouth, means that the blood leaving the heart carries too little oxygen, and it demands immediate attention. Its causes include a heart lesion that lets deoxygenated blood into the circulation, a lung problem that prevents oxygen from entering the blood, and an infection that has made the baby very ill. Pallor points toward anemia, blood loss, or shock, while a mottled, grayish skin with cool limbs points toward a failing circulation. A baby who is blue at the center, or gray and cold, travels to a hospital now, whatever the rest of the examination shows.

Vomiting in a newborn is common and usually innocent, but the color of the vomit changes its meaning. A baby who vomits green or bile-stained fluid may have an obstruction of the bowel, and that finding alone sends the baby for assessment rather than prompting a change of feed. The lethal form is a twist of the gut on its own blood supply, in which the baby becomes unwell quickly and the abdomen becomes tense and tender. A baby who has not passed meconium within the first 24 to 48 hours of life also raises the possibility of obstruction or of a disease of the nerve cells of the bowel. That interval must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, and where an ultrasound or a contrast study exists it answers the question of where the blockage lies.

Feeding difficulty is the most sensitive single sign of illness in this age group, and it is assessed by watching a feed rather than by asking about it. The clinician observes how the baby takes the breast or the bottle, whether he latches, sucks in bursts, pauses to breathe, and tires before taking enough. A healthy newborn feeds often, in the ordinary case every 2 to 3 hours, and that interval is a teaching illustration to be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. The baby who fed well and now refuses, who takes a little and vomits, or who feeds greedily and then breathes rapidly has a symptom rather than a habit. Poor feeding is read together with the weight, the urine, and the stool, because a baby taking too little will show the effect in all three. The finding that makes a feed the wrong answer is a baby who turns blue, chokes, or becomes exhausted while feeding.

Weight turns the mother's impression into a figure, and in a clinic with a cloth sling and a spring scale it is the most useful instrument available. A birth weight is recorded for every baby, and the ordinary range is generally described as somewhere between 2.5 and 4 kilograms. That range is offered only as an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the distribution differs with the population and the length of the pregnancy. Almost every baby loses some weight in the first days, in the region of 7 to 10 per cent of the birth weight, and that figure too is an illustration to be verified locally rather than carried as a rule. The trend then matters more than any single reading, and failure to regain the birth weight by the accepted age, often described as around 10 to 14 days, should be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol. A scale that is not calibrated and a sling weighed with the baby's clothes will produce a misleading trend, so the same method is used at every visit.

The cry and the tone are the newborn's vocabulary, and both change before any laboratory value does. A high-pitched, shrill cry with irritability, arching of the back, and a refusal to be consoled may mean irritation of the brain or its coverings, or severe discomfort from the gut, and either way it must be explained. A weak, hoarse, or absent cry raises the possibility of a neuromuscular problem or of a baby too ill to protest. The mother's statement that the cry is different from usual is more valuable than any description of its pitch, and it should be recorded in her own words. Tone is examined by observing the posture at rest and by handling the limbs, since a healthy newborn keeps his arms and legs flexed and resists being extended. Tone is judged against the baby's age in the womb, because a baby born early is expected to be floppier than one born at term.

Jaundice is the most common problem of the newborn period, and it becomes a diagnostic question the moment its timing is known. A yellow color appearing within the first 24 hours of life is never treated as ordinary, because it suggests that blood is being broken down faster than the liver can handle, and that boundary must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since services differ. Jaundice that appears after the first day and settles over the following days is usually the ordinary kind that follows immature handling of bilirubin. Its peak is often placed around days 3 to 5 and its resolution by about 14 days, and both figures are illustrations that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the timing shifts with feeding, prematurity, and local practice. Extent carries information too, so the baby is examined undressed in good daylight and the clinician notes how far down the body the yellow color reaches. Jaundice that has reached the palms and soles is deeper than jaundice confined to the face, and pale, chalky stools with dark urine point away from the ordinary explanation.

Jaundice that is hemolytic arises because red blood cells are destroyed too quickly, and it declares itself early and often deeply. Its causes include incompatibility between the blood group of the mother and that of the baby, an inherited abnormality of the red cell, and an infection acquired before birth. The finding that reveals it is a falling blood count with a rapidly rising level of bilirubin, and the rate of the rise matters more than a single measurement. The danger is injury to the brain from bilirubin that is not bound in the blood, and that injury can appear in a baby who looks alert. The level is therefore followed and treated according to the local protocol rather than according to how the baby appears.

Jaundice that is obstructive is caused by bile that cannot leave the liver, and it must never drift past the ordinary review. The classic causes are a blocked bile duct present from birth, a swelling of the liver from infection, and a metabolic disease that damages the liver. The findings that reveal it are jaundice persisting well beyond the usual settling, stools that are pale or the color of putty, dark urine, and an enlarged, firm liver. A jaundice that has not settled by the accepted age, often taken as around 14 days, must be reassessed, and that boundary is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because services differ in how long they wait. Surgical correction of a blocked bile duct succeeds far more often early in life than late, so the diagnosis is a matter of days and not of watchful waiting. Any baby with pale stools and dark urine needs assessment where the bile pigment can be measured and the liver can be imaged.

A metabolic disease punishes the clinician who becomes comfortable with a well-looking baby, because the first days are often entirely normal. The inborn errors of metabolism appear when the baby is exposed to feeds he cannot process, so the story is one of apparent health followed by poor feeding, vomiting, and drowsiness. Some babies also have seizures or an unusual smell, and the examination may show nothing specific. The findings that support the diagnosis include a low level of glucose in the blood, an acid load in the blood, a raised level of ammonia, and a baby who worsens when fed and improves when not. A newborn screening test taken from a heel prick can identify several of these diseases before they declare themselves, so the clinician asks whether the test was done and what it showed. In a clinic with no laboratory, a baby who was well and is now not, with vomiting and drowsiness, is reason enough to travel.

Two derangements deserve separate mention because they are common, dangerous, and detectable with little equipment. A low level of glucose in the blood is a particular risk in the baby who is small, born early, cold, or not feeding, and it may present only as drowsiness, jitteriness, sweating, or refusal to feed. The measurement must be interpreted against the reference range used by the local laboratory, because the line that defines a low value differs between methods and services. Dehydration develops quickly in a newborn, because there is little reserve, and the signs are a sunken soft spot, a dry mouth, fewer wet diapers, weight loss beyond the expected, and lethargy. The decision to give fluid into a vein is made on those findings rather than on a single measurement, and the finding that would make that decision wrong is a baby who is feeding well and passing urine normally. In a clinic without a laboratory, the weight trend, the number of wet diapers, and the soft spot are the whole of the assessment.

The complete examination of the newborn is performed in a warm, quiet place, with the mother present, because it is also an opportunity to teach her what is normal. The clinician begins at the head, feeling the soft spots and the sutures, looking at the face for symmetry, examining the eyes for the red reflex that reveals a clouding of the lens or a tumor behind it, and inspecting the palate for a cleft. The neck, clavicles, and arms are examined for a fracture or weakness, and the heart is listened to while the pulses in the groin are felt and compared with those in the arms. The abdomen is examined gently for enlarged organs and for a mass, and the umbilical cord is inspected for redness, discharge, or bleeding. The genitalia and the anus are always looked at directly, and the hips are examined with the maneuvers that look for a dislocation, since a hip found early can be treated without surgery. The examination is not finished until the baby has been weighed, measured, and watched feeding.

The skin is a diagnostic surface, and many findings on it are normal variations that must be distinguished from disease. A pale, mottled, or dusky color, small bleeding spots, pustules, or a rash that does not fade when pressed all change the assessment. Redness around the base of the cord, a foul discharge, or bleeding from the stump are reasons to look further and to act. The neurological assessment is conducted largely by watching, and it asks whether the brain and nerves work as expected for the age in the womb. The clinician observes posture, spontaneous movements, the symmetry of the face when the baby cries, and the reflexes every newborn should have, among them the startle reflex, the grasp, the rooting, and the suck. Seizures in the newborn are easily missed, because they may look like a flicker of the eye, a chewing movement, or a pause in breathing rather than the shaking of an older child.

In a neonatal unit, the examination is followed by tests chosen to answer questions the clinical assessment has already framed. A blood count answers the question of anemia and infection, a culture of blood answers the question of which organism is present, and a measurement of glucose, salts, and acid answers the question of metabolic derangement. A chest radiograph answers the question of lung disease and of the position of lines and tubes. An ultrasound of the head answers the question of bleeding or fluid in the brain in a baby born early, an ultrasound of the heart answers the question of structure and flow, and an abdominal radiograph answers the question of obstruction when bilious vomiting is present. Where a CT or an MRI is needed, it is chosen for a specific question, and the smallest exposure and the safest transport are part of the decision. Every reference range must be the one issued for newborns by the local laboratory and confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer is not a lesser place to reason, and most decisions that save newborn lives are made in such a room. The baby is undressed in a warm place, weighed in a cloth sling on a spring scale, and examined from head to foot with the torch for the mouth, the red reflex, the skin, the cord, and the anus. The breathing is counted with a watch over a full minute, the heart is listened to, the pulses in the groin are felt, and the temperature is repeated if it is unusual. The integrated management of childhood illness provides a systematic way of assessing the sick young infant, and the reasoning it formalizes is the same one described here. Feeding, activity, breathing, temperature, and signs of local infection are combined into a decision about what to do next. The mother is asked directly about feeding, urine, stool, vomiting, and the color of the skin, because in this age group she is the monitoring system, and a baby judged well still needs a clear plan for review.

The decision that ends most newborn consultations is not a diagnosis but a question about the journey, and it should be made explicitly. The findings that mean the journey cannot wait until morning are bilious vomiting, blue or gray color at the center, floppiness or difficulty rousing, breathing with effort or pausing, an unstable temperature, and a baby who has stopped feeding and is passing no urine. The findings that allow a baby to be watched at home are equally specific: feeding well, passing urine, a normal temperature, a normal breathing pattern, and a mother able and willing to return. Every plan of watchful waiting must include what the mother should look for, when she should return, and how she will get there. A follow-up visit arranged in advance is worth more than an instruction to return if things worsen, because many families cannot judge that for themselves. The finding that makes a decision to watch the wrong one is any doubt about the mother's ability to observe and to return, and in that case the baby travels.

A constructed illustration may make these habits concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than a portrait of any real baby. Imagine a baby of a few days who has fed poorly since the previous evening, feels cool, breathes rapidly with a grunt, and lies floppy, whose mother says the membranes were ruptured for a long time before the birth; the combination is treated as infection until proved otherwise. Now imagine a baby of the same age who has vomited green fluid twice, whose abdomen is swollen, and who has passed no stool since birth; the finding of bile in the vomit changes everything, so the baby is kept from feeding and sent for assessment. A third baby turns yellow on the second day, feeds well, passes urine and normal stool, and fades by the end of the week, so the ordinary explanation is reasonable and the baby is reviewed rather than admitted. None of these babies is real, and none of these stories should be applied to a real baby without a full assessment. What all three share is the move that matters, which is to look for the finding that would change the plan rather than to wait for a test to provide one.

To summarize, the newborn is a patient whose history is told by his mother and whose signs must be read from his behavior. The assessment is organized around feeding, tone, cry, temperature, breathing, color, weight, and jaundice. The lethal possibilities are named at the bedside rather than deferred: sepsis in the baby who is not behaving normally, a congenital heart lesion that declares itself as a collapse when the ductus arteriosus closes, a bowel obstruction revealed by green vomit and a distended abdomen, a metabolic disease that follows apparent health, and a jaundice that is hemolytic or obstructive. Every score, cut-off, reference range, temperature, rate, weight, and interval in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. The Apgar score describes the condition at birth and diagnoses nothing, and it must not be used to predict a child's future. The same reasoning runs in a neonatal unit and in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, where the weight in a cloth sling, the count of the breaths, the pulses in the groin, and the mother's account carry the decision. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A baby who worries the clinician is examined again, observed for a defined interval, and moved to a place of safety when the findings or the doubt require it.
