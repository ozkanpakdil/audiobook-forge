# Chapter 57 — Pregnancy and Obstetrics

A pregnant woman is two patients at once, and most decisions in obstetrics follow from that fact. The clinician asks whether the pregnancy is normal for its stage and whether the symptom belongs to pregnancy or to a disease that pregnancy has made more likely. Pregnancy changes the baseline, so a pulse, a pressure, or a swelling may carry a different meaning here than in another patient. It also changes the consequences, because a delay for the mother is usually a delay for the baby, and a minor condition can become lethal. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the local protocol of the service in which the woman is seen.

Several lethal possibilities must be named before any comfortable explanation is accepted, because each is betrayed by a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. The woman of childbearing age with lower abdominal pain, a missed period, and faintness may have an ectopic pregnancy, and the finding is one-sided tenderness with shoulder tip pain. The woman bleeding in late pregnancy may have a placenta previa or a placental abruption, separated by whether the uterus is soft and painless or tense and tender. Sudden tearing pain in labor with loss of the baby's heartbeat may mean a uterine rupture, a surgical emergency recognized on suspicion. Fever, a rapid pulse, and an offensive discharge after birth mean puerperal sepsis until another cause is found, and breathlessness with a swollen calf may mean a clot in the lung. Pre-eclampsia runs behind all of these, because it can turn a mild headache into a seizure or a stroke.

The first step is to establish whether the woman is pregnant and how far the pregnancy has advanced, because every finding is read against that stage. A missed period, breast tenderness, nausea, urinary frequency, and fatigue are the usual early symptoms, and a test for the hormone human chorionic gonadotropin confirms the diagnosis where one exists. Where no test exists, the clinician relies on the history and on a softened, bluish cervix with an enlarged uterus. The date is usually estimated from the first day of the last menstrual period, and that estimate is approximate and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the method differs between services. Menstrual dates can mislead after a recent pregnancy or with an irregular cycle, and an early ultrasound measurement is more reliable where it can be obtained.

The history in pregnancy is an obstetric history as well as a medical one. The clinician asks how many pregnancies the woman has had, how many ended in birth, how many ended early, and how each one ended. A previous cesarean birth, a previous stillbirth, a previous pre-eclampsia, or a previous heavy bleeding after birth all raise the chance of the same problem returning. The medical past belongs in the same conversation, among it high blood pressure, diabetes, kidney disease, epilepsy, asthma, sickle cell disease, tuberculosis, and any previous clot. The medicines she takes, including treatment for infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, must be asked about without judgment. Social questions decide whether any plan is possible, so the clinician asks who is at home, how she would reach a hospital, and whether she is safe from violence.

Pregnancy alters the vital signs and produces symptoms that would be alarming in a patient who is not pregnant. The blood pressure usually falls a little in the middle months because the vessels relax, so a reading that would be ordinary elsewhere may be an early rise for this woman. The pulse rate climbs, and a rate of 100 beats per minute at rest may be normal near the end of pregnancy, though that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted range varies with the stage of pregnancy. Nausea, heartburn, constipation, and swelling of the feet and ankles are common, but swelling of the face and hands belongs to the assessment of pre-eclampsia. Faintness, palpitations, and fatigue are frequent, and they should still prompt a search for anemia, an arrhythmia, a clot in the lung, and a thyroid disorder. The temperature should not be raised by pregnancy itself, so a fever must be explained.

The laboratory and the examination also change in pregnancy, and a finding read without that knowledge can mislead. The blood count usually falls a little because the plasma increases more than the red cells, so a mild anemia of dilution is normal, while a genuinely low count with a low measure of stored iron points to a deficiency that must be corrected. The white cell count rises slightly and climbs further during labor, so it cannot be used alone to confirm infection. The blood clots more readily, which protects the woman at birth and endangers her afterwards. Substances that measure thyroid function shift at the start of pregnancy, so the reference range is set by the laboratory and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The legs should be examined for a difference in size, warmth, and tenderness, because a clot in a deep vein is common in pregnancy and may show nothing.

The abdominal examination in pregnancy has its own language, and each part answers a question about the baby. The uterus is measured from the pubic bone to the top of the fundus, and the height in centimeters usually follows the weeks of pregnancy after the middle of the second trimester, a guide that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the expected values differ with the woman's size and the number of babies. The clinician decides how the baby lies, whether head down, bottom down, or across, and feels which part is presenting at the brim of the pelvis. Listening for the baby's heartbeat with a stethoscope designed for the purpose, or with a hand held still, confirms that the baby is alive. The baby's movements are the cheapest and most sensitive report of wellbeing that exists, and a change in the pattern the woman knows is a reason to act rather than to wait.

Every antenatal contact, however brief, is an opportunity to look for the small number of signs that change everything. The clinician asks about bleeding from the vagina, pain in the abdomen, headache that will not settle, flashing lights or blurred vision, pain under the ribs on the right, sudden swelling of the face or hands, fever, and an offensive discharge. The woman is asked about the baby's movements, leakage of fluid, breathlessness, chest pain, a painful swollen leg, and any convulsion or loss of consciousness. The pressure is measured, the urine is tested for protein, the temperature is taken, and the abdomen is examined. The purpose of this fixed set of questions is recognition of the woman who must leave immediately, while she is still able to leave.

Pre-eclampsia is the disease that most often turns a normal pregnancy into an emergency, and it begins as a disorder of the placenta. It appears after the twentieth week of pregnancy, and the period over which it may start and progress must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted boundaries are stated differently in different services. The placenta releases substances that injure the lining of the blood vessels, so the pressure rises, protein leaks into the urine, fluid moves into the tissues, and the platelets fall. The woman may feel entirely well at the beginning, which is why the pressure and the urine are measured at every visit rather than only when she complains. The risk is higher in a first or multiple pregnancy, in a woman who has had pre-eclampsia before, and in a woman with high blood pressure, diabetes, kidney disease, or a high body mass index. The condition can also appear for the first time during labor or in the days after birth.

The assessment of pre-eclampsia rests on two measurements and a set of questions. The pressure is measured with the woman seated and rested, with a cuff of the right size, and a figure such as 140 over 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the line at which the diagnosis is considered; that figure is an illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted line differs between countries, editions, and ways of measuring. Protein in the urine supports the diagnosis, but a woman with a raised pressure and no protein may still have the disease, so its absence never excludes it. The clinician asks about headache, visual disturbance, pain under the ribs on the right, vomiting, and sudden swelling, because these move the assessment to a severe form of the disease. The examination looks for brisk reflexes, clonus at the ankle, fluid in the lungs, and tenderness in the upper abdomen.

Severe pre-eclampsia is recognized by the company it keeps rather than by the pressure alone. A severe unrelenting headache, flashing lights or loss of vision, pain in the upper abdomen, vomiting, breathlessness from fluid in the lungs, and a falling passage of urine all point to organs that are being injured. A seizure in a woman with a raised pressure is eclampsia, a life-threatening event treated as an emergency while the cause is confirmed. A pattern of breaking down of red cells, raised liver enzymes, and a low platelet count is another severe form that may appear with little or no raised pressure. The decision to deliver is made by the team, guided by the condition of the woman and of the baby rather than by a single figure.

The treatment decisions in pre-eclampsia are decisions about delivery and protection of the mother, not prescriptions that can be carried away from a book. Treatment is the right answer when the pressure is high and repeated, when there is protein in the urine with symptoms, when the blood tests show organ injury, or when the baby is not growing or moving as it should. The finding that makes delivery the wrong answer for now is a mild picture in a baby too immature to be born safely, in which case the woman is watched closely where the team can act at once. Where the team decides to prevent or treat a seizure, the medicine is chosen and given under the local protocol, and the listener should understand the reasoning rather than the amount. The clinician in a small clinic who recognizes the disease and starts the journey is doing more for the woman than one who waits for certainty.

Bleeding in early pregnancy is common, and the task is to separate the small bleeds that settle from the few that kill. The clinician asks how much blood has been lost, whether it is bright or dark, whether there is pain and where it is, and whether tissue has passed. The examination looks for a rising pulse, a falling pressure, tenderness in the abdomen, and a uterus that is tender or the wrong size for the dates. An ectopic pregnancy must be excluded in every woman of childbearing age with abdominal pain and a missed period, because it can bleed inside the abdomen and kill within hours. The findings that betray a leak are tenderness on one side, shoulder tip pain, a rising pulse, a falling pressure, and fullness in the lower abdomen, and an ultrasound shows an empty uterus with a mass beside it or fluid in the abdomen.

Miscarriage is far more common than ectopic pregnancy, and the danger is bleeding rather than rupture. A threatened miscarriage is bleeding with a closed cervix and a living pregnancy, and it often settles, though the woman must be told what would bring her back. An inevitable miscarriage is bleeding with an open cervix, an incomplete miscarriage leaves tissue behind with continuing bleeding and cramping, and a complete miscarriage has emptied the uterus. A missed miscarriage is a pregnancy that has stopped developing without bleeding, and it is found when the uterus is smaller than the dates or when the baby's heartbeat cannot be found. The clinician judges the amount of blood lost, the pulse, the pressure, and the tenderness, because the woman who is bleeding heavily needs help now rather than a diagnosis.

Bleeding in the second half of pregnancy is a different problem, and its causes are distinguished by pain and by the state of the uterus. The clinician asks whether the bleeding is painless or painful, whether it began suddenly, whether there was a fall or a blow to the abdomen, and whether the baby is moving as usual. The pressure and the pulse are measured at once, the abdomen is examined for tenderness and for the tone of the uterus, and the baby's heartbeat is listened for, because a mother who is stable may be carrying a baby who is not. The most important rule is that a digital examination of the vagina and the cervix is withheld until a placenta lying low has been excluded, because a finger in the cervix can provoke bleeding that cannot be stopped. Any woman with bleeding in late pregnancy belongs where an operating room and blood are available, because the blood that is visible may understate the blood that has been lost.

A placenta previa is a placenta that lies over the opening of the cervix, and it announces itself with painless, bright red bleeding that often begins without warning. The uterus is relaxed and not tender, the baby's presenting part may be high or lying across rather than head down, and the bleeding may stop and then return heavier, which is why a first small bleed is never dismissed. The danger is sudden heavy hemorrhage as the lower part of the uterus stretches or as labor begins, and it can kill the mother and the baby. The finding that reveals it is the placenta seen covering the cervical opening on ultrasound, and where no ultrasound exists the combination of painless bleeding, a soft uterus, and a baby that is not head down should carry the diagnosis. The woman is kept at rest where blood and an operating room are ready, and the way the baby will be born is usually by cesarean birth.

A placental abruption is the early separation of a normally placed placenta from the wall of the uterus, and it is the painful counterpart of placenta previa. The bleeding may be visible or concealed behind the placenta, and this is why the amount of blood seen can badly mislead the clinician. The uterus is tense and tender, often hard to the touch and painful to press, and the woman may have constant pain with a uterus that does not relax between contractions. The findings that reveal the danger are a rising pulse, a falling pressure, a baby that is not moving or whose heartbeat is abnormal, and a uterus larger than the dates because of blood trapped inside. The woman needs to be moved at once to a place where the baby can be monitored and where a birth can be carried out quickly, because abruption is a race against bleeding that continues behind the scenes.

A uterine rupture is a tear in the wall of the uterus, and it is one of the few diagnoses made almost entirely on the story and the feel of the abdomen. It happens most often in a woman who has had a previous cesarean birth or previous surgery on the uterus, and it may happen during a long or obstructed labor. The woman describes a sudden severe pain that may be tearing in character, the contractions may stop, and the shape of the abdomen may change as the baby and the placenta move into the abdominal cavity. The clinician may feel the baby's parts unusually easily through the abdominal wall, the baby's heartbeat may be lost, and the mother may become shocked from bleeding that is largely hidden. Any woman with a previous uterine scar and sudden pain, bleeding, or loss of the baby's heartbeat must be treated as having a rupture until the operating room says otherwise.

Bleeding after birth is the other great obstetric hemorrhage, and its commonest cause is a uterus that does not contract once the placenta is delivered. The clinician assesses the amount of blood lost, the pulse, the pressure, the color of the skin, and the level of consciousness, and looks for a uterus that is soft and enlarged rather than firm and low. Other causes belong in the same assessment, among them retained pieces of placenta, a tear in the cervix or the vagina, and a disorder of clotting. The finding that makes the decision to act right is continuing bleeding with a rising pulse and a falling pressure, and bleeding that has clearly stopped with a firm uterus is what would make a wait reasonable. The woman must be where blood can be replaced and the cause treated, and the clinician who cannot control the bleeding must arrange transfer rather than watch.

The woman with puerperal sepsis has a fever, a rapid pulse, chills, and pain in the lower abdomen, and she may have an offensive discharge and a uterus that is tender and larger than it should be. The infection may also begin in a tear, a surgical cut, a breast, the urine, the lungs, or a wound from a cesarean birth, so every site must be examined rather than assumed to be the womb. The warning findings are a rapid pulse that does not settle, rapid breathing, confusion, a falling pressure, and a reduced passage of urine. The patient whose infection has become severe needs treatment and senior help at once, and the decision to give an antibiotic before the organism is known is right when the picture is severe and wrong when a clear non-infectious cause explains everything.

A clot in a deep vein and a clot that travels to the lung are more common in pregnancy and after birth than at any other time in a young woman's life, and the second half of the disease kills quickly. The risk is highest after a cesarean birth, after a long labor, with immobility, with a high body mass index, with a previous clot, and with pre-eclampsia. The finding in the leg is swelling of one calf, warmth, tenderness, and a difference in size between the two sides, but the leg may look and feel entirely normal, so a normal examination proves nothing. The finding in the lung is a sudden breathlessness, chest pain that worsens with a breath, a rapid pulse, and a cough that may bring blood, and the chest may sound clear. Where tests exist they help, and where they do not, a woman with a plausible story is treated while she travels, because the harm of a missed clot is greater than the harm of treatment given on a good clinical case.

Heart disease is among the leading causes of death in pregnancy, and it is dangerous because the extra work of pregnancy unmasks a heart that was coping before. The woman may have been told years earlier that she had a murmur or a damaged valve, and the findings that reveal the problem are breathlessness worse than expected, a loud or new murmur, an abnormal pressure or pulse, and a faint on effort. The woman is offered testing for HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis, and a positive result is what makes treatment the right answer, while a sexually transmitted infection may cause no symptoms, so questions about discharge, pain, and a partner with symptoms belong in the history. A fever in a region where malaria is transmitted is a malaria test and a journey, not a wait, because the disease can cause severe anemia and death of the baby.

In a hospital with an ultrasound, a laboratory, and an operating room, the assessment moves from suspicion to precision with tests chosen to answer specific questions. An ultrasound answers whether the pregnancy is inside the uterus, whether it is alive, how far it has advanced, where the placenta lies, and how the baby is growing, and it is the single most useful image in obstetrics. A blood count, a measure of stored iron, a clotting test, a blood group, and tests of liver and kidney function answer questions about the mother that the bedside cannot answer. An ECG and an ultrasound of the heart answer the questions raised by breathlessness or a murmur, and a CT or an MRI is used sparingly in pregnancy and only when the answer would change what is done. The operating room and the intensive care unit, or ICU, are the destinations for the woman whose bleeding, pressure, or infection cannot be controlled.

The clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer has no ultrasound and no laboratory, and yet most of the important decisions can still be made there. The history carries the greatest weight, because it never runs out: the date of the last period, the number of previous births, what happened in each, what the woman is feeling now, and how the baby is moving. The pressure is measured with a cuff, the temperature is taken, and the urine is examined for protein with a dipstick where one is available and by eye where one is not. The torch makes the gums and the conjunctiva visible for pallor, the skin visible for jaundice and rash, and the cervix visible when it is safe to look. The abdomen is palpated for the size of the uterus, the lie of the baby, and the presenting part, and a tape measure turns the fundal height into a figure that can be compared at the next visit.

The most important skill of all is recognition of the woman who must leave immediately, and the list is short enough to be remembered under pressure. She is the woman who is bleeding at any stage, especially with pain, fainting, or a uterus that is hard and tender. She is the woman in late pregnancy or after birth who has a severe headache, flashing lights, blurred vision, pain under the ribs on the right, or a fit. She is the woman with a fever and a rapid pulse who looks seriously ill, and the woman who is breathless with a swollen leg or chest pain. She is the woman whose baby has stopped moving, whose waters have broken with a green or offensive fluid, or whose baby is lying across at the time of birth. Any one of these findings is enough to start the journey, and the clinician who cannot test must rely on honest judgment rather than on false reassurance.

The weeks after birth are part of obstetrics, and they carry their own emergencies as well as their own quiet dangers. The uterus should shrink steadily and the bleeding should lessen over the first days, and a uterus that stays large or a bleeding that increases must be explained. The clinician examines the perineum or the abdominal wound, the breasts, the legs, the chest, and the temperature, because infection and clots may begin in any of those places, and pre-eclampsia can appear for the first time after birth. Communication decides whether any plan is possible, so the clinician asks who can bring the woman to a hospital, how long the journey takes, what it costs, and whether anyone at home would oppose it. A written referral should carry the dates, the findings, the pressure, the urine result, what has been done, and what is feared.

A constructed illustration may make these habits concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a woman early in pregnancy with a dull pain on one side, a small amount of bleeding, and faintness when she stands, in whom the clinician finds tenderness on that side and a rising pulse, and who is sent onward at once because the combination means an ectopic pregnancy until proved otherwise. Imagine also a woman near the end of pregnancy with a headache that will not settle and swelling of her hands, whose pressure is high and whose urine shows protein, and who is kept where the baby can be monitored. Neither woman is real, and each shows the same move, which is that the clinician asked the question the ordinary routine would have skipped.

To summarize, pregnancy changes the normal examination and creates its own diagnoses, so every finding is read against the stage of the pregnancy and against the woman's own baseline. The dangerous conditions are named where they appear, among them an ectopic pregnancy, a placental abruption, a placenta previa, a uterine rupture, puerperal sepsis, pre-eclampsia with its seizures and its organ injury, and thromboembolism, and each is recognized by a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. Bleeding in early pregnancy is separated by whether the pregnancy is inside the uterus and whether the woman is stable, and bleeding in late pregnancy by whether the uterus is painless or painful and tense, with the digital examination withheld until a low placenta has been excluded. A woman who is bleeding, fitting, feverish, breathless, or whose baby has stopped moving must travel now, and the clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer can recognize her. Every figure here must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories.
