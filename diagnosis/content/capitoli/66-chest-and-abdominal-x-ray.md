# Chapter 66 — Chest and Abdominal X-Ray

A radiograph is a shadow, and the whole skill of reading one follows from that single fact. The beam passes through the patient, and the structures in its path absorb it in proportion to their density, so what reaches the film is not a picture of the body but a record of how much of the beam was taken away, and every black area and every white area is a statement about what lay in the way. This chapter gives a sequence for the chest radiograph and a sequence for the film of the abdomen, chosen so that the reading does not depend on the finding being obvious, because the finding that kills is often the one nobody expected to see. The material is educational and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Four findings deserve to be named before the sequence begins, because each changes what is done within minutes and each is easily missed by an eye looking for something else. Free air under the diaphragm means that a hollow organ has perforated, and it appears as a crescent of gas between the liver and the right dome that has no business being there. A pneumothorax appears as a lung edge with no vessels beyond it, and it becomes a tension when the mediastinum shifts and the patient can no longer breathe. A widened mediastinum after a fall or with tearing chest pain may mean an injury to the aorta, revealed by a silhouette broader than the patient should have, an abnormal contour, a trachea that has moved, or a cap of density at the apex of the lung. A bowel obstruction shows loops that are distended on one side of a blockage and empty on the other, and its danger is not the blockage itself but the loss of blood supply that may follow. Naming them at the start keeps the eye from settling on the first abnormality it finds.

To predict what a disease will look like, the listener has to hold in mind the four densities that a radiograph can tell apart. Air absorbs almost none of the beam and appears black, fat absorbs a little, soft tissue and water absorb more and appear as shades of gray, and bone or metal absorb most and appear white, so a lung that has filled with pus, blood, or water turns from black toward white where the filling has occurred. The same logic explains why a structure hidden behind the heart cannot be seen, because the beam was already weakened by everything it passed through, and all of those structures are added together on one flat image. A clinician who reasons in densities rather than in named diseases can look at an unfamiliar film and say what must be happening inside the chest.

The film is an object before it is an image, and the first step of the sequence has nothing to do with medicine. The name on the film must be checked against the patient in front of the clinician, the date and the time must be read, and the side marker must be confirmed, because a film of the wrong patient or the wrong side has caused more harm than any subtle sign. The clinician also looks for an earlier film of the same patient, since a comparison with a previous study is often worth more than any single observation on the current one. In a hospital the images are stored and can be brought up beside the new one, while in a clinic the earlier film may be a single sheet in a folder that the patient carries, and it should be asked for every time.

The projection is the next question, because the same chest looks different in each of the standard positions. The film taken with the patient standing and the beam passing from back to front is the most faithful, since the heart lies close to the film and is not magnified, and the shoulder blades are rotated clear of the lungs. The film taken at the bedside with a portable machine passes from front to back, the heart appears larger, and the shoulder blades fall across the upper zones, so a heart that looks enlarged on such a film may be entirely normal. Position matters as much as direction, because a patient who is lying flat cannot show free air under the diaphragm, and fluid around the lung spreads out in a layer rather than collecting at the base. Three technical qualities then decide whether the film can be trusted at all: rotation, judged by the position of the two clavicles relative to the spine, penetration, judged by whether the vertebral bodies can be seen faintly behind the heart, and inspiration, judged by the level of the diaphragm and the number of ribs above it. A film that fails these checks is not a film to be read but a film to be repeated.

The lateral film is the companion of the frontal one, and it answers questions that the frontal view cannot. It shows the space behind the breastbone and the space behind the heart, both of which are hidden from the front, and it displays the posterior angle between the diaphragm and the chest wall, where a small collection of fluid may gather before it is visible from the front. The lateral view also shows the spine through the clear lung, and a spine that becomes whiter as it descends is a sign that something behind it has increased in density. Because the two views are taken at right angles, a finding that appears on both can be placed in three dimensions, while a finding on only one should be treated with suspicion until it is confirmed. A clinician who reads only the frontal film is reading one half of the available evidence.

The chest sequence begins with the airway, which is the easiest structure to see and the easiest to forget. The windpipe should run down the midline and divide into the two main bronchi, and a windpipe that is pushed to one side tells the clinician that something on that side is pushing, while a windpipe pulled to one side tells of something that has collapsed and is pulling. The difference is not academic, because a large collection of air or fluid pushes the airway away and demands immediate attention, while collapse of a whole lung pulls it toward the side of the collapse and follows a different path. The clinician also looks at the caliber of the windpipe and the bronchi, since a narrowing near the great vessels or a mass that has grown into the airway may be the reason for a breathlessness that no other sign explains. Following the airway first means that a shift, which is the loudest finding on many chest films, is never missed.

Breathing comes second, and it means the lungs and the covering around them. The two sides are compared for their overall blackness, and any region that is whiter than the same region on the other side must be explained. A patch of lung that has filled with pus produces a consolidation, and the finding that confirms it is the air bronchogram, which is the outline of an air-filled airway running through the white area because the surrounding air sacs have filled while the airway has stayed open. A nodule or a mass is a rounded opacity, and a cavity is a rounded opacity with a darker center, which tells the clinician that tissue inside has died or that a hole has formed. A diffuse increase in the markings of the lung, with lines and small dots rather than one patch, points to disease of the supporting tissue between the air sacs rather than of the air sacs themselves, and that distinction changes the list of causes entirely.

The silhouette sign is the most useful single idea in the reading of a chest film, and it follows directly from the addition of shadows. When two structures of the same density lie against each other, the border between them disappears, so the loss of a border tells the clinician which structure the disease is touching. A whiteness that has erased the right heart border lies in the middle lobe or the inner part of the right lung, one that has erased the left heart border lies in the lingula, and one that has erased a hemidiaphragm lies at the base. Fluid around the lung behaves differently again, because it settles and forms a curved upper edge at the side of the chest, and a large collection pushes the mediastinum away while a collapsed lung pulls it toward the same side.

Circulation is the next step, and it begins with the heart and the central column of the chest. The heart shadow is judged for its size, its shape, and its position, and a heart wider than one half of the width of the chest is the traditional description of enlargement, offered here as an illustration of the reasoning, so confirm the proportion against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the projection and the phase of breathing both change it. A bulge in the contour points to the chamber beneath it, and a double contour on the right may be the first clue to disease in a valve. The mediastinum is where a widened silhouette becomes an emergency, because it may be an acute problem of the aorta in a patient with tearing pain or after a fall, revealed by the widening itself with an abnormal contour or a cap of density at the apex of the lung. A width of 8 centimeters at the level of the aortic arch is a figure often quoted in teaching, offered here only as a teaching figure, so the measurement that should prompt action must be checked against the guideline in force where you work, since it depends on the projection and on how the film was framed. The pulmonary vessels are followed from the center toward the edge, because their pattern tells the clinician whether the pressure behind them has risen.

The picture of a heart that is failing is a picture of pressure that has moved backward, and the radiograph shows it in the order the pressure travels. The heart may be enlarged, the upper lobe vessels may become more prominent than the lower ones because the blood is being sent upward, and the hila may look congested and indistinct. Fluid may collect around both lungs, more often on the right, and it may hide in the fissures between the lobes as a line or a thickening that is easy to overlook. When the fluid enters the air sacs themselves, the whiteness surrounds the hila in a pattern that fades toward the edge of the lung, and a clinician who has seen the same patient earlier will recognize the change faster than any report.

The diaphragm is the fourth step, and it is where two of the dangerous findings live. The domes should be smooth and curved and the angles between the diaphragm and the chest wall should be sharp, because a small collection of fluid may blunt an angle before it is visible anywhere else. Free air under the diaphragm is the finding that means a perforated hollow organ, and it appears as a crescent of gas between the liver and the right dome that does not belong to the stomach or the bowel. The film must be taken with the patient sitting or standing and must include both domes, because a patient lying flat spreads the gas along the front of the abdomen where a chest film cannot see it. On the left side the stomach bubble is normal, so the clinician must judge whether the gas under the left dome has the shape of a stomach or the shape of a free collection, and a sliver of gas that follows the curve of the dome and crosses the midline belongs to a surgical emergency rather than to a clinic visit.

A pneumothorax is the other dangerous finding at this step, and it is recognized by the absence of something rather than by its presence. The lung edge appears as a thin white line, and beyond that line there are no vessels at all, which tells the clinician that the black space is air outside the lung rather than lung that is simply overinflated. When the pressure in that space rises, the mediastinum is pushed to the opposite side, the diaphragm on that side is flattened, and the patient becomes short of breath and unstable; this is the tension pneumothorax, which is treated on the clinical picture before any film is seen. On a film taken with the patient lying flat, the air rises to the front of the chest and may show only as an unusually clear upper zone or a deep groove at the angle, so a film that looks almost normal does not exclude the diagnosis. In a clinic without a radiograph, the same reasoning is carried by the hands and the ears, since breath sounds reduced on one side and a note like a drum when the chest is tapped are the radiographic signs made audible.

Certain places on a chest film are known to hide findings, and the sequence should pause at each of them. The apices come first, because a small pneumothorax or an early scar may sit above the clavicle shadow and be read as nothing. The area behind the heart is next, because a lesion there is invisible from the front unless the clinician looks at the lateral view or notices that the border has changed. The angles below the lungs are the third, because a small effusion blunts an angle before it produces any other sign. The bones are the fourth hiding place, since a rib fracture or a deposit in the spine is easy to pass over while the eye is occupied with the lungs, and the soft tissues of the neck and the chest wall are read in the same sweep. A clinician who visits each of these areas in turn, every time, will find the finding that a quick glance would have missed.

The abdominal film asks a different set of questions, and its value depends entirely on whether the clinician has a question for it. A plain film of the abdomen is a poor tool for the patient with undifferentiated abdominal pain, because most of the causes produce no finding on it, and a normal film is therefore a false comfort. Its real strength lies in four specific questions, which are the pattern of the gas, the outlines of the organs, the presence of free air, and the presence of calcification. The stomach holds a bubble of air, the colon holds gas divided into segments by the folds of its wall, and the small bowel holds very little gas in a healthy person, so gas that is visible in the small bowel in quantity is itself a finding. The small bowel tends to lie centrally and to show fine lines across its whole width, while the colon runs around the edge and shows thicker, incomplete folds of its own, and a difference between two segments of the same bowel is more informative than the absolute size of either.

A bowel obstruction is the dangerous pattern in the abdomen, and it is produced by a simple mechanism. When the bowel is blocked, the segment above the block fills with swallowed air and with the fluid the bowel itself secretes, and it distends against the blockage while the bowel below it empties. The film therefore shows loops that are wider above the block, less gas below it, and, on a film taken with the patient upright, the horizontal line of an air-fluid level in each loop that holds both gas and liquid. A volvulus is a twist, and it produces a single, enormous, gas-filled loop that has lost its normal shape and looks like a bent inner tube or a coffee bean, a surgical emergency rather than a medical one. A bowel that has stopped moving produces distension of both the small and the large bowel without a point of transition, because the bowel has become quiet rather than blocked, and that distinction is an important judgment the film can support. Small bowel loops beyond 3 centimeters across are commonly called dilated, and that figure is offered as a teaching illustration, so confirm the value against the current guideline and the protocol where you work, because it shifts with the part of the bowel and with the magnification of the film.

One film often cannot settle the question, and the change between two films usually can. A repeat film after an interval of 6 hours is often described in teaching as a way to show a pattern that a single film could not, and both the interval and the reasoning behind it belong to local practice, so verify them against the guideline that governs your service, because the useful timing depends on the patient and on the question being asked. What the clinician looks for across the two films is not a different disease but a direction, since loops that have widened further and fluid that has appeared where there was none are statements about movement. Free air in the abdomen is the same danger as free air under the diaphragm, and the gas rises, so an upright chest film is more sensitive for it than a film taken with the patient lying down, while a film taken with the patient lying on the left side lets the gas collect along the wall of the abdomen. A normal film does not exclude a perforation, and a clinician who relies on the film alone may send home a patient who needed an operation.

The outlines of the organs are the next question, and they are read by looking for the shapes that should be there. The liver fills the right upper abdomen, the spleen sits behind the stomach on the left and is usually not visible unless it has enlarged, and the two kidneys lie beside the spine with their long axes tilted toward each other. The muscles beside the spine make a shadow on each side, and the loss of one of those shadows suggests that something is happening in the space behind the abdomen. Calcification is the fourth question, and it most often rewards a careful eye, because calcium absorbs the beam strongly and appears as a white density against the softer gray of the tissue around it. Stones in the kidney and the urinary tract are often seen, and their position along the tube from the kidney to the bladder helps to identify them, while stones in the gallbladder are usually not visible because most contain too little calcium. Calcification in the pancreas appears as scattered white specks across the upper abdomen, and an old infection can leave calcified lymph nodes, but a calcification is never the whole diagnosis.

A constructed illustration may make the sequence concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a young man brought to a hospital after a fall, who is short of breath and whose chest film is taken lying down at the bedside, and in whom the film is rotated, the heart looks large, and the left lung looks darker than the right. The clinician who checks the projection first knows that the heart is magnified by the position, and the clinician who then compares the two sides for vessels rather than for whiteness finds that the left side has no vessels at its edge, which is the pneumothorax that the rotation almost concealed. Imagine instead an older patient with a swollen abdomen and vomiting, whose film shows loops that are wider in one region than another, with air-fluid levels on the upright film and no gas in the lower bowel. The two films look similar to an untrained eye and speak different sentences to a trained one, and neither patient is real, so neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment.

The radiographic finding is what turns a suspicion into a decision. Free air under the diaphragm, or gas in a place where gas cannot normally be, makes the decision to operate the right one, and it makes it urgent, while the finding that would make that decision wrong is gas explained by a recent operation, a tube, or a normal stomach. A pneumothorax with a shifted mediastinum and a struggling patient makes the decision to relieve the pressure before any further image is taken, and a clinician who waits for a second film has misunderstood the priority. A patch of consolidation in a febrile, breathless patient makes treatment of an infection the right answer when the picture fits, and the same patch in a patient who is not infected makes that answer wrong, which is why the film is read beside the patient rather than at a distance. A widened mediastinum in a patient with sudden severe pain makes the decision to seek immediate surgical help the right one, and a normal film in that same patient does not make it wrong.

In a hospital the film is usually read twice, once by the clinician who ordered it and once by the radiologist, and the two readings serve different purposes. The clinician reads with a question in mind, while the radiologist reads with a fixed sequence and a habit of noticing the incidental finding, and neither reading replaces the other. The report should be read as an answer to a question rather than as a diagnosis, and a report that does not address the question asked should be taken back to the radiologist rather than filed and forgotten. When the report and the clinician disagree, the useful move is to look at the film again with the report in hand and the patient in mind, because each of them holds information the other lacks. In a clinic with a light box and no radiologist, the discipline of the sequence matters more rather than less, because there may be a single film, taken at an earlier visit, and nobody to ask. The reading is done on a proper light source in a darkened room with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer close at hand, and what was seen is written down in the order it was sought.

Several habits spoil the reading of a radiograph, and each of them is easy to fall into. Reading the film before seeing the patient is the first, because a film read without a history becomes a hunt for something, anything, and the eye will find it. Treating the film as the diagnosis rather than as a question is the second, because a normal film in a patient with a convincing story is a reason to look further rather than a reason to stop. Accepting a report as the end of the reasoning is the third, and the failure to compare with what came before is the fourth. Every radiograph also has limits that the clinician should be able to state out loud, because a flat addition of a curved body hides what lies behind the heart, and an abdominal film is even less forgiving, since the bowel overlaps itself and the organs lie in front of and behind one another. A clinician who reads in a fixed order, who writes the findings down, and who can say what a film cannot show will avoid all of these traps.

The decision to order a film is part of the reasoning and not a reflex. A chest film is the right answer when the question is whether there is consolidation, fluid around the lung, a pneumothorax, a widened mediastinum, or the pattern of a failing heart, and when the answer will change what is done. It is the wrong answer when the examination has already answered the question, when the patient is too unstable to leave the bedside for anything except a portable film, and when no result could change the plan. In a program that screens for tuberculosis, the chest radiograph has a defined role, and that role is set by the World Health Organization and by the national program rather than by the individual clinician, so it should be checked there. A film that has been read and acted upon still leaves a patient who must be followed, and the signs that should bring that patient back are the same signs that made the film necessary, which are a worsening breathlessness, a new pain, a fever that will not settle, a failure to improve, or a new weakness.

To summarize, the radiograph is a shadow read by a sequence that does not depend on the finding being obvious, and the sequence is what protects the patient from the eye that settles too early. The film is first checked for its patient, its date, its side, its projection, and its technical quality, then the chest is read through the airway, the breathing, the circulation, the diaphragm, the edges, and the bones, and the abdomen through the gas pattern, the organ outlines, the free air, and the calcifications. The dangerous findings are named as the sequence meets them, which are free air under the diaphragm, a pneumothorax, a widened mediastinum, and a bowel obstruction, each with the finding that reveals it. Every figure in this chapter, whether a width, a proportion, a caliber, or an interval, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and the way each film is taken. The reasoning is the same with a radiologist beside the clinician and with a single film on a light box in a clinic, and this is educational material that does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.
