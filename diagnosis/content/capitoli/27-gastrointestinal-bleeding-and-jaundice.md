# Chapter 27 — Gastrointestinal Bleeding and Jaundice

Bleeding from the gut and yellowing of the skin are two of the oldest signals in medicine, and they arrive together often enough that the clinician should hold them in one frame. Both ask the same three questions, which are where the source lies, how fast the process is moving, and what dangerous thing it may turn out to be. Bleeding travels downward from a point that cannot be seen, and jaundice spreads outward from an organ buried under the ribs, so the diagnosis is assembled from indirect evidence and from the way the picture changes over hours. This chapter is educational material and cannot replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which you work. The reasoning is the same in a hospital with endoscopy and in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch and a thermometer, but the decision that follows from it is very different.

The causes that must not be missed are few, and each has a finding that betrays it. A bleed from swollen veins at the lower end of the esophagus, the variceal hemorrhage of raised pressure in the portal circulation, appears in a patient who already carries the marks of chronic liver disease and who vomits fresh blood and then turns pale, sweaty and tachycardic. A perforated peptic ulcer announces itself with sudden severe pain and a rigid, board-like abdomen, while an ulcer that bleeds without perforating may erode an artery and cause a large loss with little warning. A ruptured aortic graft presents as bleeding from the gut in anyone who has had surgery on the aorta, sometimes after a smaller warning bleed, and it is fatal when it is not recognized. Cholangitis, which is infection behind an obstruction of the biliary tree, produces fever with chills, yellowing of the skin and pain in the right upper abdomen. Obstructive jaundice in an older patient is a malignancy until it is proven otherwise, especially when it is painless and progressive and accompanied by weight loss and by a gallbladder that can be felt below the rib margin.

The first practical question in any bleed is whether the source lies above or below the junction where the duodenum becomes the jejunum, because that distinction changes the likely cause, the instrument that will find it, and the speed required. Blood from above that point usually arrives as vomited blood, fresh and bright or dark and partly digested, and it may also pass downward as black, tarry, offensive stool that the clinician calls melena. Blood from below the junction usually arrives as bright red blood through the rectum, though a brisk upper source can look the same when the bowel moves too quickly for the blood to darken. A volume of perhaps 50 to 100 milliliters is often quoted as the smallest amount that will blacken stool, but treat the figure as an illustration and confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the amount depends on how quickly the blood travels and on how long it rests inside the bowel.

How much blood has been lost governs everything that follows, and it must be answered from the circulation rather than from the laboratory. In the first hours of an acute hemorrhage the concentration of hemoglobin may still look normal, because red cells and plasma are lost together and the blood that remains is not yet diluted. The count falls later, as fluid moves from the tissues into the vessels and as any fluid given to the patient dilutes what is left, so a normal early result is a trap rather than a reassurance. The bedside therefore reads the pulse, the blood pressure, the rate and depth of breathing, the warmth of the hands and feet, the state of alertness, and the volume of urine passed. A pulse above 100 beats per minute and a systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury are traditional markers of significant loss, and each of those figures must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since age, pregnancy, physical fitness and long-standing high blood pressure all move the value that matters. A rise in the pulse when the patient sits or stands is often the earliest sign that the circulation is short of volume.

The history in a patient who is bleeding is short and pointed, because time is being spent while blood is being lost. The clinician asks when the bleeding began, whether it is continuing, how much the patient believes has come away, and whether there has been vomiting of blood, black stool or bright red blood. Previous episodes, a known ulcer, an earlier operation on the stomach or the aorta, and any known disease of the liver are asked for by name, because each moves the differential diagnosis sharply. Medicines matter enormously, and the clinician asks about anti-inflammatory painkillers, about drugs that thin the blood and about alcohol, since some of these injure the lining of the stomach and others make a bleed that has started harder to stop. The clinician also asks about weight loss, a change in the habit of the bowel, and a family history of cancer of the stomach or bowel, because a first bleed in an older adult may be the presenting sign of a tumor.

The examination begins at the door, with the clinician noting whether the patient is pale, sweating, restless, drowsy or confused, because these are the outward signs of a failing circulation. The pulse and the pressure are recorded with the patient lying, sitting and standing where that is safe, and the respiratory rate is counted rather than estimated. The abdomen is inspected for distension, for prominent veins on the wall, for a scar from earlier surgery and for the rounded fullness of free fluid, and it is palpated for an enlarged liver or spleen and for tenderness. The rectal examination is not optional in a gut bleed, since it confirms whether blood is present, describes its color, and can reveal a mass that the rest of the examination has not reached. The skin and the eyes are examined for yellowing, for small spider-like vessels on the upper trunk, for redness of the palms and for bruising, since each of these points towards the liver as the origin of the problem.

A variceal hemorrhage is the bleed that kills most quickly, and it deserves its own reasoning. Long-standing liver disease raises the pressure in the vein that drains the gut, and that pressure is carried backward into the veins of the esophagus and the upper stomach, which swell and become thin-walled. When one of them bursts, the patient vomits a large volume of blood, the pressure falls, and the liver that caused the problem is often no longer able to make the factors that clot the blood. The findings that reveal it are the marks of chronic liver disease, an enlarged spleen, free fluid in the abdomen, a history of heavy alcohol use or long-standing viral hepatitis, and a previous episode of bleeding. The clinician who sees fresh blood in the vomit of a patient with any of those features should treat the source as variceal until it is shown otherwise, and where endoscopy is not available the decision is to begin the journey to a place that has it immediately.

Peptic ulcer disease remains the most common cause of a major upper bleed in many settings, and it has two faces. The first is the chronic ulcer that erodes into a vessel and bleeds, sometimes painlessly, sometimes after a period of indigestion or treatment with anti-inflammatory painkillers. The second is the perforated ulcer, in which the wall of the stomach or the duodenum gives way and its contents spill into the abdomen, producing sudden severe generalized pain, a rigid abdomen that moves little with breathing, and a patient who lies very still because any movement hurts. The finding that reveals the perforation best is the rigid abdomen together with a loss of the normal dullness over the liver, since gas escaping under the diaphragm can press against that organ and change its note. Either face of the ulcer can coexist with the other, and a patient who has bled and then develops severe pain has almost certainly perforated as well.

A ruptured aortic graft is the diagnosis that is missed because the clinician does not ask about surgery. In a patient who has had a graft placed on the aorta, an abnormal connection can form between the graft and the bowel, and it typically produces a small warning bleed that stops on its own before a massive and usually fatal one follows. The presentation is therefore bleeding from the gut, most often from above or from a site that endoscopy cannot identify, in a patient with a history of aortic surgery and often with pain in the back or the abdomen. The finding that reveals it is the combination of that surgical history with a bleed that has no obvious cause, and the clinician should ask directly about any previous operation on the aorta in every patient who bleeds from the gut. A pulsatile, expansile mass in the upper abdomen may also be present, and its presence makes the situation more urgent still.

Bleeding from below the duodenum has a different list of causes, and the age of the patient reorders it. In a young person, bleeding that comes and goes, particularly painless bright red blood on the paper, is often from a crack at the anal margin or from swollen veins in the anal canal, but that assumption must be made carefully. In an older person the common causes shift towards outpouchings of the wall of the colon, towards abnormally fragile vessels in the lining of the bowel, towards inflammation of the bowel and towards a tumor. Infection and inflammation produce bleeding with diarrhea, urgency and cramping, and the bleed is usually only part of a picture that also includes fever. A tumor of the colon may bleed slowly and invisibly, producing anemia and fatigue rather than a visible loss, which is why the clinician never dismisses a first episode of rectal bleeding in an older adult as hemorrhoids.

Once a bleed is recognized, the next decision is how urgently it must be managed, and that decision rests on a few features rather than on the appearance of the blood alone. Continuing bleeding, unstable vital signs, a pressure that falls when the patient sits up, a very low count on arrival, an age in the later decades, serious disease of the heart, lung or kidney, and treatment that thins the blood all push the patient towards urgent action. Formal scores exist that combine such features, among them the score named for Glasgow and Blatchford, and they formalize the reasoning that some patients with a normal pulse and pressure still need close observation and early endoscopy. This book does not restate the contents of any such score, because each was derived in a particular population and is revised from time to time, so read the current description and check it against the local protocol before using it to decide anything. What matters at the bedside is that normal first observations in a patient who has bled a large volume are a moment to keep watching, not one to relax.

Jaundice is the yellowing of the skin, the whites of the eyes and the deep tissues that appears when bilirubin, the yellow pigment made from worn-out red cells, accumulates faster than the liver can process and clear it. The reasoning divides it into three broad places where the process can fail, which are before the liver, inside the liver and after it. Before the liver means that the pigment is produced in excessive amounts, usually because red cells are destroyed too quickly, so the liver is overwhelmed. Inside the liver means that the organ itself is injured and cannot take up, process or release the pigment, which happens in infection, in damage from alcohol or medicines and in long-standing scarring. After the liver means that bile is made but cannot drain, because a stone or a tumor is blocking a duct, and the pigment is pushed back into the blood. The distinction matters because the treatment, the urgency and the danger are entirely different in each case.

The color of the urine and the stool together form the most useful piece of information the patient can give, and they are asked for explicitly rather than inferred. Dark urine that stains the water like tea means that processed bilirubin is reaching the urine, which points to the liver or to an obstruction after it. Stools that have become pale, clay-colored or putty-colored mean that bile is not reaching the gut, which points to obstruction or to severe injury of the liver, while normally colored stools make complete obstruction unlikely. In jaundice that begins before the liver, the urine usually stays clear of bilirubin, the stools keep their normal color, and the patient is anemic and may have an enlarged spleen. The clinician also asks about itching, which accompanies cholestasis, about pain, which is more typical of stones and of infection than of tumor, and about fever with chills, which suggests infection behind an obstruction.

The speed and the shape of the jaundice carry as much meaning as its presence, and so does the rest of the history. Jaundice that appears within days of an illness like influenza, deepens rapidly and then begins to fade suggests acute hepatitis, particularly in a young person with dark urine and a tender liver. Jaundice that comes in waves with severe pain suggests a stone passing through the duct, while jaundice that deepens steadily over weeks without pain suggests a growing obstruction, most often a tumor. A patient who has had jaundice before, or who has known gallstones or surgery on the biliary tree, brings a different set of probabilities to the same yellow skin. The clinician also asks about weight loss, a new diagnosis of diabetes, treatment with a medicine that can injure the liver, the use of alcohol, recent travel and sexual history, since each of these can redirect the reasoning.

The examination of the jaundiced patient is a search for the organ that has failed and for the marks of long-standing disease. The liver is assessed by its span from the upper border percussed in the chest to the lower edge, by the character of that edge, by whether the surface is smooth or irregular, by whether it is tender and by whether it moves with breathing. A tender, smooth, enlarged liver suggests acute inflammation, a hard, irregular, non-tender liver suggests scarring or a tumor, and a small shrunken liver suggests advanced disease. The spleen is sought below the left rib margin and described by its size, since an enlarged spleen accompanies the destruction of red cells, infection and the raised pressure of liver disease. The clinician also looks for the marks of chronic liver disease, among them small spider-like vessels on the upper trunk, redness of the palms, enlargement of the breasts in a man, clubbing of the fingers and a coarse tremor of the outstretched hands.

One physical finding deserves its own paragraph, because it points towards a cancer rather than a stone. When jaundice is deep but painless and the gallbladder can be felt as a smooth, rounded, non-tender swelling below the right rib margin, the obstruction is more likely to be a slowly growing tumor of the head of the pancreas or of the bile ducts than a stone. The reasoning is that a stone usually scars the gallbladder and prevents it from distending, while a tumor obstructs gradually and allows the organ to fill. This is a tendency and not a law, so it should raise the probability of a tumor rather than settle the diagnosis. The findings to seek with it are weight loss, a mass in the upper abdomen or a recent diagnosis of diabetes, and where imaging exists such a patient needs it promptly.

Cholangitis is the infection that develops when the biliary tree is obstructed and bacteria multiply behind the block, and it can progress to a life-threatening illness within hours. The classical pattern is fever with chills, jaundice and pain in the right upper abdomen, and a later description adds a falling blood pressure and confusion. No single part of that pattern is required for the diagnosis, and a patient who presents with fever and jaundice alone may already be seriously ill. The clinician should therefore treat any patient with jaundice and a temperature as potentially infected until proven otherwise, and should look deliberately for the signs of shock, which include a rapid pulse, cool hands and feet and a reduced volume of urine. A delay in relieving the obstruction is the delay that kills these patients, and the decision to move them is a clinical one made at the bedside.

Obstructive jaundice deserves a separate caution because its benign and its malignant causes look alike at first. A stone in the common duct causes pain and often infection. A tumor of the head of the pancreas, of the bile ducts or of the ampulla where the ducts enter the bowel causes jaundice that is usually painless, progressive and unrelenting, and it is the reason that obstructive jaundice in an older adult must be treated as a malignancy until it is excluded. The patient may also describe itching, pale stools, dark urine, weight loss and a new and unexplained diabetes, and the gallbladder may be palpable below the rib margin. The clinician who assumes a stone because stones are common will occasionally be wrong in the way that matters most, so keep both possibilities visible until the evidence separates them.

Jaundice that begins before the liver is the least common of the three groups, and it is recognized by the combination of anemia, an enlarged spleen and a liver that is normal or only mildly abnormal. The pigment is produced faster than the liver can handle, and the patient may be pale, tired and short of breath rather than deeply yellow. The causes include inherited diseases of the red cell, destruction of red cells by antibodies, a reaction to a medicine, and infection with a parasite such as malaria. The urine stays clear of bilirubin because the pigment has not yet been processed by the liver, and the stools keep their normal color for the same reason.

Several patients deserve special caution because the usual signs mislead in them. The older patient may be taking a medicine that prevents the heart rate from rising, so the tachycardia that would have warned the clinician never appears. The trained athlete may have a resting pulse far lower than the average, so a rate that would alarm most adults looks normal in him, and the direction of change matters more than the absolute number. The patient with dark skin may show jaundice first in the sclera and under the tongue rather than in the skin, and pallor may be difficult to see, so the clinician looks at the conjunctivae, the palms and the lining of the mouth. The patient whose kidneys are failing may not produce the changes in urine output that would otherwise signal a failing circulation, and the pregnant patient, the patient with a disorder of bleeding and the patient whose immune system is suppressed by illness or treatment all change the pace of the decision rather than the principles of it.

In a hospital with a laboratory, imaging and an endoscopy service, the assessment becomes a set of questions asked in a deliberate order. Blood is taken for the count, for the clotting of the blood, for the function of the kidney and the liver and for the group and cross-match that allows blood to be replaced, while the patient is examined and monitored rather than sent away to wait. A tube passed into the stomach can show whether fresh blood is present, and where it is, endoscopy can find the source and treat it during the same procedure. Computed tomography, or CT, is used when the source is not found by endoscopy or when bleeding continues, and it can show a tumor, an abnormal tangle of vessels or blood within the wall of the bowel. The intensive care unit, or ICU, is the place for the patient whose circulation or breathing needs continuous support, and the decision to move such a patient there rests on the whole clinical picture.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch and a thermometer, the same reasoning is compressed into fewer decisions. The clinician counts the pulse and the breathing rate, measures the temperature, records the pressure if a cuff is available, looks at the eyes and the skin in good light, feels the abdomen for a liver, a spleen and free fluid, and performs the rectal examination when a bleed is suspected. The color of the urine can be seen in a clear container, and the color of the stool can be described by the patient and confirmed on the glove. If the patient is bleeding, is pale or sweating, has a rapid pulse or a falling pressure, or is jaundiced with a fever, the task is no longer diagnosis but safe and rapid transfer, with the airway protected, the patient kept warm and lying flat, and nothing given by mouth if an operation may be needed. If the patient is stable and the bleeding has stopped, the task is to record what was found, to arrange the earliest possible assessment and to tell the patient exactly what should bring him back sooner.

A constructed illustration may help to fix the reasoning, and it describes no real person. Imagine an older man with known cirrhosis who arrives with vomiting of fresh blood, a pulse of 120 beats per minute and a pressure of 80 millimeters of mercury, and whose abdomen is distended with fluid and whose spleen is felt below the left rib margin; the working diagnosis is a variceal hemorrhage until it is proven otherwise. Both of those figures are illustrations, and the values that should trigger action must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because they differ with age, with physical fitness and with the definition being used. Now imagine a woman of middle age whose skin has turned yellow over several weeks, whose stools have become pale, whose urine is dark and who has lost weight without pain; the reasoning points towards obstruction and towards a tumor rather than towards a stone. In each of these, the point is not the answer but the way one further question moves the order of the possibilities.

The record of a patient who has bled or turned yellow should show what was found, what was considered and what the patient was told to watch for. It should state the color and the amount of any blood, the observations and their timing, the findings of the abdominal and rectal examinations and the reasoning that led to the plan. Where a diagnosis was not established, the note should say so plainly and should name the possibilities that remain, so that the next clinician does not begin from nothing. Safety-netting is part of the diagnosis rather than a courtesy after it, and the patient should be told which symptoms mean return immediately, among them fresh bleeding, vomiting of blood, black stool, fever with jaundice, confusion and pain that becomes severe. The patient should also be told where to go and how quickly, in words that assume no medical knowledge, and the advice should be written down wherever literacy allows.

A small number of errors in reasoning account for most of the harm in these two presentations. The first is to attribute rectal bleeding to hemorrhoids in a patient whose age makes a tumor more likely, and the second is to accept a normal early blood count as proof that a hemorrhage has been small. The third is to attribute painless jaundice to a mild viral illness in a patient whose jaundice is deep, progressive and accompanied by weight loss. The fourth is to treat a patient with a temperature and jaundice as a routine case rather than as a possible infection of the biliary tree. The fifth is to forget that a patient who has had surgery on the aorta can bleed from the graft, and the sixth is to let a single normal examination close the question in a patient who is clearly unwell.

To summarize, bleeding from the gut and jaundice are each an invitation to find the source, to judge the loss, and to name the dangerous possibility before the comfortable one. In bleeding, the appearance of the blood separates an upper from a lower source only roughly, the circulation tells the truth about volume before the blood count does, and a variceal hemorrhage, a perforated ulcer and a bleeding aortic graft must be excluded early. In jaundice, the color of the urine and the stool and the character of the liver and spleen place the patient before, within or after the liver, and an obstruction that is painless and progressive is a malignancy until it is shown not to be. Cholangitis is the diagnosis that turns jaundice into an emergency, and it must be recognized from the combination of fever, yellowing and pain rather than awaited with certainty. Every number in this chapter, whether a pulse, a pressure, a volume, a temperature or an interval, is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such figures differ between countries, between editions and between laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision or clinical judgment. In the hospital with endoscopy and in the clinic with a stethoscope and a torch, the question is the same, and the answer that matters most is how fast this patient must travel and to what destination.
