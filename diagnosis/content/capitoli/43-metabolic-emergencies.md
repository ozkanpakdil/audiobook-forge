# Chapter 43 — Metabolic Emergencies

The metabolic emergency is a disturbance of the body's chemistry severe enough to threaten the brain, the heart, or the breathing, and it is dangerous because it is both easily missed and often reversible. What organizes this chapter is one habit, which is that the bedside question of whether the chemistry has gone wrong is asked before the comfortable explanations are accepted, because drowsiness, weakness, and confusion can come from a stroke, an infection, or a number nobody measured. The reasoning runs from the history to a few bedside observations and only then to the laboratory, which confirms rather than replaces the clinical idea. This chapter is educational material only, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

Four dangers deserve naming before the ordinary reasoning begins, each with the finding that betrays it. Hypoglycemia comes first, because it is common, it mimics every neurological presentation, and it can leave lasting damage if it is not found quickly; the revealing finding is a glucose measured at the bedside rather than assumed. A potassium far outside its ordinary range comes second, because it can stop the heart without warning, and the finding is a change in the electrical tracing of the heart or a weakness no other cause explains. The hyperglycemic crises come third, announced by deep sighing breathing, profound thirst and urination, dehydration, and a breath smelling of ketones. Poisoning comes fourth, and it may be the only diagnosis in a patient who was well a few hours earlier; the revealing finding is often a companion who is also unwell, and carbon monoxide is betrayed by more than one person in the same room sharing the same headache and confusion.

The history that carries a metabolic diagnosis is short and specific, and it is asked even when the patient cannot answer. The clinician asks what was last eaten and drunk, whether there has been vomiting or diarrhea, how much urine has been passed, and whether there is unusual thirst. The questions turn to what the patient takes, including medicines for diabetes, water tablets, steroids, and anything bought without a prescription, and whether any of it was missed, doubled, or changed. The account of what was swallowed, when, how much, and with what is the center of every suspected poisoning, and it is asked without accusation or surprise.

The examination is brief and directed, and its purpose is to find the system that is compensating and the one that is failing. The clinician records consciousness, the rate and depth of breathing, the pulse, the pressure, the temperature, and the hydration of skin and mouth. The breath is smelled, the skin is examined for color, moisture, and coolness, the pupils are inspected with the torch, and the muscles are tested for tone and power. The single most useful instrument is a glucose meter, because it answers in a minute a question the rest of the examination can only suggest, and a clinic that has one holds a decisive advantage.

Hypoglycemia must be considered in every patient whose consciousness is altered, whatever the story suggests. The brain depends on a continuous supply of glucose and has almost no store, so a low glucose first changes behavior or awareness, and that change can look like drunkenness, a stroke, a seizure, or exhaustion. Sweating, trembling, hunger, and a racing heart may warn the patient, but those signs can be absent, especially after many years of diabetes when the warning responses fade. A patient can converse normally and become unresponsive shortly afterwards, so one lucid moment proves nothing about the glucose now. A glucose of about 3.5 millimoles per liter is often named as the boundary, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since meters, laboratories, and services draw the line differently.

Hypoglycemia is most likely in those who take medicines that lower the glucose, and the risk rises when the amount changes, a meal is missed, alcohol is taken, or an infection or kidney injury appears. Advanced kidney disease, a day of vomiting, fasting before a procedure, and the small stores of a newborn create vulnerability without any diabetes at all. In a clinic with a glucose meter and no laboratory, this is the first thing excluded in any patient who is drowsy, sweating, fitting, or behaving strangely, because it is the one cause that can be corrected on the spot. If the syndrome disappears when the glucose is restored, the clinician has found the explanation rather than guessed it.

Diabetic ketoacidosis develops when there is not enough insulin for the body to use glucose, so the cells behave as though starving while the blood is full of sugar. The body breaks down fat, and the acids produced accumulate faster than they can be removed, which makes the condition a metabolic acidosis with ketones in the blood and the urine. The history is usually a story of days rather than hours, with increasing thirst, large volumes of urine, weight loss, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain that can suggest a surgical emergency. The patient may know the sugar is high, may have run out of treatment, or may have an infection such as pneumonia or a urinary infection that pushed control out of balance.

On examination the patient is usually dehydrated, with a dry mouth and reduced skin turgor, and the eyes may be sunken in a child or an older person. The breathing is deep and sighing rather than rapid and shallow, the body's attempt to blow off acid, so it is a sign of severity rather than comfort. A breathing rate of 30 breaths per minute in an adult is often used as a marker of that severity, yet the figure must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the rate that should alarm depends on age, setting, and the definition in use. The breath may smell sweet and fruity of ketones, though its absence proves nothing, and a fever should always be sought, because infection is the usual trigger even when no fever appears in a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed.

The most dangerous part of ketoacidosis is not the sugar but the potassium, because the total amount in the body falls even while the measured level may be high or normal. A potassium of 6 millimoles per liter in this setting is often treated as an emergency, but that figure is an illustration rather than a universal law and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because services and editions set the point of alarm at different levels. The potassium has moved out of the cells as the acid rose, so a normal or raised reading hides a serious deficit that will declare itself as treatment moves it back. Abdominal pain in ketoacidosis is a great mimic, because it can be severe, provoke vomiting, and settle as the acidosis is corrected, yet it can also be a genuine surgical emergency; pain that worsens as the chemistry improves belongs to a surgeon rather than to the glucose meter.

Several conditions produce the same acidotic picture, and each has a distinguishing feature. Starvation ketosis follows a long period without food, particularly in a child or a pregnant woman, and usually produces milder acidosis with a low or normal sugar. Alcoholic ketoacidosis follows a stretch of heavy drinking with poor eating, suggested by vomiting, abdominal pain, and a sugar that is not markedly raised. Lactic acidosis arises when tissues receive too little oxygen, as in shock, sepsis, or a failing heart, so the pressure, the color of the skin, and the urine carry more information than the sugar. Kidney failure accumulates its own acid, suggested by a long kidney history, a reduced urine output, and a swollen rather than dehydrated patient.

Two poisonings belong in this list because they are easily mistaken for a metabolic disease. Methanol and ethylene glycol are alcohols that are not the ordinary drinking alcohol, and both produce a severe acidosis with a raised gap between the measured ions; methanol suggests itself through visual disturbance, and ethylene glycol through flank pain and later kidney injury. Salicylate poisoning can produce fast deep breathing with an acid base balance that moves in either direction, and the patient may sweat, vomit, and hear ringing in the ears. A raised gap also appears in kidney failure, lactic acidosis, and ketoacidosis, so it raises a question rather than answering one, and the interval after which it is safe to discharge such a patient must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol for that substance.

Hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state is the other great hyperglycemic emergency and behaves differently in ways that matter at the bedside. It tends to appear in an older patient with type 2 diabetes, often with an infection or a stretch of poor drinking, and it develops more slowly, so the patient may be profoundly dehydrated and drowsy when help is sought. The sugar is usually higher than in ketoacidosis while ketones are absent or minimal, because there is enough insulin to prevent fat breakdown but not enough to control glucose. A glucose above 30 millimoles per liter is commonly quoted alongside this state, and that figure is only an illustration, so verify it against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the boundary differs between editions and laboratories. The danger is the extreme loss of water from the circulation, which can lead to clots, kidney injury, and a falling level of consciousness, and the signs of stroke or infection must be sought at the same time.

Electrolytes are the charged particles on which nerves, muscles, and the heart depend, and their disturbances cause symptoms because the electrical behavior of cells depends on the difference between the inside and the outside of the membrane. A small change in potassium, calcium, magnesium, or sodium can therefore produce weakness, cramp, an irregular heartbeat, or a seizure, and the same change can cause no symptoms until the moment it becomes dangerous. The blood level is only a partial guide, because it does not show what is stored in the cells, so a serious total deficit can sit behind a normal reading. The clinician should ask what the patient has lost, what the patient has taken, and what the kidneys are doing, because those three questions explain most electrolyte emergencies before any result returns.

Potassium is the electrolyte whose disturbance most often kills quickly, because the rhythm of the heart depends on it and the first symptom can be a cardiac arrest. A high potassium is suggested by weakness climbing from the legs, a slow or irregular pulse, numbness and tingling, and a change in the electrical tracing; kidney failure, medicines that hold potassium in the body, and severe muscle injury are the usual settings. A low potassium is suggested by cramp, fatigue, constipation, a fast pulse, and, in the worst cases, weakness of the muscles of breathing, and it usually follows vomiting, diarrhea, or water tablets. The electrical tracing may be the first place a dangerous potassium is seen and in some patients the only clue, so an electrocardiogram should be recorded when the possibility is serious. The tracing can also look deceptively ordinary, so a normal tracing does not settle the question, and the figure that demands urgent action is stated differently by different services and must be measured against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Calcium and magnesium come next, because they govern the excitability of nerve and muscle and a disturbance of one often accompanies the other. A low calcium produces tingling around the mouth and in the hands and feet, cramp, a spasm of the hand when a pressure cuff is inflated, a twitch of the face when the facial nerve is tapped, and in severe cases a seizure or a spasm of the airway. It appears in disease of the parathyroid glands, severe inflammation of the pancreas, long-standing kidney failure, and after major illness, and a low magnesium will keep the calcium low until it is corrected. A high calcium produces thirst, frequent urination, constipation, drowsiness, and confusion, and is a cause of an altered mental state easily attributed to age. The clinician should test for these signs with the pressure cuff and the tapping finger, because they cost nothing, and should confirm the levels that define danger against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Sodium disturbance is the electrolyte problem that most often presents as a change in the mind, and the speed of the change matters more than the number itself. A sodium that has fallen slowly may produce headache, nausea, confusion, unsteadiness, and a fall, while the same level reached quickly can cause a seizure, a coma, and swelling of the brain. A risen sodium produces thirst, weakness, and confusion, and in the extreme a shrinkage of brain cells that pulls on the vessels within the skull. The causes come from the history and the examination, since vomiting and diarrhea lose salt and water while heart, liver, and kidney failure retain water, and some medicines shift the balance silently. The clinician must ask about weight change, dryness of mouth and skin, fullness of the neck veins, and swelling of the legs, because those findings divide the causes, and the rate at which a sodium may safely be corrected must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Poisoning is a diagnostic subject in its own right, approached with the discipline of any other emergency rather than as a separate specialty. The clinician asks what was taken, when, how much, and what else was taken with it, because the combination often matters more than any single substance. The questions turn to how it entered the body, whether swallowed, breathed, injected, or absorbed through the skin, and whether the exposure was single or repeated. The reason should be asked about with care, because a deliberate overdose carries a risk of a further attempt and different needs from an accidental one. The clinician also asks what the patient ate and drank, whether a container can be read, and whether anyone else is affected, since a shared exposure points to a hazard in the home or the workplace, and a reported amount is often only an estimate made under stress.

The examination of the poisoned patient is a survey of what the substance has done rather than a hunt for its name. The clinician records the rate and depth of breathing, the pulse, the pressure, and the temperature, because the pattern of those signs often points to the class of poison. The pupils are examined with the torch, the skin is inspected for moisture, color, rash, and needle marks, and the mouth is examined for burns, smell, and fragments of a tablet. A temperature above 39 degrees Celsius, or a fall far below the ordinary range, is a reason to seek urgent help, and the figure that should alarm must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted boundary shifts with age and with the condition measured.

Toxidromes are patterns of observable signs that suggest a class of substance, and they are described here by what the clinician can see rather than by a list of antidotes. The patient who is drowsy or unresponsive with pinpoint pupils, slow breathing, and a normal or slow pulse fits the pattern of opioid excess, and the finding that confirms it is the reversal of the picture when the effect is blocked. The patient who is drowsy but easily roused, slurs words, and has normal pupils fits the pattern of a sedative, where the danger is loss of the airway and aspiration of stomach contents. The patient who smells of alcohol may also have a head injury, a low glucose, or a metabolic disturbance, so intoxication is a reason to look further and never a reason to stop. A recognized pattern narrows the search but is not a diagnosis and does not exclude a second poison or a coexisting illness.

Three further patterns are worth fixing in memory because each has a characteristic appearance at the bedside. The agitated, sweaty, hot patient with a fast pulse and wide pupils fits the pattern of a stimulant, and the danger is a rising temperature, a seizure, and an irregular heart rhythm. The flushed, dry patient with wide pupils, a full bladder, and a confused or hallucinating mind fits the pattern of an anticholinergic substance, and the dry skin separates it from the sweating patient. The patient who is drooling and tearing, with small pupils, a slow pulse, a wheeze, vomiting, diarrhea, and incontinence of urine and stool, fits the pattern of a cholinergic poison such as an agricultural insecticide, one of the few true emergencies of poisoning because the breathing can fail quickly.

Carbon monoxide deserves its own place because it kills without warning and because ordinary instruments mislead. It is produced by any fire burning without enough air, such as a stove, a heater, or a charcoal fire in a closed room, and it binds to the oxygen carrier of the blood far more tightly than oxygen, so tissues are starved while the blood looks normal. The patient complains of headache, nausea, dizziness, and difficulty thinking, and may be found drowsy or unresponsive, and the revealing finding is often another person or a pet in the same space with the same symptoms. The cherry red skin color sometimes described is unreliable and often absent, and a pulse oximeter can read a normal saturation in a patient who is seriously poisoned, because the device cannot tell the poisoned carrier from the healthy one. When this poisoning is suspected, the patient must be removed from the source and given oxygen while help is summoned, and the decision about further treatment belongs to a service with the right facilities.

Some poisons do their harm long after the first effects have passed, and these are the exposures most easily sent home too early. A common pain medicine taken in a large amount can injure the liver after a delay of a day or more while the patient feels only mildly unwell, and the finding that identifies the injury is a rise in the liver tests rather than the patient's appearance. Certain mushrooms produce vomiting and diarrhea that settle before the liver fails, and that apparent improvement is the most dangerous moment. Methanol and ethylene glycol act after the substance has been converted into a toxic product, so a patient who looks well in the first hours may be in the calm before a severe acidosis. Iron and some other substances injure the stomach and the liver in stages, and the interval after which a patient is safe to discharge depends on the substance and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The treatment decision in a metabolic or toxic emergency follows the same reasoning as any other, and this book names the decision rather than the medicine. Treatment is the right answer when a dangerous level of a reversible substance is present or rising, when there is organ injury, or when the patient is deteriorating and the diagnosis is secure enough to act on. Treatment is the wrong answer when the exposure was trivial and the patient is well, when another cause explains the picture entirely, and when acting before the relevant samples are taken would destroy the evidence the diagnosis depends on. In poisoning the clinician must ask whether reversing the effect will conceal the signs that show the poison is still acting, because a patient who looks better after an antidote may still be absorbing what was swallowed. The finding that settles the question is the direction of the patient over the next hour rather than a single reading.

In a hospital the laboratory turns the clinical idea into measurements, and each measurement should answer a question the clinician has already asked. Glucose is measured first and confirmed by the laboratory when the meter reading is inconsistent with the patient, and the salts allow the gap between the measured ions to be calculated, which points toward the class of acidosis. Kidney function, liver tests, and the blood count identify the injured organ and the infection that may have started the whole disturbance. The acidity of the blood and the gases within it show whether the breathing is compensating and how well the lungs work, and the ketones confirm the ketoacidotic process. The blood gas deserves to be read as a story, asking whether the blood is acid or alkaline, whether the change comes from breathing or metabolism, and whether the body is compensating as expected, while remembering that a patient can carry two disturbances at once and that the figures defining a normal gas must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The patient who seems to improve can be the patient in the most danger, and this pattern appears in almost every metabolic emergency. A corrected glucose may leave a brain injury that the sugar cannot undo, and a ketoacidosis that is improving may be followed by a fall in potassium or by swelling of the brain, particularly in a child. A poisoned patient may pass through an apparent calm between the first effects and the delayed injury to the liver, the kidney, or the brain, and the clinician reassured by that calm may send the patient away too soon. A patient whose breathing slows because the acidosis is corrected is improving, while one whose breathing slows because the effort of compensating is exhausted is deteriorating, and only repeated observations with their direction can separate the two.

In a clinic whose equipment is a stethoscope, a torch, a thermometer, and a glucose meter, the same reasoning is carried by the history and a few bedside observations. Glucose is measured in every patient who is drowsy, confused, fitting, sweating, or unwell, and it is measured again after any correction, because one reading can mislead and the direction of change is what matters. The urine can be tested for ketones where a strip is available, and the torch makes the pupils, mouth, skin, and needle marks visible. The clinic's decisions are made earlier than the hospital's, because a low glucose that corrects fully with a clear cause may be observed and fed with an explicit plan for review. Deep sighing breathing with ketones, a suspected poisoning with drowsiness or slow breathing, or a weakness suggesting a dangerous electrolyte disturbance means the journey must begin now, and hypoglycemia must be excluded before any other explanation is accepted.

The record should show the reasoning and not only the results. It should contain the time of each glucose measurement, what the patient was doing then, what was given and why, and what changed afterwards, so that the next clinician sees a direction rather than a point. It should name the poisons considered and those set aside, with the reason, because a recorded suspicion can be revisited and a forgotten one is lost. The patient sent home must be told exactly what should bring him back, including drowsiness, confusion, vomiting that will not stop, a return of thirst and urination, or worsening weakness, and a follow-up visit arranged in advance is worth more than a promise to return if things worsen.

A constructed illustration may fix these habits, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine an older man brought to a clinic because he has become drowsy and stopped eating for a few days, with a dry mouth, a fast pulse, and a glucose meter reading far above the ordinary range while his urine tests free of ketones. Those figures are illustrations of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the values that define a hyperglycemic emergency differ between editions and laboratories. The absence of ketones does not make him safe, because his danger lies in the water he has lost and the infection that may have started the decline, so the decision is transport. Now imagine a young woman found unresponsive at home with a slow pulse, pinpoint pupils, shallow breathing, and a low glucose, in whom both explanations must be addressed at once; neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment.

To summarize, the metabolic emergency is a disturbance of the body's chemistry that threatens the brain, the heart, or the breathing, and it is dangerous because it is both easily missed and often reversible. The causes that must be named early are hypoglycemia in every patient with altered consciousness, a potassium far outside its ordinary range, the hyperglycemic crises, and poisoning. The reasoning begins with the history of food, fluid, urine, medicines, alcohol, and what was swallowed, continues with a brief examination and a glucose at the bedside, and only then turns to the laboratory, which in the hospital and the clinic alike confirms rather than replaces the clinical idea. Diabetic ketoacidosis and its mimics, the hyperosmolar state, and the electrolyte disturbances that cause weakness, arrhythmia, or seizure are separated by the story and by repeated observation rather than by a single result. Poisoning is a diagnostic subject in its own right, approached through the questions of what, when, how much, and with what, and its toxidromes are read as observable patterns rather than a list of antidotes. Every figure in this chapter, whether a level, a rate, a pressure, a temperature, a gap, or an interval, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories.
