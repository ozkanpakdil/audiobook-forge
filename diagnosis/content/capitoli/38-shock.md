# Chapter 38 — Shock

Shock is the state in which the circulation no longer delivers enough oxygen and fuel to the tissues, and it is diagnosed at the bedside rather than in the laboratory. It is not the same as a low blood pressure, although the two often travel together, and a patient may be in shock while the cuff still shows an acceptable figure. The reasoning is mechanical, because every cause belongs to one of four mechanisms: the container has lost its contents, the vessels have opened too wide, the pump has failed, or the outflow is blocked. Those mechanisms produce different findings, and the findings are what separate them in the first minutes. The urgency is identical in all of them, because tissue that is not perfused begins to die, and the patient who is compensating well may be closest to stopping. This chapter is educational material, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

The lethal causes deserve to be named before the ordinary reasoning, each with the finding that would reveal it. Hemorrhage is the most common, and the finding that betrays it is a rising pulse with a falling pressure in a patient who has a source of bleeding, or a source concealed in the chest, the abdomen, the pelvis, the thigh, or the gut. Tension pneumothorax gives a sudden breathlessness, absent breath sounds on one side, a hollow note when that side is tapped, a windpipe pushed away, and visibly distended neck veins. Cardiac tamponade gives a falling pressure, distended neck veins, and heart sounds that are muffled, which is why a penetrating wound near the heart is never watched quietly. Pulmonary embolism may present as a sudden collapse with a clear chest, a fast pulse, poor oxygen levels, and a pressure that will not hold. Anaphylaxis arrives within minutes of an exposure, with swollen lips, a hoarse voice, a rash, a wheeze, and a pressure that falls while the skin may first feel warm.

Perfusion is not the same as pressure, and confusing the two is the central error this chapter exists to prevent. Blood pressure is the product of how much blood the heart pushes out and how much resistance the vessels offer, so a patient can defend a normal figure by narrowing every vessel while the tissues beyond are starved. When cells are deprived of oxygen they switch to a wasteful form of metabolism, producing lactate and acid. This is why confusion, a rising breathing rate, and a shrinking urine output may appear before the pressure changes at all. The skin, the brain, and the kidney complain first, because the body sacrifices them in that order to protect the heart and the brainstem. What the examiner is really looking for is the direction of travel, whether the patient is holding the circulation together or beginning to lose it.

Blood pressure is still worth measuring carefully, because its errors are more often errors of technique than of interpretation. The cuff must fit the arm, the arm must rest at the level of the heart, and the patient should be still before the reading. A systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury is widely taught as the point at which shock must be assumed, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the value that should trigger action differs with age, pregnancy, and usual baseline. The more useful comparison is with the patient's own normal pressure, which is why asking about previous readings belongs to the examination rather than to a history that may be skipped. A pressure that has fallen by 40 millimeters of mercury from a known usual value matters even when the new figure still looks respectable, and that comparison must also be checked against the local standard, since the threshold for concern varies between services. Both arms should be measured when the first reading is unexpected, and the reading should be repeated before any decision rests on it.

The pulse pressure is the difference between the systolic and the diastolic figure, and it carries information that neither figure carries alone. A narrow pulse pressure suggests that the heart is ejecting a small volume into a tightly constricted circulation, which is the pattern of hemorrhage, dehydration, and tamponade. A wide pulse pressure suggests that the vessels are open too wide, which is the pattern of the early septic patient and of some forms of anaphylaxis. The character of the pulse matters as much as its rate, because a thready pulse barely felt under the fingers is different from a full bounding one, and both may be fast. The normal resting pulse for an adult is often quoted as between 60 and 100 beats per minute, but that range is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it shifts with age, fitness, medicines, and pregnancy. The clinician should record the rate, the rhythm, the volume, and the patient's own baseline together, because a rate that is normal for one person may alarm another.

Capillary refill is the cheapest perfusion test in medicine, and it is performed by pressing the skin over the sternum, or a fingertip, until the color blanches, then releasing and counting how long the color takes to return. A return within about 2 seconds is usually taken as reassuring, while a delay beyond 3 seconds suggests that the periphery is being starved, but those figures are illustrations of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the timing depends on the room, the age of the patient, and the method used. The central sites, the sternum and the forehead, are more reliable than the fingers, which are affected by cold and by narrowing of the arteries. The sign becomes far stronger when it is joined to the temperature of the skin and the mental state, because no single bedside finding is sufficient by itself. The examiner should also look for mottling over the knees and the shins, which is a patchy blue and white discoloration that appears when blood is redirected away from the surface.

The temperature and moisture of the skin divide the causes of shock into two broad families. A cold, clammy patient with pale or mottled skin and cool knees has a circulation that is tightly shut down, and that pattern belongs to hemorrhage, dehydration, a failing heart, and the obstructive causes. A warm patient with flushed skin, wide vessels, and a bounding pulse has a circulation that has opened too wide, and that pattern belongs to the septic, the anaphylactic, and the neurogenic causes. The distinction is a starting point rather than a verdict, because sepsis often begins warm and turns cold as the patient deteriorates. A patient with a failing heart may have cool limbs and a high jugular venous pressure, while a patient who is bleeding has cool limbs and a low one, so the neck veins separate the two. The clinician who feels the hands and the knees and compares them with the trunk is gathering this information with no instrument at all.

The patient who is compensating well is the patient most easily lost, because the numbers remain acceptable while the reserve is spent. The first response to a falling circulating volume is a rising pulse and a tightening of the peripheral vessels, and the pressure is defended until that compensation fails. A young, fit adult can lose a large proportion of the blood volume, often quoted as around 30 per cent, before the systolic pressure falls, but that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The findings that announce the approaching failure are the ones the numbers hide: anxiety or drowsiness, a climbing breathing rate, a falling urine output, cool mottled skin, and a narrowing pulse pressure. The clinician who records these observations at intervals and compares them will see the change before the pressure admits it. A normal pressure in a patient who looks unwell is a reason to examine again, not a reason to relax.

The history is taken quickly and often from someone other than the patient, because confusion is one of the earliest effects of poor perfusion. A family member or the person who brought the patient may know what happened, what medicines are taken, and what the patient was doing in the hours before. The speed of the onset is the first question, because a collapse in seconds suggests an embolism, a rhythm disturbance, a tamponade, or an allergic reaction, while a decline over hours suggests bleeding, infection, or dehydration. The next questions concern loss: blood from any site, vomiting, diarrhea, heavy menstrual bleeding, a pregnancy that may lie outside the uterus, or a recent procedure. Then come the questions about the chest, the breathing, the abdomen, and the urine, and finally those about medicines, allergies, and previous illness. The clinician should ask directly about a possible pregnancy in any woman of childbearing age, because that answer changes the differential within seconds, and the account should be written down with the time it was given.

The examination follows a fixed order so that nothing important is skipped and nothing important is delayed. The clinician begins at the end of the bed with the general appearance, the work of breathing, the level of alertness, and the color of the skin. The pulse, the pressure, the breathing rate, and the temperature are then measured and written down with the time, followed by the temperature gradient from trunk to knee. The neck is examined for the jugular venous pressure, which is the single most useful bedside sign for separating a pump problem from a volume problem. A collapsed venous pressure points toward lost filling, or toward vessels that have opened too wide, while a raised one points toward a pump that cannot move what it receives, or toward a physical obstruction to filling. The chest is examined for breath sounds and dullness, the heart for murmurs and muffled tones, and the abdomen for distension, tenderness, and a pulsatile mass, and every wound and line is inspected directly.

The examiner should also look for the pulsus paradoxus, an exaggerated fall in the systolic pressure during inspiration, which is sought by watching the gauge or feeling the pulse while the patient breathes quietly. Two bedside maneuvers then ask whether the heart can use more filling, and both are interpreted as tests rather than treatments. In the passive leg raise the legs are lifted while the trunk stays flat, which briefly returns blood to the heart and produces a measurable change in the pulse or the pressure. A rise in the pressure, or a fall in the pulse, suggests a circulation that is short of volume and can respond to it, while no change suggests that the pump or the obstruction is the problem. The same question can be asked with a small fluid challenge, and the finding that makes filling the right answer is a clear improvement in the pressure and in the signs of perfusion. The findings that make it the wrong answer are a rise in the jugular venous pressure, new crackles, or worsening breathlessness, and no quantity or rate of fluid belongs in this chapter.

Hypovolemic shock is the mechanism in which the container has lost its contents, and it is divided by what has been lost. Blood loss is the most urgent form, and it may be visible or concealed, with the concealed sites being the chest, the abdominal cavity, the space behind the peritoneum, the pelvis, the thigh, and the gut. Water and salt loss occurs with prolonged vomiting, diarrhea, heavy sweating, and the passage of large volumes of dilute urine, and the findings are a fast thready pulse, cool pale skin, delayed refill, a narrow pulse pressure, thirst, and a falling urine output. A patient who has bled into the abdomen may show nothing more than a rising pulse, a falling pressure, and a distended tender abdomen, with no wound anywhere on the skin. Bleeding into the chest gives breathlessness and dullness at the lung base, a fracture of the thigh bone can hold a large volume inside the limb, and a patient taking a medicine that prevents clotting may bleed severely from a source that would otherwise stop. The finding that reveals concealed bleeding is a patient with the signs of shock and no visible reason for them, and a normal blood count early after acute bleeding proves nothing.

Tension pneumothorax is an obstructive cause in which air collects in the chest under pressure and prevents the heart from filling. It occurs after trauma, after a procedure on the chest, in a patient with lung disease, and sometimes in a patient being ventilated by machine. The findings are a sudden worsening of breathing, absent breath sounds on one side, a hollow note when that side is tapped, neck veins that are visibly distended, and a windpipe pushed toward the opposite side. The pressure falls, the pulse rises, and the patient becomes agitated and then drowsy as the heart is compressed. The finding that makes the diagnosis is the combination of a distressed patient, one silent side of the chest, and a rising venous pressure, and the decision is to release the trapped air before any image is taken, because waiting for a chest radiograph here risks the patient's life. The finding that would make that decision wrong is a chest that is dull and quiet because of fluid rather than air, and in a clinic far from imaging the same signs and the same decision apply.

Cardiac tamponade occurs when fluid or blood collects in the sac around the heart and prevents it from filling. It follows penetrating wounds of the chest, heart surgery, inflammation of the pericardium, cancer, and kidney failure. The classical findings are a falling pressure, distended neck veins, and heart sounds that are muffled or difficult to hear. The pulse may fall noticeably during inspiration, the heart may look large on an image, and the tracing on an electrocardiogram, or ECG, may suggest that the heart is being compressed. The finding that confirms it at the bedside is fluid or blood seen around the heart on an ultrasound examination, and the decision is to drain that collection urgently, which belongs to a clinician trained and equipped to do it. The finding that would make drainage the wrong answer is a different cause of the same signs, such as a tension pneumothorax, and every figure associated with these signs must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Pulmonary embolism occurs when a clot travels from a vein, usually in the leg or the pelvis, and lodges in the arteries of the lung. It may present as a sudden breathlessness, a sharp chest pain, a cough with blood, a fainting episode, or a cardiac arrest, and the examination may find a clear chest despite severe breathlessness. The findings that point to it are a fast pulse, poor oxygen levels in the blood, a raised jugular venous pressure, and signs of strain on the right side of the heart. The risk factors belong in the history: recent surgery, immobility, a long journey, cancer, pregnancy, the period after childbirth, a previous clot, and a medicine that prevents clotting which has recently been stopped. The finding that reveals it in a well-resourced setting is an image of the lung arteries, and at the bedside it is an ultrasound showing a struggling right ventricle or a clot in a large vein. The decision to break up the clot with treatment belongs to the clinician who can weigh the confirmation against the risk of bleeding, and the finding that would make that treatment wrong is active bleeding or a recent operation that cannot tolerate it.

Anaphylaxis is a distributive cause that unfolds within minutes of an exposure, and it is unique among these emergencies because it is treated on the clinical picture and not on a test. It follows a sting, a food, a medicine, a vaccine, or contact with latex, and it may follow an exposure the patient has tolerated many times before. The findings are a sudden rash or flushing, swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat, a hoarse voice, a wheeze, vomiting and abdominal cramps, and a pressure that falls while the skin is still warm. The clinician should look for swelling in the airway before anything else, because a patient whose voice has changed or who is struggling to swallow is losing the airway while the pressure is still being measured. The finding that makes treatment the right answer is a sudden illness with skin, airway, breathing, or circulatory signs after a plausible exposure, and the decision is made at once, because waiting to see whether the reaction is severe enough can be fatal. The finding that would make treatment the wrong answer is a clear alternative explanation, such as a fainting episode with no skin or airway signs, although anaphylaxis can occur without any rash at all.

Septic shock is the most common distributive cause, and it is the subject of the next chapter, so only its place in this reasoning is described here. It occurs when the response to an infection damages the circulation and the organs, and the earliest sign is often a warm periphery, a wide pulse pressure, and a fast pulse while the pressure is still held. As the process advances the periphery turns cold and mottled, the pressure falls, the breathing rate climbs, and the urine output shrinks. The source may be anywhere, and the hunt for it includes the lungs, the urine, the abdomen, the skin, the joints, the lines and tubes in the body, and the heart valves. A patient who is older, who is receiving treatment that suppresses the immune response, or who has recently had surgery may show none of the classical signs, and a new confusion may be the only clue. The finding that makes the diagnosis likely is infection together with evidence that an organ is failing, and the decision to treat before the organism is known is made on that picture rather than on a single laboratory value.

Cardiogenic shock is the mechanism in which the pump has failed, and its most common cause is a heart attack that has damaged a large part of the muscle. The findings are a cold clammy patient with a raised jugular venous pressure, crackles at the bases of the lungs, a fast pulse, and a low pressure that does not improve when the patient is filled. The pattern may be confused with hypovolemic shock, and the venous pressure and the lung sounds are the findings that separate them, because a patient who is bleeding has a flat neck and a clear chest. A new murmur in this setting suggests that a valve, or the wall between the chambers, has ruptured, and it changes the urgency of the surgical decision. Other causes include a rhythm disturbance that is too fast or too slow, inflammation of the heart muscle, a failing valve, and the final stage of long-standing heart disease. The finding that makes support of the pump the right answer is a heart that fails to empty despite adequate filling, while the finding that makes it the wrong answer is a circulation that is empty and simply needs volume.

The remaining mechanisms are less common but must be held in mind, because each has a specific answer. Neurogenic shock follows an injury to the spinal cord, and it produces a low pressure with a slow pulse and warm dry skin, which is the opposite of the fast pulse of hemorrhage, so the pulse is the finding that separates them. Adrenal crisis occurs in a patient whose adrenal glands cannot respond to stress, often after long treatment with steroids is stopped, and it produces a pressure that refuses to rise despite filling, with nausea, weakness, low sugar, and disturbed salt balance. A child, a pregnant woman, and an older patient each change the usual signs, because the child defends the pressure until collapse, the pregnant woman has more blood to lose before the pressure speaks, and the older patient may take medicines that blunt the response. In all of them the trend, the skin, the mental state, and the urine output carry more weight than a single measurement.

In a well-resourced hospital the same reasoning is supported by instruments, and they change the confidence of the answer rather than its logic. A catheter placed in an artery gives a continuous pressure reading and allows the examiner to see the pulse pressure breath by breath, which is useful when the cuff reading is unreliable. An ultrasound examination of the heart at the bedside is the most informative single test in this chapter, because it shows whether the chambers are full or empty, whether the muscle is contracting, whether a valve has failed, whether fluid surrounds the heart, and whether the right side is strained. Repeated measures of lactate and blood gases, a catheter in the bladder, and tests of kidney function, liver function, blood sugar, and salt balance show which organs have suffered, while cultures show what organism is responsible when infection is the cause. Every reference range and every figure from the laboratory must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the values differ between laboratories and the units differ between countries.

In a clinic whose equipment is a stethoscope, a torch, a thermometer, and a watch, the same differentiation is made with the same reasoning and fewer numbers. The watch allows the pulse and the breathing rate to be counted rather than estimated, and the count is repeated so that the trend becomes visible. The thermometer turns an impression of fever or of cold into a figure that can be compared over time, and the torch makes the mouth, the throat, the skin, the eyes, and the nails visible. The blood pressure cuff remains one of the most powerful tools in the room, provided the cuff fits and the technique is repeated, and the skin of the knees, the temperature of the hands, the refill over the sternum, and the alertness of the patient supply the rest of the picture. A patient with cold mottled skin, a rising breathing rate, a falling pressure, a change in alertness, or a suspected concealed loss of blood must travel, and the journey should begin before the picture becomes unmistakable. A patient with warm skin, a steady pulse, and a stable mental state may be watched while the cause is pursued, provided that the review is arranged and the patient can return, and the transfer should carry a clear description of the findings and the time they were recorded.

A constructed illustration may fix these habits, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a young man brought in after a motorcycle crash, who is awake and talking, whose pressure measures close to his usual figure, and whose pulse is 120 beats per minute. The examiner notices that his hands and knees are cool, that the color over his kneecaps is mottled, that his capillary refill is delayed, and that his abdomen is becoming distended and tender. The pressure may still look acceptable, but the direction of the pulse and the skin says that the compensation is nearly spent, and the concealed source is in the abdomen. That figure and that rate are illustrations of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the threshold for alarm varies with age and with the definition in use. The lesson is not the number but the move, because the clinician who examined the skin and the abdomen found what the pressure had not yet admitted.

The whole process in shock is a loop that turns faster than in any other chapter. The clinician forms a mechanism from the history and the first look, predicts the findings it should produce, and examines deliberately to test the prediction. The result either supports the mechanism or forces a revision, and the revision is repeated as often as the patient's condition requires. The findings that matter most are the ones that change, because a pressure that is stable while the skin cools and the breathing climbs is not stability at all. A patient who improves and then worsens is a familiar pattern, and the second decline usually means that the original cause continued quietly while the first problem was being managed. The speed of the loop is set by the patient and not by the ward round, and a patient who is deteriorating earns a shorter interval between examinations.

To summarize, shock is a failure of tissue perfusion rather than a number on a gauge, and it is organized by four mechanisms: a container that has lost its contents, vessels that have opened too wide, a pump that has failed, and an obstruction that prevents filling. The lethal causes are named at once, among them hemorrhage, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary embolism, and anaphylaxis, and each carries a finding that can be sought with the hands and the eyes. The blood pressure is valuable but late, the pulse pressure and the capillary refill add meaning to it, and the temperature of the skin separates the shut-down circulation from the wide-open one. The patient who compensates well is the patient to watch most closely, because the pressure is the last sign to change, especially in the young, the pregnant, the athletic, and the child. Every figure in this chapter, whether a pressure, a rate, a proportion, or a duration, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such values differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and the treatment of shock belongs to the clinician who is with the patient and to the system that supports that clinician.
