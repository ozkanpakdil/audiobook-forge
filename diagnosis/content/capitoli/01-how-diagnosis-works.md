# Chapter 1 — How Diagnosis Works

Diagnosis is not a test result, and it is not a list of diseases learned by heart. It is a cognitive act performed under uncertainty, in which a clinician assembles fragments of a story, a body, and a few measurements into a working explanation of what is wrong with this person at this moment. Before anything else, this book must say plainly what it is. It is educational material only; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is actually with the patient. Nothing spoken here should be used to decide the care of a real person without that judgment, and without the local rules and the current guideline that govern your setting. With that established, we can turn to the reasoning itself.

Clinical thought moves in two modes, and most teaching introduces only one of them. The fast mode is pattern recognition, in which the experienced eye takes in the whole scene at once and an answer arrives before the reasoning can be inspected. The slow mode is hypothetico-deductive, in which a hypothesis is proposed, its consequences are worked out, and findings are sought that would support it or defeat it. Neither mode is the correct one, and neither is a beginner's error or an expert's shortcut. What separates a mature clinician from a merely competent one is not the possession of one mode but the ability to notice which mode is running and to change it on purpose.

The experienced clinician slides between the two modes without ceremony. A familiar story at the start of a clinic is recognized within seconds, and the rest of the encounter becomes a search for the small deviations that would make the familiar answer wrong. A strange story, or a familiar story in an unfamiliar patient, stops the fast mode and forces the slower one to take over. The signal to switch is a kind of discomfort, the nagging sense that something does not quite fit, that the pattern is almost right but not right enough. That discomfort is not weakness, and it deserves to be trained, because it is the only warning the fast mode gives before it misleads you.

Pattern recognition is powerful because it compresses years of experience into an instant. It works because disease does often present in recognizable shapes, and because the human mind is remarkably good at detecting those shapes from incomplete information. The clinician who recognizes a stroke, a measles rash, or a tension pneumothorax in the first seconds has gained time that no laboratory can return. This speed matters most where it is least available, in a crowded clinic or an understaffed emergency department. Yet the same mechanism that produces the instant answer also produces the confident wrong answer, and it does so silently.

The most common way the fast mode fails is not that the pattern is false but that the patient is atypical. Frail older people, infants, pregnant women, and people whose immune defenses are suppressed often present in shapes the textbook did not draw. A constructed illustration makes the point: an older patient with a perforated ulcer whose only complaint is mild indigestion, whose abdomen is soft, and whose temperature is normal, may be recognized as a classic case of nothing at all until the clinician deliberately looks for the signs of peritonitis and shock. The finding that saves this patient is not a memory but a habit, the habit of asking what dangerous thing could hide behind a bland story. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is treated as a fever in some protocols and not in others, so confirm what your own service calls a fever against the current guideline and the local protocol before you rest any reasoning on it.

Pattern recognition also fails when the clinician stops thinking too early. The first plausible explanation tends to close the search, and every later finding is then bent to fit it. This is not stupidity; it is how a mind conserves effort. The remedy is procedural rather than moral: name the working diagnosis out loud, then ask what findings would prove it wrong, and go looking for at least one of them. A clinician who never tries to break his own hypothesis is not being decisive, he is being unprotected.

The alternative to the instant answer is a loop, and the loop is the engine of the whole book. A hypothesis is formed from the story and the first observations. From that hypothesis, a predicted finding is derived: if this is the disease, then this sign or this result should be present. The prediction is tested by examining the patient or by ordering a test, and the hypothesis is then revised in the light of what was found. The loop repeats until the explanation is good enough to act on, or until the clinician decides that it cannot be resolved with the tools at hand.

The discipline of the loop lies in making the prediction before looking. If the clinician examines first and interprets afterwards, the finding is free to mean anything, and it will usually mean whatever the clinician already believed. A prediction made in advance is a small act of self-binding, and it converts an examination into evidence rather than decoration. This is also why a finding that was not predicted is so valuable: it is the one piece of information least contaminated by hope.

Revision is not failure, and a diagnosis that survives contact with new information is stronger for it. When a predicted finding appears, the hypothesis gains support but is not proved, because other diseases can produce the same sign. When the predicted finding is absent, the hypothesis loses support but is not always dead, because the test may have been performed badly or the disease may be early. The clinician who treats every negative as a refutation will abandon true diagnoses, and the clinician who treats every negative as an accident will keep false ones. Judgment lives in between those two errors.

Time is itself a diagnostic instrument, and it is the one that is always available. Many conditions declare themselves not in a single examination but in their trajectory, so a finding that is equivocal now becomes decisive when it is repeated in a few hours. The patient who looks slightly unwell and is unchanged after observation is a different patient from the one who is quietly deteriorating. This is why the interval between observations matters, and why the clinician should record not only what was found but when it was found and what had changed since the last look.

Probability enters the reasoning in plain language rather than in arithmetic. When a clinician says that a diagnosis is likely, unlikely, or possible but must be excluded, he is speaking about how much of the available evidence points that way. The listener does not need to calculate anything; the listener needs to hear that probability is a property of the whole clinical picture and not of any single symptom. A symptom that seems dramatic may be common and harmless, while a quiet detail may be the one that changes everything. Thinking in probabilities means holding several explanations at once and letting the balance move as the evidence accumulates.

Pre-test probability is the name for the estimate a clinician makes before a particular piece of evidence arrives. It comes from the patient's age and sex, the setting, the season, the duration of the complaint, and the clinician's experience of similar patients. A finding never enters an empty mind; it enters a mind that already has a prior, and the same finding moves different priors by different amounts. This is why two clinicians can examine the same abdomen, find the same tenderness, and reach different conclusions, and why the disagreement is often about the prior rather than about the finding.

The prior is heavily shaped by where the patient is sitting. A district clinic, an emergency department, and a referral hospital each admit a different mixture of illness, and the same complaint carries a different weight in each. Chest pain that arises from a strained muscle in a young person in a general clinic may be produced by a clot in the lung in the same young person brought to an emergency department after a long flight. The symptom has not changed; the population has. Diagnosis is therefore always local, and a rule that was derived in one setting must be re-examined before it is trusted in another.

Prevalence is the word for how common a condition is in the group being considered, and it is the single most powerful influence on what a finding means. If a disease is common, a suggestive finding raises its probability a great deal; if the disease is rare, the same finding may leave it rare. In a general clinic, chest pain from a common and benign cause may occur in 1 in 4 people who present that way, while a dangerous cause may be far less frequent. That proportion is an illustration, so confirm the figure for your own population against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the composition of every clinic differs.

The mathematics is usually hidden, but the movement it describes is simple. A pre-test probability can be turned into pre-test odds, and a finding then multiplies those odds by a factor that describes how much the finding shifts belief. The result is post-test odds, which can be turned back into a probability if anyone needs one. The clinician who never performs this calculation still performs the movement, because the whole encounter is a sequence of such multiplications. What matters is knowing the direction and the rough size of each shift, not the arithmetic that formalizes it.

A likelihood ratio is the name for that factor, and it can be understood without a single computation. It answers one question: how much more often is this finding seen in people who have the disease than in people who do not. A finding with a large ratio moves belief strongly towards the disease, a finding with a ratio near one moves it hardly at all, and a finding that is more common in health than in disease moves belief away. The listener who remembers those three sentences has everything the arithmetic would have given, and will use it more often because it costs nothing to recall.

The counter-intuitive case deserves special attention because it misleads so many. A finding can be present in most people with a disease and still be nearly useless, if it is equally present in people without it. A red rash may accompany a serious infection in almost every case, but if the same rash is common in healthy people in the same ward, its appearance changes very little. The question is never whether a sign is common in the disease, but whether it is more common in the disease than in the alternative explanations.

The same logic governs the language of test performance. A test that is positive in nearly everyone with the disease is good at ruling the disease out when it is negative, and a test that is negative in nearly everyone without the disease is good at ruling the disease in when it is positive. Neither property survives a change of population unchanged, which is why a result that is decisive in a referral hospital may be almost meaningless in a screening clinic. The number that describes the test is not a property of the test alone; it is a property of the test in a particular kind of patient.

A test result is not a diagnosis, and a laboratory does not think. It measures, counts, or images something, and it returns a number or a picture to be interpreted in the light of the whole picture. The interpretation is where error enters: a normal result in a patient who is dangerously ill may mean the test was too early, or too insensitive, or performed on the wrong sample. The clinician who orders a test to settle a question must first decide what each possible result would change, and if nothing would change, the test should not be ordered at all.

A diagnosis made at the bedside and a diagnosis confirmed in a laboratory are two different achievements, and both are legitimate. The bedside diagnosis is built from the history and the examination, and it is often accurate enough to start the right decision immediately. The confirmed diagnosis waits for evidence that is independent of the clinician's senses, and it is what allows a disease to be named with confidence, recorded, and communicated. The two are not rivals; the bedside diagnosis decides what to do now, and the confirmation decides what is true.

Confirmation has limits that are easy to forget. A laboratory can only answer the question it was asked, on the sample it was given, at the moment it was taken, and its answer depends on the quality of the whole chain that produced it. A result can be falsely reassuring when the disease is early, and falsely alarming when the patient is not representative of the group in which the test was studied. The clinician who understands this treats results as evidence to be weighed, not as verdicts to be obeyed.

The question is therefore not only what disease is most likely but what disease is most dangerous. The two answers are frequently different, and the difference is where patients are harmed. A common benign explanation may be the most probable, while a rare lethal one is the most consequential, and both must be carried in mind at once. This is why a differential diagnosis is ranked twice, once by likelihood and once by danger, and why the dangerous end of the list is examined first.

Naming the dangerous possibility early is a rule of safety rather than a symptom of anxiety. In chest pain the possibility of a torn aorta or a clot in the lung must be considered alongside the far more common muscular cause, and the finding that would reveal it must be sought deliberately. In headache the possibility of bleeding around the brain must be weighed against tension headache, and in back pain the possibility of compression of the spinal cord against a strained muscle. The clinician who asks what would kill this patient first is not being pessimistic, he is ordering the search correctly.

Red flags are the practical form of that priority. They are findings that, when present, change the acceptable pace of the encounter and sometimes the place of care. Their value is not that they prove a dangerous disease but that they prevent the clinician from relaxing too early. A patient who has no red flags after a deliberate search is in a very different position from a patient who was never asked the questions that would have found them.

The history is the most powerful diagnostic instrument in medicine, and it is available everywhere. It carries more information per minute than any test, and it is the only instrument that can tell the clinician what the patient is afraid of, what has changed, and what the patient has already tried. A history taken well narrows the field before the patient is touched, so that the examination becomes a set of targeted questions rather than a ritual tour of the body. The examiner who listens first examines better.

The examination is applied reasoning, not a checklist performed for its own sake. Each maneuver exists because some disease produces a sign that can be elicited in a particular way, and the clinician who knows the reason will notice the sign and understand its absence. A sign that is sought without a question behind it is easily missed, because the eye sees what the mind is prepared to recognize. The examination should therefore be performed with a short list of hypotheses in mind and a clear idea of what each finding would do to them. This is why the order of the examination can be reorganized around the leading question without losing anything essential.

Single signs are usually weaker than the pattern they belong to. One finding may be suggestive, but several findings that point the same way, or a finding combined with the right history, are far more convincing. This is why a diagnosis assembled from a constellation is more robust than one resting on a memorable detail. It is also why a clinician should be suspicious of a diagnosis that depends entirely on one striking sign.

None of the examples in this book describe a real patient. Where a case is used, it is a constructed illustration built to make a piece of reasoning visible, and it should be read as a teaching device rather than a record. Real patients are messier, more variable, and more surprising than any illustration, and the listener should expect that. The purpose of an illustration is to fix a habit of thought, not to supply a portrait of a disease.

Reasoning with limited resources is not a lesser version of reasoning with many. It is the same reasoning, asked to work with fewer inputs, and it exposes the logic more clearly because nothing can be hidden behind a machine. In a clinic with a stethoscope, a thermometer, and a torch, the clinician must decide which question matters most, which sign is worth the time, and what to do when the answer remains uncertain. Those decisions are the essence of diagnosis, and they are made in every setting, with or without a laboratory. A duration such as 3 days may mark one condition as acute and another as chronic, but the interval that separates them is not fixed, so check the current guideline and the local protocol rather than trusting a remembered figure. The clinician who reasons well with little is not improvising; he is doing exactly what the hospital clinician does, with fewer distractions.

In a well-resourced hospital the clinician can reach for imaging, for a laboratory, and for colleagues. In the clinic at the end of the road the same clinician reaches for a careful history, a systematic examination, and the courage to name uncertainty. The tools differ, and the decisions about referral differ with them, but the questions are identical. A clinician who can diagnose without equipment is not a relic; he is the proof that the method is sound.

The book therefore works on two levels throughout. The first is the principle: why the question is asked, why the sign appears, and what the finding does to the probability of disease. The second is the practice: what is said, done, looked for, and written down, in a hospital with every resource and again in a clinic with almost none. The principle is what makes the practice portable, and the practice is what keeps the principle honest. Listening for both is what allows one book to be useful in two very different rooms.

What transfers between those rooms is reasoning, not equipment. A clinician who has learned to form a hypothesis, predict a finding, test it, and revise is not disabled by the absence of a scanner, because the method does not depend on the scanner. Equipment extends the senses, but it does not supply the questions, the priorities, or the judgment about what the answer means. That is why a good clinician with few tools so often outperforms a poor clinician with many.

Vital signs are the cheapest evidence available at the bedside, and they are often the first thing to contradict a reassuring story. A pulse of 110 beats per minute may be fear, fever, or blood loss, and the rate that should alarm you must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol because it shifts with age, pregnancy, and fitness. A systolic pressure of 90 millimeters of mercury means something different in a trained athlete and in a patient who is normally hypertensive, so treat it as an illustration and confirm the threshold that matters against the current guideline and the local protocol. A respiratory rate that has climbed since the last observation may be the most sensitive sign of all, precisely because it is the one most often estimated rather than counted. The point is not the individual figure but the direction in which the figures are moving.

Equipment can also create a false sense of completeness. A normal computed tomography, or CT, scan of the head does not remove the possibility of a bleed that has not yet declared itself, and a normal blood count does not remove the possibility of serious infection. In the same way, an electrocardiogram, or ECG, may be normal between episodes of a rhythm disturbance, and the intensive care unit, or ICU, with all its monitoring, still relies on a person noticing that the patient looks worse. Machines report what they measure, and the clinician must still decide what the measurement means.

Uncertainty is the medium in which diagnosis is performed, and tolerance of it is a skill. Some clinicians reduce uncertainty by ordering more tests, which often replaces one kind of uncertainty with several new ones. Others reduce it by deciding early, which feels decisive and can be equally expensive for the patient. The mature response is to hold the question open for exactly as long as it needs to be open, and to act decisively when the decision can no longer wait.

Calibration is the name for knowing how much to trust your own judgment. A clinician who is right nine times out of ten when he feels certain, and wrong half the time when he feels uncertain, has useful self-knowledge and can act on it. A clinician whose confidence is unrelated to his accuracy will be dangerous in both directions, because he will neither check what he should nor defer when he should. Calibration is learned from feedback, which is why follow-up and the review of outcomes are part of diagnosis and not an administrative extra.

The patient's own account is the beginning and the correction of every hypothesis. Patients describe their illness in the language they have, and the clinician's first task is to understand what the words point to rather than to translate them too quickly into a diagnosis. Time and again the decisive information arrives in an aside, a hesitation, or a detail the clinician did not ask for. The habit of listening past the first answer is one of the most valuable things a clinician can cultivate.

Documentation is reasoning made visible. What is written down should show not only what was found but what was considered and what was excluded, so that the next clinician can pick up the thread. A record that lists findings without the thinking behind them forces the next reader to start again, and a record that states a diagnosis without the evidence leaves no way to challenge it. The written note is therefore part of the diagnostic process, not a report filed after it.

Safety-netting is the acknowledgment that a diagnosis is a statement about the present, made with imperfect information. The clinician who is not certain must tell the patient what to watch for, where to return if it appears, and when the picture should be reviewed. This is not a substitute for a decision; it is a second decision layered on the first, and it protects the patient when the first decision turns out to be wrong. It is also the point at which the reasoning meets the patient's own judgment and daily life.

The same method that diagnoses a patient also teaches a clinician. Each encounter is an experiment in which a prediction was made and an outcome followed, and the clinician who notices the gap between the two improves. Reflection on cases, including the ones that went well, is how the fast mode is built and how its errors are found. The listener who is studying rather than practising can use the same loop, predicting what a described case will show before the explanation is given, and learning from the difference.

To summarize, diagnosis is a loop of hypothesis, prediction, test, and revision, run in two modes and always in the presence of uncertainty. It asks not only what is most likely but what is most dangerous, and it ranks findings by how much they change belief rather than by how dramatic they seem. Every number, score, cut-off, and reference range that this book mentions is an illustration only, and each must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol before it influences any real decision. This book is educational material, it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient. If a patient is deteriorating, the reasoning pauses and the patient is treated, because no hypothesis is worth the delay of someone who is becoming more ill.
