# Chapter 60 — Adolescents

The adolescent is neither a large child nor a small adult, and a method built for either will fail him in a particular way. A child is examined through the parent who answers for him, an adult speaks for himself, and the adolescent sits between the two, able to give a full account and often unwilling to give it in front of the person who brought him. His body changes fast enough that every measurement is a moving target, so the normal for his age is a range rather than a line. The illnesses that begin now often announce themselves through behavior rather than through a single complaint. The organizing idea of this chapter is developmental: ask first what is normal for this stage, then ask what has departed from it, and remember that this is educational material which does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

Several possibilities at this age can kill, and each is named here with the finding that reveals it, because deferring them is how they are missed. A young person who has harmed himself, who wishes to be dead, or who has begun to give away what he owns may be at risk of suicide, and the finding that matters is not the wound but the intent, learned only by asking directly. One who has lost weight, fears food, purges, or exercises past exhaustion may have an eating disorder, and its severity shows in a slow pulse, a fallen temperature, and a pressure that drops on standing. One brought in drowsy or unrousable may be intoxicated, and the pupils, the speed of breathing, the smell of the breath, and marks of injection narrow the cause. A young woman with lower abdominal pain and fever may have a pelvic infection, and the emergency behind it is a pregnancy outside the uterus, revealed by a positive pregnancy test and pain on one side. One with bone pain that wakes him at night, bruising, night sweats, or a lump that will not go away may have a malignancy of the young, revealed by a blood count or an imaging study.

Growth and puberty are the first instruments of diagnosis here, because the normal is what makes the abnormal visible. The clinician establishes where the young person stands on the curve of height and weight for age and sex, whether growth has been steady, and whether the pubertal changes have begun in the expected order and time, and judges puberty as a sequence of stages rather than a moment. A healthy young person may still be earlier or later than his classmates, so the comparison that matters is with his own earlier measurements, and a height that has crossed downward through several channels, a growth spurt that never came, or a stalled puberty changes reassurance into investigation. The organic causes must not be missed: a long-standing illness of the gut, kidney, heart, or lung, a disorder of the thyroid or growth hormone, a tumor near the pituitary, and, in a young woman who has never menstruated, a chromosomal or anatomical cause. The findings that point away from simple delay are a falling growth rate, a headache or changed vision, a lost sense of smell, a swollen thyroid, a chronic bowel complaint, and a pubertal stage that has not advanced over a long interval, while a growth rate below 4 centimeters a year is offered as an illustration of when to investigate and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because charts and editions state it differently.

The consultation is arranged in two parts, and the order is itself part of the care. The clinician begins with the young person and the parent together, which respects the worried parent and lets the family account be heard, and then asks to speak with the young person alone, which is where most of the important history is given. Before the parent leaves, the clinician states plainly what will be kept private and what will not, so the young person is not asked to trust a promise that cannot be kept. A confidence may be broken when someone is in danger, whether the young person, another person, or a child in the household, and when the law requires a report of abuse or of an age of consent that has been crossed. The adolescent is told who else will be spoken to, what will be said, and why, and the age at which he may be seen alone or consent to his own care is set by law rather than medicine, so it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol of the jurisdiction.

Once the two are alone, the history is taken in the ordinary structure, using the Oldcarts framework so that nothing about the complaint is left out, but the questions are asked in a register an adolescent will answer. The clinician avoids the interrogative tone, gives the young person the first word on every subject, and asks about the complaint in his own terms before offering the medical vocabulary. The domains to cover are the home and who lives in it, the school or work and how it is going, the friendships and activities, the use of alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, and other substances, the sexual history, and the mood, sleep, and appetite. The clinician also asks what the young person believes is wrong, because that belief shapes whether he accepts the plan and whether he returns. A history taken this way takes longer than one taken from a parent, and it is the single measure that most often changes the diagnosis at this age.

A set of subjects is almost never raised with a parent in the room, and each changes the diagnosis if it becomes known. He may not say in front of a parent that he is sexually active, that a contact was unwanted, that his attraction is to the same sex, that he does not feel at home in the body he was born in, that he has been drinking or using a substance, that he has been hurt at home, or that he has thought about ending his life. The clinician therefore returns to these subjects in the private part of the visit and asks in plain language, without a preamble that signals alarm. The young person who is asked about his safety at home, about who shares his bed, and about the gender he understands himself to be is being told that these things are speakable. Gender and sexual identity are not diseases, but the distress around them is real and carries a greater risk of self-harm, so the assessment must include them rather than avoid them.

The menstrual history is a vital sign here and is too often left to the parent to describe. The clinician asks when the periods began, how far apart they are, how long they last, how heavy the flow is, whether they are painful, and whether they have stopped, and records the answers in the young person's own words. A period that has never begun by the expected age, one that has stopped after starting, and one that has become very heavy or painful each open a different line of reasoning: pregnancy, a hormonal disorder, a bleeding tendency, an infection, or an anatomical problem. Pregnancy must be considered in any adolescent with abdominal pain, vomiting, fainting, or a missed period, and it is considered privately and without judgment, because the fear of the reaction is exactly what delays the diagnosis. The finding that settles it is a pregnancy test, and where the test exists it should be offered to every young woman of this age with abdominal pain, whatever the story suggests, while where no test exists the clinician reasons from the history, the examination, and the passage of time and is explicit about the uncertainty that remains.

Mental illness is the epidemic of this age, and much of it is invisible unless it is sought. Depression may present as irritability rather than sadness, as a fall in school performance, as a withdrawal from friends, or as a headache or stomach ache with no explanation, while anxiety may present as a refusal to attend school, as a panic described as a physical illness, as a preoccupation with the body, or as a sleep that will not come. Attention deficit and hyperactivity are usually recognized earlier, but may first be noticed when the demands of secondary school rise above the young person's ability to compensate, and psychosis announces itself through withdrawal, decline, suspicion, or speech that has become hard to follow. The clinician asks about mood, sleep, appetite, energy, guilt, concentration, and the wish to be dead, and asks specifically about self-harm, because the question does not plant the idea and the silence does not remove it. The risk of harm to the self and to others is assessed in every young person with a mental complaint, and the assessment includes intent, plan, means, and protective factors, because the protective factors are what the plan will lean on, while a physical cause such as infection, a thyroid disorder, a low sugar, a drug, or a disease of the brain is excluded first, and any figure on a score or questionnaire must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since instruments differ between services and editions.

Self-harm and suicidal intent are related but not identical, and separating them is the most important judgment in this part of the chapter. Self-harm without the wish to die is often a way of managing unbearable feeling, of punishing the self, or of communicating a distress that has no words, and it marks great suffering even when the wound is superficial. Suicidal intent is present when the young person wishes to be dead, has a plan, has the means within reach, has made an attempt, or has become suddenly calm after agitation, because that calm may be the resolution before the act. The findings that raise risk are a previous attempt, a family history of suicide, a recent loss or humiliation, an alcohol or drug problem, an isolating illness, and access to a lethal method at home. The clinician asks directly whether he has thought about ending his life, whether he has a plan, and whether he has acted, then asks what has kept him alive, and the decision that follows is about safety rather than diagnosis, including what he will do before the next contact, who will be with him, and what will be removed from the home, while any number on a risk scale aids the reasoning and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol.

An eating disorder is a diagnosis of behavior and physiology at once, and the examination is as important as the history. The young person may restrict food, purge by vomiting or laxatives, exercise to exhaustion, or eat in secret and compensate, and the account may be denied completely and still be visible in the body. The findings are a fall in weight or a failure to gain what growth requires, a slow pulse, a low pressure that drops further on standing, a low temperature, cold hands and feet, fine downy hair on the face and arms, swollen salivary glands, eroded tooth enamel, calluses on the knuckles, and a dry skin. In a young woman the periods may stop and in a young man sexual development may stall, while the laboratory, where it exists, shows disturbed salts, a low blood sugar, and poor liver or kidney function. The young person who has lost a great deal of weight is at risk during the early part of any refeeding, so that decision belongs where the salts and the heart rhythm can be watched, and a pulse below 50 beats per minute or a temperature below 36 degrees Celsius is offered as an illustration of severity that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the line at which the body is failing is recorded differently in different services.

Substance use is common at this age, and the diagnostic task is to separate ordinary experiment from intoxication, dependence, and complication. The clinician asks, without shock and without euphemism, what the young person has used, how often, by what route, in what company, and what it has cost him in money, school, relationships, and sleep. Alcohol is the substance most often involved and the one most easily underestimated, because its effects on judgment, mood, and risk are absorbed into the story of a difficult week. Cannabis may present as a loss of drive, a fall in performance, a persistent cough, or a psychosis in a vulnerable young person, while synthetic stimulants may present as agitation, a racing heart, a high temperature, and paranoia. Opioids and sedatives present with slow breathing, pinpoint pupils, and reduced consciousness, and the finding that makes reversal of the intoxication right is inadequate breathing together with a consciousness that cannot be roused, while injection brings infection of the blood, skin, and heart valves, and a fall, a fight, or an assault may hide behind the drug.

Sexual health is asked about as directly as any other system, and the questions are the same for a young man as for a young woman. The clinician asks whether the young person is sexually active, whether the activity is wanted, whether a barrier or another method is used, whether a partner has had an infection, and whether any contact was coerced or with someone older or in a position of power. A sexually transmitted infection may be silent, and the finding that reveals it may be a discharge, a sore, a rash, a growth, a burning on passing urine, or nothing at all, which is why testing is offered rather than withheld until symptoms appear. Pelvic inflammatory disease is the infection that ascends from the cervix to the uterus and the tubes, and it presents with lower abdominal pain, discharge, fever, pain during intercourse, and tenderness on moving the cervix, while its consequence is scarring of the tubes, which brings infertility and a pregnancy outside the uterus. The finding that reveals the surgical emergency behind such a presentation is a positive pregnancy test with one-sided pain and a falling blood count, and that combination is treated as an emergency before any test confirms it, while the decision to test for the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, is offered with counseling and consent, and the interval after which a negative result becomes reliable must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A malignancy in an adolescent is uncommon, and its danger lies in the ease with which its early symptoms are attributed to sport, growth, or mood. A cancer of the blood may present with pallor, tiredness, fever, bruising without injury, bleeding from the gums or nose, and bone or joint pain that wakes the young person at night. A lymphoma may present as a lump in the neck, armpit, or groin that does not settle, with night sweats, weight loss, and itching of the skin. A tumor of bone usually presents as pain near the knee or shoulder that is worse at night and out of proportion to activity, and a swelling over it turns the story serious. A tumor of the brain may present with morning headache, vomiting, changed vision, weakness, or a decline in school performance, and the finding that reveals it may be a swollen optic disc seen through the torch or ophthalmoscope, while a germ cell tumor of the testis presents as a lump the young man may notice in the shower but not mention, which is why examining the testes is part of examining every adolescent boy who will allow it.

Many adolescents live with a chronic illness that began in childhood, and the reasoning here is shaped by the transfer from pediatric to adult care. The conditions that matter most are diabetes, asthma, epilepsy, sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis, inflammatory bowel disease, heart disease present from birth, and the late effects of a cancer treated in childhood. The questions change with the transfer: who holds the knowledge of the illness, who decides about treatment, whether the young person can describe his own condition and its warning signs, and whether he understands what happens if he stops. A decline in control that appears as a new symptom may be the first sign of a depression, a substance, a difficulty at home, or a simple failure to fill a prescription, and each is a diagnosis in its own right. The transfer should be planned rather than allowed to happen, because a gap in care is a diagnosis of omission that shows up later as an emergency, and the age at which a service hands the young person over is a local convention to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Sport is the setting in which the most dangerous cardiac condition of this age is found, and the finding is easy to miss because it appears only under effort. A young athlete who faints during exercise, rather than after it, may have a structural disease of the heart, a disorder of the heart muscle, or an abnormal pathway of conduction, and a faint during exertion is never attributed to heat until the heart has been considered. The history that matters is a previous faint, chest pain on effort, breathlessness out of proportion to the exercise, palpitation, and a family history of sudden death in a young relative. The examination may find a murmur that changes with standing or with the strain of a held breath, a displaced impulse, or a pressure that differs between the arms. An electrocardiogram, or ECG, should be obtained where it exists, and an ultrasound of the heart names most of these conditions, so the young person with a positive finding is referred before the next competition rather than after it, while in a clinic without either instrument the decision is the referral, and the finding that makes it urgent is the faint during effort.

The risk-taking that shapes this age is asked about in the same breath as the symptoms, because the injury of today and the diagnosis of tomorrow both come from it. The clinician asks about the use of a seatbelt and helmet, about driving and riding with a driver who has been drinking, about carrying a weapon, about physical fights, and about any contact that was not wanted. A head injury is common here, and its warning findings are a loss of consciousness, repeated vomiting, a worsening headache, deepening drowsiness, weakness on one side, a seizure, and fluid or blood from the nose or ear. A young person who is repeatedly injured is telling the clinician something about his circumstances, and the pattern may point to violence at home, bullying at school, or a risk he has not named. The clinician asks without moralizing, because a young person who feels judged will end the conversation, and the answers change the plan, whether it is a discussion about safety, a referral, or a report the law requires.

Some adolescents arrive acutely, and a few of the emergencies of this age present through the abdomen or through a faint. The young woman with sudden severe pain in one side of the lower abdomen, a missed period, and a fainting may have a pregnancy outside the uterus, which can bleed into the abdomen and kill quickly, and the findings that reveal it are tenderness on that side, a falling blood count, and a positive pregnancy test. The young person with pain that begins around the navel and settles in the right lower abdomen, with fever, loss of appetite, and tenderness worst when the hand is released, may have appendicitis, and the finding that decides the operation is the examination repeated rather than a single opinion. A twisted ovary presents with sudden severe pain and vomiting, and is a surgical emergency that an ultrasound names if the young person reaches a place where one exists. A faint on standing is common and often benign, but a faint during exercise, with chest pain, or with a family history of sudden death is a cardiac presentation until proved otherwise. In a clinic with no tests, the decision is not the diagnosis but the journey, and the finding that makes the journey immediate is severe pain, a racing pulse, a falling pressure, or a young person who looks unwell.

In a hospital the assessment of an adolescent can answer in hours questions that would otherwise take weeks, and the choice of tests follows the reasoning rather than replacing it. A pregnancy test is among the first tests in any young woman with abdominal pain, a missed period, a faint, or vomiting, and it is offered privately with the result handled according to the young person's wishes within the limits of the law. A blood count answers the question of anemia, infection, and a malignancy of the blood, a test of the salts and sugar answers the question raised by purging, starvation, or diabetes, and a test of liver and kidney function answers the question of overdose or long-standing illness. An ultrasound is the study of choice in the pelvis, because it names the pregnancy outside the uterus, the ovarian cyst, the twisted ovary, and the collection of pus without radiation, while computed tomography, or CT, and magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answer questions about the head, chest, and bone. A screen for substances is useful when it will change management and is ordered with the young person's knowledge, because a covert test destroys the trust on which the next disclosure depends, and the laboratory sets its own reference ranges, so every figure returned must be read against the interval of that laboratory and confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer is not a poorer place to reason, and at this age the instruments that cost nothing matter most. A chart on the wall and a pen allow height, weight, and body mass index to be plotted at every visit, and the trend across months is worth more than any single measurement, while the body mass index is a ratio read against the age and sex of the young person and against the chart in use, so its reference interval must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The torch makes the throat, ears, eyes, pupils, skin, and scars visible, and a bright light on the arms and thighs finds the marks a sleeve was hiding. The thermometer and a watch turn the impression of illness into a temperature, a pulse, and a breathing rate that can be repeated and compared, and the pressure can be taken with a cuff that fits the arm. A urine dipstick, where it exists, answers the question of infection, sugar, and protein, and a pregnancy test kit answers the question that must not be left to guesswork, while the examination is completed by watching the young person walk, by listening to how he speaks about his own life, and by asking the question the clinician has been avoiding.

Explaining the plan to the young person and to the parent is a skill of its own, and it is where the boundary set at the beginning is kept or broken. The clinician tells the young person first what has been found and what it means, in words that belong to him, and asks what he wants the parent to know. The parent is then given what is necessary for safety and care, and is helped to understand what the young person needs rather than what the parent fears. Where the two disagree, the clinician holds both in the room without pretending the disagreement has gone, because a plan imposed on either will fail. The clinician says plainly what happens next, who to contact, what would make the situation urgent, and what the young person is responsible for, and checks that both have understood by asking them to repeat it back. Follow-up is part of the treatment here, so the clinician arranges a review soon enough to matter, names the findings that should bring the young person back earlier, and contacts rather than discharges one who misses an appointment, because the absence may itself be the symptom.

To summarize, the adolescent is reasoned about through development, through confidentiality, and through the things said only when the parent has left the room, and every judgment begins with what is normal for that young person's stage. The dangers are named early and with the finding that reveals them: self-harm and suicide, an eating disorder, a substance intoxication, a pelvic infection with a pregnancy outside the uterus behind it, and a malignancy of the young. Growth and puberty are plotted rather than estimated, the mood, sleep, appetite, self-harm, substance, and sexual history are asked about directly, and the chronic illness of childhood is handed over with the young person's own knowledge rather than without it. The same consultation is conducted with the parent in the room and with the young person alone, and both are told plainly what will and will not be shared. Every figure in this chapter, whether an age, a temperature, a rate, a pressure, a growth rate, or a reference interval, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories, and between the charts used for growth. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A young person who is listened to alone, asked the direct question, and given a clear plan is safer than one who is investigated thoroughly and heard not at all.
