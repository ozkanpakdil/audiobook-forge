# Chapter 75 — Clinical Judgment Under Uncertainty

Most decisions a clinician makes are made before the diagnosis is known, and the skill this chapter describes is the skill of working well inside that gap. Uncertainty is not a failure of training or a sign that the history was taken badly, because the information available at the moment of decision is always incomplete and the patient cannot wait for it to become complete. The question is never whether uncertainty exists, but which uncertainty can be carried and which must be resolved first. A working diagnosis is the best current explanation, held with the honesty that it may be wrong, and a plan is the set of actions that follows from it together with the findings that would overturn it. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

The most dangerous position in medicine is not the dramatic presentation but the patient who is watched while something lethal is already under way. The greatest risk in this chapter is deterioration during observation, and the finding that reveals it is a change in the vital signs taken together rather than any single abnormal value. A breathing rate that has climbed, a pulse that has quickened, a pressure that has fallen, or an alertness that is no longer what it was all say more than one reading alone. A patient with an infection who is watched expectantly may be developing sepsis, and the earliest evidence is often the breathing rate written beside the pulse. A patient with fever and headache who is watched may be developing meningitis, and the finding that betrays it is resistance when the neck is flexed. A patient with abdominal pain who is watched may be developing a perforated bowel, revealed by a change in the character of the pain and an abdomen that has become rigid and tender. Watchful waiting is a plan only when it has a defined question, a defined interval, and a defined action if the answer is bad.

Uncertainty is tolerable when every explanation on the short list is benign enough to wait, when the patient is stable by every measure available, and when a clear path exists to reach the answer within a time the illness allows. Uncertainty demands action when any possibility on that list could kill or disable the patient within the interval that observation would consume. This distinction separates the clinician who waits well from the clinician who waits dangerously, and it is decided by the worst possibility rather than the most likely one. The reasoning is not a calculation of probability alone, because a rare catastrophe that can be prevented weighs more than a common illness that would settle by itself. A useful habit is to name the one finding that would make waiting unacceptable, and to commit to looking for it at a stated time.

Watchful waiting becomes a plan when it is written down in the same terms as any other decision. The plan names the working diagnosis, the question that observation is meant to answer, the findings that will be sought, and the interval at which the patient will be reviewed. An interval of 4 hours is often used for a patient whose condition could change quickly, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the safe interval depends on the illness, the setting, and the resources available. The plan also names the person responsible for the review, because an observation that belongs to nobody is an observation that will not happen. It names the action if the patient improves, if the patient is unchanged, and if the patient worsens. It names what the family should watch for, and how they will reach help. A plan without a trigger for action is simply a delay with better manners.

The working diagnosis is a tool rather than a verdict, and it must be held in a way that allows it to be discarded. Naming it honestly means saying to the team, and where appropriate to the patient, that this is the current best explanation and not a certainty. The clinician who writes probable pneumonia on a chart and then stops looking for anything else has converted a working diagnosis into a closed question. The honest form records what the explanation accounts for and what it leaves unexplained, and an unexplained finding is a signal that the working diagnosis may be wrong. A patient whose fever is explained by a chest infection but who also has a new weakness of one side has two problems until proven otherwise. The clinician who states the uncertainty aloud gives the next clinician permission to disagree, which is one of the most useful gifts one doctor can give another.

The discipline of saying aloud what would change the plan is the single most protective habit in the care of an uncertain patient, because it converts a vague intention to keep an eye on things into a set of observable conditions. The statement takes a simple form: if this finding appears, then we will do that. If the breathing rate rises above the level we have set, the patient goes to the intensive care unit. If the pain moves to the right lower abdomen and the abdomen becomes rigid, the surgeon is called. If the confusion does not clear when the infection is treated, imaging of the brain is arranged. Each condition is spoken in advance, so that the decision is made calmly rather than at the moment of crisis, and so that any member of the team can act on it. A plan that exists only in one clinician's head dies when that clinician goes off duty.

Every diagnosis rests on assumptions about the patient, and those assumptions must be revisited whenever the patient changes. The assumption that the patient is normally independent may fail when a relative describes a slow decline over months. The assumption that the patient can give a reliable history may fail when the patient is confused, intoxicated, or speaking a language the clinician does not share. The assumption that the patient will return if worse may fail when the clinic is far away or the family has no transport. The assumption that the fever is infectious may fail when the patient has been taking a new medicine for several weeks. Reviewing these assumptions at each encounter catches the error that repetition would otherwise hide, and also catches the opposite error in which an early hypothesis is abandoned too readily because it once seemed uncertain.

Time is itself a diagnostic instrument, and in many presentations it is the only one that resolves the question. The abdomen that is soft at the first examination and rigid an hour later has told the clinician more than any single test could. A fever that settles over 2 days may belong to a self-limiting illness, while a fever unchanged after that interval asks a different question, and any such interval is an illustration to be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the expected course differs with the cause and with the patient. The clinician should record the time at which each finding was found, so that trajectory can be read from the record. The patient who is improving and the patient who is not are separated by the direction of change rather than by any single set of numbers. Where resources allow, the examination is repeated by the same clinician, because a comparison between two observers is weaker than one made by a single pair of eyes.

A short list of lethal possibilities deserves to be held in mind whenever a patient is observed without a diagnosis, because each can progress while the clinician waits. Sepsis is revealed by a rising breathing rate, a falling pressure, mottled skin, and a shrinking passage of urine. Meningitis is revealed by a stiff neck, a dislike of light, and a rash that does not fade when pressed. A pregnancy outside the uterus is revealed by lower abdominal pain, a missed period, and tenderness on internal examination, and in a clinic without imaging the possibility alone should prompt urgent referral. A clot in the lung is revealed by sudden breathlessness, a fast pulse, and a swollen tender calf, although the leg may look entirely normal. A tear in the aorta gives pain that moves between the chest and the back together with a difference in pressure between the two arms. A heart attack is revealed by chest discomfort with sweating and nausea, and the electrocardiogram, or ECG, together with the history carries the decision. A stroke is revealed by sudden weakness of the face, arm, or leg on one side with a change in speech.

In a well-resourced hospital, uncertainty is managed with instruments of measurement and with the people who can be gathered around the patient. The pressure, the pulse, the breathing rate, the temperature, and the level of alertness are recorded at intervals, and the trend is read rather than any single figure. Laboratory tests, an ECG, a chest radiograph, ultrasound, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging can be arranged within hours, and each is chosen because its result would change what is done next. The hospital can also bring the patient back for review, admit for observation, and call a colleague from another specialty for a second opinion. The advantage is not that uncertainty disappears but that it can be reduced faster and that deterioration can be detected earlier. The risk of the hospital is that the monitors are trusted more than the patient, so that a machine reading a normal number is believed while the patient is visibly worse, and alarms silenced repeatedly have allowed patients to deteriorate unnoticed in well-equipped wards. The clinician who looks at the patient before looking at the screen keeps the instrument in its proper place.

In a clinic that has a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, uncertainty is not abolished but it is managed with the same reasoning. The history is taken more carefully rather than less, because it is the instrument that never runs out, and the examination is performed completely because it may not be repeated. The torch makes the throat, the eardrum, the skin, the eyes, and the mouth visible, and the thermometer turns an impression into a number that can be compared at the next visit. The stethoscope carries the sounds of the heart, the lungs, and the abdomen, and a watch allows the pulse and the breathing rate to be counted rather than guessed. The clinician then builds a plan for the patient to carry home, and that plan is the treatment of uncertainty in this setting. The limitation of this setting is real and should be stated plainly to the patient rather than hidden, because the patient who knows the limit can help to compensate for it. Where the reasoning concludes that the answer cannot be reached without a test, the decision is not to guess but to send the patient where the test exists.

Of the two settings, observation at home holds the greater risk, and the reason lies in the distance between the patient and help. A patient in a hospital bed who worsens is seen within minutes, while a patient at home may spend hours or a day before anyone notices. The home setting also holds fewer instruments, fewer observers, and often a family who cannot judge whether a change is serious. The risk is greatest for the very young, the very old, the pregnant patient, the patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, and the patient who lives alone or far from care. The clinician should therefore raise the threshold for sending a patient home rather than lower it, because caution is the correct response to the greater hazard.

Mitigation begins with a safety net that the patient and the family can actually use. The clinician states in plain words the findings that should bring the patient back, and writes them down so that they are not lost. The patient is asked to repeat those findings in his or her own words, which is the only reliable test that the message was understood. The clinician checks that the patient can reach the clinic or the hospital, that someone at home can recognize a change, and that a telephone number works. A named person is given responsibility, whether a relative, a community health worker, or the clinician on the next shift. A review is arranged at a definite time rather than left to the patient's judgment, and where the journey to help is long the threshold for return is set lower and said explicitly. A safety net that has never been tested is a hope rather than a plan.

Some patients should not be observed at home at all, and the clinician who recognizes them prevents the most serious failure. The patient who looks unwell despite normal numbers, the patient whose vital signs are already abnormal, and the patient whose pain is severe or worsening all need more than a home plan. The patient with signs of organ failure, the patient with a stiff neck and a rash that does not fade, and the patient who is confused or difficult to rouse need treatment and senior help at once. The pregnant patient with abdominal pain or bleeding needs urgent assessment, because a pregnancy outside the uterus can bleed catastrophically. The patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, the patient with a device or a line in the body, and the infant who is feeding poorly should be referred even when the examination seems reassuring. The patient who cannot describe symptoms, who lives alone, or who has no means of returning should be admitted where that is possible. The decision to refer is a diagnostic decision, made on the reasoning rather than on a single measurement.

The patient who is watched for too long is the central failure this chapter exists to prevent. It does not happen because the clinician is careless, but because a working diagnosis has quietly become a belief. The clinician sees the patient improve in one respect and fails to weigh the finding that has become worse. The plan drifts from an interval into a hope, and the review promised at 4 hours is postponed until the next morning, and even that interval must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because what is safe in one service is not safe in another. The patient who is watched too long often has a normal early examination, and that normal examination is exactly what makes the delay feel safe. The remedy is a fixed trigger that does not depend on the clinician's impression at the end of a long shift, so that when the trigger is met the action happens without further deliberation. A clinician who notices the delay and escalates late has still done the right thing and should never be discouraged from doing it.

There comes a point in every uncertain case where the patient needs treatment before the diagnosis is settled, and choosing that point is part of the reasoning. Treatment before diagnosis is right when the patient looks seriously ill, when a dangerous treatable cause is strongly suspected, or when the harm of waiting exceeds the harm of treating something that turns out not to be present. It is also right when the patient's immune defenses are suppressed, because the usual signs may not appear and the illness can move faster than the tests. Treatment before diagnosis is wrong when a clear non-infectious cause explains the picture, when the patient is stable enough to allow the diagnosis to be completed safely, and when treating first would destroy the samples on which the diagnosis depends. The clinician should take the samples that can be taken without delaying treatment, and should record what was given and when. The finding that would justify treatment and the finding that would forbid it are more useful to name than any particular medicine, because the reasoning travels between settings while the available drugs do not. This book names the decision and never the amount, and nothing in it should be used as a prescription.

Tests are used differently under uncertainty, and the difference lies in what a result is expected to do. A test that can rule a dangerous condition in is worth doing early even when the suspicion is low. A test that can only rule a dangerous condition out is worth doing when a negative result would genuinely allow a safe plan. A normal result in a patient who is visibly unwell is not reassurance but a reason to reconsider the working diagnosis. A test whose result will not change the plan should not be ordered, because every test carries a cost, a delay, and the risk of a false result that sends the patient down a wrong path. Where the test does not exist, the clinician must ask whether the reasoning can proceed without it, and if it cannot, the patient must travel. Sending a patient away for a test that will not change management consumes a journey that might be needed later for a real one.

A time-limited trial is a useful device when the diagnosis cannot be settled and some decision must still be made. The clinician defines the finding that will be judged, the interval over which it will be judged, and the action that will follow either improvement or failure. An interval of 48 hours is sometimes used for such a trial, and that figure is an illustration of the reasoning that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the appropriate interval depends on the illness and on the setting. If the patient improves in the expected way, the working diagnosis is supported but not proven. If the patient does not improve, it is abandoned and the reasoning begins again. The trial protects the patient from indefinite treatment based on a hypothesis that has never been tested, and it protects against changing course too often because the interval is fixed in advance.

Some patients leave the clinic with a symptom but without a diagnosis, and that outcome can be safe if it is handled deliberately. The clinician states what has been excluded, what remains possible, and what the course of the illness has been so far. A review is arranged, and the patient is told that the absence of a name does not mean the absence of a plan. The clinician watches for the appearance of a new finding, because a diagnosis often declares itself over weeks rather than in one encounter. The temptation to give a harmless label in order to end the consultation should be resisted, because a false label can delay a true one for a long time. Where the patient is anxious, the anxiety should be acknowledged without letting it become the diagnosis. The record should make clear that the question remains open, so that the next clinician does not read the absence of a diagnosis as the absence of a problem.

Uncertainty belongs to the patient as much as to the clinician, and it should be explained in words that neither frighten nor deceive. The clinician can say that there are several possible explanations, that the most likely is one of them, and that the plan is designed to catch the others if they appear. The patient should hear what is being done and why, and should know what the plan will be if the first explanation turns out to be wrong. Saying that we do not know yet is honest and is usually better received than a confident answer that later collapses. The explanation should leave room for the patient's own knowledge, because the patient may know which symptom came first or which medicine was started recently. A family who understands the plan is a second observer and a partner in the safety net, while the clinician who shares uncertainty without sharing a plan has transferred anxiety rather than information.

Uncertainty handled alone is heavier and more error-prone than uncertainty handled by a team. Saying aloud to a colleague that the diagnosis does not fit is not an admission of weakness but a recognized method of reducing error. A second clinician often sees the finding that the first had stopped noticing, because attention narrows when a hypothesis forms. The team also allows the plan to be handed over safely, with the working diagnosis, the trigger for action, and the interval of review all stated. A handover that says only that the patient is stable, without saying what would make the patient unstable, has lost the reasoning. Escalation to a senior clinician is a diagnostic act, and the threshold for it should be low when the patient is uncertain rather than high.

The record of an uncertain case should contain the thinking and not only the findings. It should state the working diagnosis, the alternatives that remain possible, and the reasoning that ranked them. It should state the plan, the interval of review, the findings that would trigger action, and who is responsible. It should record what the patient was told, and in what words the safety net was given. The record should also show what was considered and set aside, and why, because that reasoning is what makes the next review faster. Soap notes are a familiar structure for this writing, and the assessment and the plan are the parts that carry the uncertainty. A record that shows only a diagnosis and a treatment leaves the next clinician unable to see how close the patient is to changing course.

A constructed illustration may make these habits concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a middle-aged patient brought to a hospital with fever and a cough, whose chest radiograph suggests an infection, and who is admitted with a working diagnosis of pneumonia. The breathing rate is recorded at 30 breaths per minute, which is faster than expected, and that figure is an illustration of reasoning and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the expected range depends on age and on the reference being used. The team writes a plan that names the trigger, which is a further rise in the breathing rate, a fall in the pressure, or a new confusion, and names the intensive care unit as the action if the trigger is met. Overnight the pressure begins to fall and the patient becomes drowsy, and the trigger is recognized by the nurse on the next shift because it was written down. The working diagnosis was reasonable, and the outcome was decided not by its correctness but by the plan attached to it, and neither the patient nor the hospital is real.

Now imagine the same reasoning in a clinic that has a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer. A young patient comes with lower abdominal pain, a missed period, and a pulse of 110 beats per minute, and that rate is another illustration that must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since what is normal varies with age, pregnancy, and fitness. The clinician cannot test for pregnancy and cannot image the pelvis, but the reasoning does not require either, because a missed period, lower abdominal pain, and a racing pulse together raise the possibility of a pregnancy outside the uterus. The dangerous cause is named at the bedside, the patient is not sent home to be watched, and the journey to a hospital begins immediately with a companion and a note that states the suspicion. The clinician also explains why the journey cannot wait, which is part of the treatment of uncertainty rather than an addition to it. In this case the plan was decided by what could not be excluded rather than by what was most likely, and again the patient is constructed and the situation is described for teaching only. The reasoning transfers between the two settings even when the instruments do not, and that is the argument of this whole book.

To summarize, uncertainty is the ordinary condition of clinical work rather than a failure, and the task is to decide which uncertainty can be carried and which demands action before anything else is done. The dangerous possibility is named early, and the presentation this chapter warns about most strongly is the patient who is watched while something lethal is already under way. The finding that reveals it is a change in the vital signs taken together, particularly the direction of the breathing rate, the pulse, the pressure, and the level of alertness. Watchful waiting is a plan only when it names the question, the interval, the trigger for action, and the person responsible, and a working diagnosis is held honestly as the best current explanation rather than as a verdict. Assumptions about the patient must be revisited each time the patient changes, and the clinician must say aloud what would change the plan. Observation at home carries the greater risk because help is further away, and it is mitigated by a safety net that is written, taught back, tested for transport and contact, and reviewed at a stated time. Every score, cut-off, interval, rate, pressure, and temperature named in this chapter is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because those figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and the clinician is not required to be certain but is required to be honest, to plan, and to act when the plan says act.
