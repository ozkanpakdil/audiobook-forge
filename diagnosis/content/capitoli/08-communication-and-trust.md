# Chapter 8 — Communication and Trust

A diagnosis is only as good as the information it is built from, and almost all of that information passes through a conversation. Trust is therefore not a courtesy added to the clinical encounter; it is a diagnostic instrument, and a patient who does not trust the clinician will withhold the detail that would have changed the answer. A clinician who is technically excellent and conversationally clumsy will reach confident conclusions from incomplete data and will never know what was missing. This chapter is educational material, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient. What follows is a way of thinking about the conversation, not a script, and every local rule about consent, confidentiality, and reporting must be confirmed in your own setting.

Trust changes what the patient is willing to say, long before any difficult question is asked. People disclose shameful, frightening, or illegal information to a person they believe will not use it against them, and withhold the same information from a person they expect to judge or dismiss them. Two clinicians can therefore ask identical questions in identical words and receive different histories, not because the patients differ but because the rooms feel different. A constructed illustration makes the point: a patient with a discharge and a fever who fears that a sexually transmitted infection will be read as evidence of infidelity may report only the fever if the clinician's manner suggests disapproval. The remedy is not a warmer personality but deliberate habits that tell the patient, before any sensitive question is asked, that the room is safe.

The patient reads the clinician constantly, and what the patient reads shapes what the patient says. Whether the clinician sits or stands, whether the eyes rest on the patient or on a screen, and whether the first question is open or closed all carry a message about how much the patient's account is worth. A clinician who writes while the patient speaks says that the record matters more than the telling, and one who interrupts after a few seconds says that the conclusion has already been reached. These signals are not etiquette; they decide whether the patient produces the second half of the story, which is usually the half that matters. Deliberate habits are more reliable than good intentions, because intentions are invisible and habits are not.

The opening of the encounter deserves protection because it is the only part that belongs entirely to the patient. A patient allowed to speak without interruption will often arrive, after a minute or two, at the concern that actually brought them, which is frequently not the first symptom mentioned. Patients test the clinician before they trust the clinician, and they begin with the safe complaint while deciding whether the dangerous one can be said. This is why the question about anything else, asked at the end of the visit, so often produces the real agenda, and why it must be asked on purpose. The clinician who asks it without sighing has understood that the extra item is not an interruption of the work but the work itself.

In this part of the encounter there is a lethal possibility that must be named early, and it is that the patient is in danger, from their own hand or from another person. The findings that reveal it are specific: a stated intention to end life with a plan and access to the means, a previous attempt, injuries that do not match the explanation given for them, or a household in which violence is escalating. A patient who describes any of these needs the pace of the encounter to change at once, because a diagnosis made while someone is unsafe is made on borrowed time. The clinician should not wait for the patient to volunteer such material, since disclosure usually follows a direct question and rarely precedes it. Asking clearly, without alarm in the voice, converts a hidden risk into a manageable one, and the steps that follow belong to local protocol and to services that differ between places.

When the patient and the clinician do not share a language, the conversation acquires a third participant, and that participant changes the clinical data. A family member, a cleaner, or another patient pressed into service is not an interpreter; they are a person with their own knowledge, loyalties, and fears, and they will edit what they hear. Interpretation is a skill with rules, and the rules exist to keep the questions and the answers as close to the original as two languages allow. The clinician speaks to the patient rather than to the interpreter, because the patient is the source and the interpreter is only the channel. The clinician also uses the first person and briefs the interpreter beforehand about what will be asked, then speaks with the interpreter afterwards about what was noticed but not translated.

Where the interpreter sits determines who is being addressed, so an interpreter placed beside or slightly behind the patient, rather than opposite the clinician, keeps the line of speech between the two people who matter. Confidentiality must be stated to the interpreter as plainly as to the patient, and the interpreter must understand that everything heard stays in the room. In a small community the interpreter may know the patient, and the fear of gossip can silence the patient; when that is possible, an interpreter from outside the community, or a telephone service, is worth the trouble. A trained interpreter will sometimes stop and ask for a question to be rephrased, which is a sign of competence rather than delay. What an interpreter must never do is answer for the patient, soften a disclosure, or summarise a story that was told at length.

A child must never be used as an interpreter for a parent, and this is a rule without exceptions in the sensitive parts of the history. The reasons are not only about accuracy, though a child's vocabulary will distort matters of anatomy, illness, and treatment. The deeper reason is that a child asked to carry a parent's sexual history, a parent's fear of violence, or a parent's wish to die has been given something no child can carry, and the harm lasts beyond the consultation. When no adult interpreter is available, the clinician should postpone the sensitive questions rather than proceed, and should attend first to whatever is urgent and can be managed without them. Where language is a barrier in a life-threatening emergency, the clinician acts on what can be understood and secures proper interpretation as soon as possible.

Culture shapes how distress is shown, and the display is not a measurement of what is felt. Some patients are taught to bear pain in silence, and their calm face may conceal something severe, while others express agony openly as a matter of custom and their volume may not indicate the severity of the cause. A clinician who reads the display as the intensity will under-treat the stoic and over-investigate the expressive, and both errors have consequences. It is more useful to ask about the effect of the pain on sleep, work, walking, and appetite than to grade the grimace. Pain reported as pressure, heat, or heaviness rather than as pain is still pain, and the clinician should follow the patient's vocabulary rather than correct it.

Every patient carries an explanation of why this has happened, and that explanation shapes both what is disclosed and what is accepted. A patient who believes the illness is a punishment may hide it, a patient who believes it is caused by witchcraft may seek the traditional healer first, and a patient who believes it is trivial may not return when it worsens. The clinician who asks what the patient thinks is going on, and what the patient fears most about it, obtains information that no examination can supply. These questions also begin the negotiation about treatment, because a plan that contradicts the patient's understanding will be quietly abandoned. It is worth knowing which healers and which other services the patient consulted before arriving, because those choices are part of the clinical picture.

In many families the right to speak about illness belongs to someone other than the patient, and the clinician must find a way to hear the patient without insulting the family. The husband who answers for his wife and the eldest son who answers for his mother are not necessarily obstructing; they may be expressing the only form of care they know. Yet a history delivered entirely by a relative has had the patient's own sensations, fears, and disclosures removed. A constructed illustration: a young woman whose mother answers every question about abdominal pain, and whose own account, obtained when the two are briefly separated, includes a pregnancy that the mother does not know about. The maneuver that obtains this is not confrontation but a routine practice of speaking with every adult patient alone for part of the encounter. Framing that separation as a habit of the clinic, applied to everyone, removes the implication that this particular patient is hiding something.

Sensitive questions are asked in plain words, and asking them is a clinical skill that improves with practice. Euphemism fails because the patient may not recognize the subject at all, or may take the delicacy as a sign that the topic is shameful, which confirms the fear that kept it hidden. Force fails for the opposite reason, since a patient who is pushed will say whatever ends the pushing and the clinician will record a false answer as a true one. The middle path is to name the topic directly, ask permission to ask, and explain why the question bears on the diagnosis. The clinician should then be silent and let the patient answer at their own pace, because the pause after a difficult question is where the truth usually arrives.

A sexual history is taken for diagnostic reasons, not out of curiosity, and the reasons should be visible to the patient. The questions that matter are whether the patient is sexually active, with whom, and whether any symptom or risk bears on the presenting complaint, such as discharge, pain, bleeding, or possible exposure to infection. The clinician must not assume the patient's partner's gender, the patient's own orientation, or that a married patient has only one partner, because each assumption closes a door. It is also necessary to ask whether protection is used, whether partners have changed, and whether a previous infection was treated or only suspected. Asking whether the patient has ever been tested for the human immunodeficiency virus is an ordinary question in many settings and should be asked in the same tone as the rest. Since the questions a service considers mandatory, and the reporting duties that follow some answers, differ between countries and clinics, they must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Violence at home is one of the few diagnoses that can be made only by asking, and it must be asked when the patient is alone. The question should be direct: whether anyone at home has hurt, threatened, or frightened them. A positive answer changes the plan at once, because the finding that matters is not the bruise but the presence of a person who is dangerous, and the safest route out of the room may not be the obvious one. Suspicion should rise before any answer is given when injuries are of different ages, when they involve the face, neck, or breasts, when the explanation changes with retelling, or when the person who insists on translating is the person the patient seems afraid of. If the patient discloses violence, the clinician's task is to believe, to record factually, to offer local sources of help, and to let the patient make the decisions that are theirs to make. Where the patient is a child, an older person who depends on a carer, or an adult who cannot protect themselves, the duties are governed by law and local policy and must be established in advance.

Asking about suicidal thought does not plant it, and the belief that it does has cost many lives. The question should be asked plainly, in words the patient uses, whenever the presentation suggests despair, severe pain, chronic illness, loss, or a sudden change in behavior. If the answer is yes, the clinician must go on to ask about the thought itself, whether there is a plan, whether the means are available, whether there has been a previous attempt, and whether the patient intends to act. The finding that turns a worrying answer into an emergency is a plan together with access to the means and a stated intention, and a previous attempt raises the concern further. The patient should not be left alone while that assessment is completed, and the local pathway for urgent mental health care should be activated rather than improvised. The instruments and scores used to structure the assessment, and the point at which a service escalates, differ between editions and settings, so any figure is an illustration only and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Substance use is asked about for the same diagnostic reasons and in the same ordinary tone, because a patient who expects a lecture will report nothing. The useful questions are what is used, how often, in what circumstances, what it does for the patient, and what happens when it is stopped. Alcohol is the substance most often under-reported, and the clinician should ask about the first drink of the day, the drink needed to feel steady, and any occasion when the patient tried to cut down. The dangerous possibility that must be named here is withdrawal, which can progress to confusion, hallucination, seizures, and death in a patient who has been drinking heavily and has recently stopped. The findings that reveal it are tremor, sweating, a pulse of 110 beats per minute, agitation, and a clouding of attention that fluctuates, appearing after an abstinence whose length varies with the patient. A pulse in that range is not proof by itself, since fear, fever, and pain produce it too, and the rate that should raise concern differs between guidelines, editions, and services, so confirm the threshold your own service uses against the current guideline and the local protocol. Treatment is the right answer when the withdrawal picture is present and the history supports heavy daily use, and it is the wrong answer when another cause explains the presentation and the patient is stable.

A relative or friend can supply information the patient cannot, and the clinician should ask for it while making clear why it is wanted. The most valuable collateral history is not a second opinion about the diagnosis but a description of change: what the patient could do last month and cannot do now, how sleep, appetite, and mood have altered, whether medicines are actually being taken, and whether the patient has been confused, withdrawn, or unsteady. It helps to ask the relative to describe what they have seen rather than what they think it means, since a relative's interpretation is shaped by love, exhaustion, and fear. Consent should be sought from the patient whenever the patient can give it, and the patient should be told what will be discussed and why. The relative's account should be recorded as the relative's account rather than merged silently into the patient's story.

When the patient's account and the relative's account conflict, the conflict is itself a finding and should not be resolved by choosing the more likeable narrator. The two may differ because of memory, because of what each was in a position to see, because one is protecting the other, or because an interpreter has smoothed over a gap. The clinician should resist the temptation to confront, which usually produces defense rather than accuracy, and should instead return to the patient with a different question and a different route into the same ground. It is often useful to say what has been understood so far and to invite correction, since a patient will amend a summary who would not contradict a challenge. A relative who reports a change that the patient denies may be describing early cognitive impairment, deafness, or a language barrier rather than a disagreement about facts.

A particular conflict deserves attention because it is dangerous and easily missed. A relative may say that the patient has become confused, while the patient speaks coherently, denies any problem, and performs well in the room. The dangerous possibility is delirium, a disturbance of attention and awareness that develops over a short period and fluctuates, and the finding that reveals it is inattention that comes and goes, best detected by asking the patient to say the months backwards or to count down, and by watching through the day rather than at one moment. Delirium in an older person often signals infection, dehydration, a metabolic disturbance, or a medicine effect, and its appearance should redirect the encounter towards finding the cause. The patient may also be febrile, and a temperature of 38 degrees Celsius or more is then a prompt to look for infection rather than to assume the confusion is benign; the figure that defines a fever is not the same in every guideline or ward, so it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, as must the interval that separates an acute change from a long-standing one. A patient whose confusion is dismissed because one conversation went well is a patient in whom a treatable and sometimes lethal condition has been missed.

The same conversation is conducted under very different conditions depending on where the patient is seen. In a hospital or a long appointment, an hour may be available, and that time should go to the parts of the history that need it: the sensitive questions, the account of what the patient fears, the interpreter, and the collateral history. In a busy clinic where five minutes is all that exists, the clinician must triage the conversation as the examination is triaged, deciding which questions are indispensable and which can wait. The indispensable questions are the ones that change what happens today, such as whether the patient is safe, whether a symptom demands urgent referral, and whether the patient understands what to do next. What must not happen in a short encounter is the silent omission of a dangerous question because there was no time, and the clinician should say honestly what could not be covered and arrange the longer conversation. In a well-resourced hospital the record often arrives before the patient, and the previous diagnosis is evidence of what someone once thought rather than a measurement of what is true now. What counts as an adequate consultation, and how soon a patient should be reviewed, differs between services, so those intervals must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol.

In a clinic that has a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, communication carries even more weight, because there is no laboratory to correct a history that was not taken. The whole diagnostic process may rest on what the patient says and on what the clinician can see, hear, and feel, so a withheld detail is not a gap in the data but a hole in the reasoning. Trust also decides whether the patient returns, and a patient who does not return cannot be reassessed when the picture changes. The clinician should therefore be explicit about the limits of what can be done here, about what the examination has shown, and about the signs that should bring the patient back. Safety-netting is not a substitute for a decision, but without tests it is often the most important part of the plan, and the questions asked in a district clinic are the same questions asked in a teaching hospital.

Just as too little trust starves the reasoning, too much trust can mislead it, and the clinician must notice which patients are believed easily. A patient who is articulate, calm, and medically informed is often believed more than one who is vague, distressed, or intoxicated, and the difference in belief is not always justified by the facts. The clinician's own assumptions about who exaggerates and who understates are a source of error, and they operate unnoticed. It is worth asking whether a decision would be the same if the same symptoms were described by a different person. The patient who seems to be seeking attention may be describing something real, and the discipline is to apply the same search for dangerous possibilities to everyone, letting findings rather than manner move the differential.

The physical examination is a form of communication, and it can build trust or destroy it within seconds. The clinician should explain what is about to be done, ask permission before touching, warn before a maneuver that may hurt, and keep the patient covered except for the area being examined. A chaperone should be offered when the examination is intimate or when either person would be more comfortable with one present, and the offer should be recorded. In many cultures a same-gender examiner is expected for certain examinations, and arranging this may decide whether the examination happens at all. A patient treated with predictable respect during the examination will often disclose afterwards the detail that was withheld during the history, while hurried and unexplained hands tell the patient that the body is an object rather than a person.

The clinician's own emotions are part of the instrument and need to be noticed rather than denied. Irritation at a patient who returns repeatedly, fear of a patient who is aggressive, disgust at a wound, and exhaustion at the end of a long shift all change the tone of the voice and the length of the pause. A patient reads those changes accurately and responds by shortening the story. The clinician who notices the feeling can decide what to do with it, which is usually to slow down, ask one more open question, and check the assumption that produced it. A constructed illustration: a clinician who assumes that a frequent attendee has nothing new, and who therefore does not ask about the chest pain that began that morning.

Some encounters do not produce trust, and the clinician must still work. A patient may be frightened, hostile, intoxicated, in pain, or convinced that the clinician cannot help, and no amount of warmth will change that within the available time. In such a case the clinician should aim for the minimum that safety requires: the dangerous possibilities are asked about, the examination is completed, and the plan is stated in the simplest words available. If the patient refuses a necessary part of the assessment, the refusal should be recorded, the risks explained, and the offer left open for another time. Trust is sometimes built over several encounters rather than one, and the clinician who plants the possibility of return has not failed.

When the patient cannot give a history, the conversation changes shape but does not disappear. An unconscious patient, a patient with a severe communication difficulty after a stroke, a child too young to describe symptoms, and a patient whose memory is impaired all require information from someone else, and the clinician must weigh that information carefully. The relative's account may be excellent on function and behavior and unreliable on sensation, which only the patient can report. Observation becomes the substitute for speech, so posture, breathing, the response to approach, and the change over time are the patient's testimony when words are unavailable. In an emergency the clinician may act without consent to prevent death or serious harm, and the rules governing that exception must be known before the moment arrives, with the reasoning documented.

The end of the conversation is a diagnostic opportunity, because asking the patient to repeat the plan reveals what was actually heard. A patient who cannot restate the plan has not understood it, and the misunderstanding will surface later as a missed diagnosis rather than as a communication problem. It also helps to ask what the patient is most worried about now, since the answer often contains the detail that the questions did not reach. Summary and silence together do more than another round of questions, because a patient who is listened to will often add the crucial sentence unprompted. The clinician should state clearly what will happen next, what should prompt an earlier return, and who to contact, in words the patient can repeat.

To summarize, communication is a diagnostic instrument, and trust determines how much of the patient's truth reaches the reasoning. The clinician builds it with predictable habits rather than charm: listening before interrupting, asking direct and unforced questions about sex, safety, suicidal thought, and substance use, using a trained adult interpreter, speaking with the patient alone, seeking collateral history without surrendering the patient's own account, and treating a conflict between accounts as a finding to be tested. Culture shapes how distress is expressed and who is allowed to speak, and the clinician follows the patient's meaning rather than correcting it. The same conversation must be compressed into a few minutes or expanded into an hour, but the dangerous questions cannot be dropped in either setting, and the clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer depends on that conversation more than any hospital does. Every score, cut-off, interval, and threshold mentioned in this chapter is an illustration only and must be verified against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This chapter is educational material, it is not medical advice, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient, so that when a patient is in danger from themselves or from another person, that danger is addressed before any diagnostic elegance is pursued.
