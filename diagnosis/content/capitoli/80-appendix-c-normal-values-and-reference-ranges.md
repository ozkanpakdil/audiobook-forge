# Appendix C — Normal Values and Reference Ranges

A reference range is a promise a laboratory makes about a measurement, and it is made for a particular method, a particular population, and a particular place. When a report says that a result lies between two figures, it means that most of the healthy people studied fell inside them, not that a value outside them is a disease. The range belongs to the machine, the units, and the people from whom it was drawn, so a range carried from one country to a patient in another is a guess dressed as a fact. This appendix gathers values met most often in the adult, the child, and the pregnant patient, and it gives them only as illustrations of the kind of figure a report may contain. Every one of them must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because reference intervals differ between laboratories, between countries, and between editions. This appendix is educational material, not medical advice, not a protocol, and not a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and no patient described in it is real.

The vital signs are the measurements a clinician makes rather than orders, and they are the ones most often compared against memory instead of a record. In a resting adult the temperature is often given as 36 to 38 degrees Celsius, the pulse as 60 to 100 beats per minute, and the breathing rate as 12 to 20 breaths per minute. The pressure is commonly written as a pair of figures such as 120 over 80 millimeters of mercury, and oxygen saturation at sea level in a healthy adult is usually stated as 95 to 100 per cent. Each of these is a teaching illustration and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted boundaries shift with the method, the age, and the edition consulted. The breathing rate is the sign least often counted properly and most often informative, and pulse and pressure together say more than either alone.

Children are not small adults, and their normal values move with age in a way that surprises anyone who learned only adult figures. A newborn's pulse may sit between 100 and 160 beats per minute and falls gradually through infancy and childhood toward the adult range, and the breathing rate follows the same descent. The pressure is lower in childhood and climbs with growth, so a figure unremarkable in an adult may be a warning in a small child, and one alarming in an adult may be ordinary in an infant. Hemoglobin and the red cell indices change in the first months of life, and the white cell count is higher in the newborn than it will ever be again. Because these boundaries are set locally and revised from time to time, confirm every pediatric figure against the current guideline and the local protocol, and read the child's own previous results whenever they exist.

Pregnancy changes almost every measurement a laboratory makes, and a range taken from a non-pregnant adult will mislead in a woman who is expecting. The pulse rises a little and the pressure tends to fall in the middle months before returning toward its old level near term, so a low reading may be entirely physiological. The blood is diluted by the extra plasma, so the hemoglobin falls even though the total red cell mass has increased, and a value suggesting anemia outside pregnancy may be expected here. The kidneys filter more, so creatinine and urea fall, the thyroid hormones shift in a pattern that can mimic disease, and clotting factors rise so that a swollen calf is never dismissed. Gestational age and the laboratory's own trimester-specific ranges change the answer, so take these figures to the current guideline and the local protocol before you interpret them.

The blood count is the most frequently ordered set of numbers in medicine and the one most often read without its context. Hemoglobin in a healthy adult man is often given as 13 to 17 grams per deciliter and in a woman as 12 to 15 grams per deciliter, while the white cell count commonly falls between 4 and 11 thousand cells per microliter and the platelet count between 150 and 400 thousand per microliter. The hematocrit is the proportion of blood occupied by red cells, and the red cell indices describe the size and the color of the cells rather than their number. A low hemoglobin with small pale cells suggests a different problem from a low hemoglobin with large cells, and the indices make that distinction rather than the hemoglobin alone. These intervals are illustrations printed by one laboratory for one population and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since units, methods, and boundaries vary between services and editions.

The chemistry panel answers questions about the salts, the sugar, and the waste products, and each of its figures belongs to its own population. Sodium is often quoted as 135 to 145 millimoles per liter, potassium as 3.5 to 5.0 millimoles per liter, bicarbonate as 22 to 28 millimoles per liter, and chloride as 98 to 107 millimoles per liter. Fasting glucose is commonly given as 4 to 6 millimoles per liter, calcium as 2.2 to 2.6 millimoles per liter, and magnesium as 0.7 to 1.0 millimoles per liter, although the units used for sugar and calcium differ between countries. A potassium at the edge of its range matters more than a sodium at the edge, because the heart tolerates a mild disturbance of sodium far better than a mild disturbance of potassium. Every one of these intervals is an illustration and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the figures, the units, and the analytic methods are not the same everywhere.

The kidney values are read together rather than singly, because no one of them describes filtration on its own. Creatinine is often reported as 60 to 110 micromoles per liter, and the estimated glomerular filtration rate, calculated from creatinine with age and sex, is commonly given as above 90 milliliters per minute for each 1.73 square meters of body surface area. Urea is usually quoted as 2.5 to 7.0 millimoles per liter, and creatinine depends on muscle, so a frail older patient may have a reassuring value while filtration is poor. A rise in creatinine over days is more alarming than the same value reached slowly over years, and the rate of change most often decides the urgency. Because the equations and the units differ between laboratories and are revised between editions, confirm every kidney figure against the current guideline and the local protocol, and read the trend alongside the value.

The liver values divide into those that speak of injury and those that speak of function, and the two do not always move together. Bilirubin is often reported as 5 to 20 micromoles per liter, albumin as 35 to 50 grams per liter, and total protein as 60 to 80 grams per liter. The enzymes alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase are commonly quoted with an upper boundary near 40 units per liter, alkaline phosphatase with one near 120 units per liter, and gamma-glutamyl transferase with one that differs between men and women. The clotting tests, the prothrombin time and the international normalized ratio, describe what the liver can still make, and they worsen when function fails rather than when cells are merely injured. All of these boundaries vary with the method and the population, so take them to the current guideline and the local protocol rather than relying on memory.

The inflammatory markers are the least specific numbers in the laboratory and the easiest to over-read. C-reactive protein is often described with a boundary near 5 milligrams per liter, the erythrocyte sedimentation rate with a boundary that rises with age and differs between men and women, and procalcitonin with a boundary some services use to support a decision about infection. Ferritin is both an iron store and an inflammatory marker, so a raised value may speak of inflammation as readily as of overload. None of these substances names a cause, since they say that something is happening while the history and the examination say what, and a marker that climbs in a patient who looks worse is a reason to hunt for a collection of pus. Because assays and boundaries are set locally and revised between editions, confirm every one of these figures against the current guideline and the local protocol, and never treat a single result as a diagnosis.

The blood gas describes how well the lungs and the kidneys are keeping the blood in balance, and it is meaningless without the sample it came from. Arterial blood in a healthy adult is often given a pH between 7.35 and 7.45, a partial pressure of oxygen between 80 and 100 millimeters of mercury, and a partial pressure of carbon dioxide between 35 and 45 millimeters of mercury, with bicarbonate between 22 and 26 millimoles per liter. Venous blood gives different figures, and a sample from a vein cannot be judged against arterial boundaries. Altitude lowers the oxygen figure in a healthy person, age lowers it a little, and a low oxygen with a high carbon dioxide tells a different story from a low oxygen with a normal one. Take every figure here to the current guideline and the local protocol, because the boundaries differ between laboratories and between the editions on which they rely.

The urine is the specimen a clinic can obtain without a needle, and its values depend on the method as much as any in medicine. The specific gravity of a normal urine is often given as 1.005 to 1.030, and the reaction is usually acidic, between 4.5 and 8.0, depending on the diet. A dipstick may report protein, glucose, ketones, blood, leukocyte esterase, and nitrite, and microscopy may report white cells, red cells, casts, crystals, and organisms, each with a locally set boundary. A collection over 24 hours measures how much protein or how much of a substance leaves the body in a day, and it is uninterpretable if the collection was incomplete, while a positive dipstick for blood means little in a menstruating woman or after exercise. Because strip chemistry, microscopy, and units vary between services, confirm each figure against the current guideline and the local protocol, and read the urine with the history rather than apart from it.

The endocrine values are the ones most often misread, because the hormone is interpreted without the gland and the patient it serves. Thyroid stimulating hormone is often quoted as 0.4 to 4.0 milliunits per liter, with free thyroxine reported in a range each laboratory sets around its own method. Glycated hemoglobin, which reflects the average sugar over the previous weeks, is commonly described with a boundary near 6.5 per cent, and a fasting glucose with one near 7 millimoles per liter, although the units and the boundaries differ between countries. Cortisol moves through the day, so a single value cannot be judged without the hour it was taken, and in acute illness almost every hormone can be disturbed without glandular disease. Confirm all of these boundaries against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the assays are not standardized between laboratories and the same patient can appear to change simply by changing the hospital.

The clotting tests describe how quickly blood forms a clot in a test tube and how well the liver and the platelets are doing their work. The prothrombin time is often given as 11 to 14 seconds, the activated partial thromboplastin time as 25 to 35 seconds, and fibrinogen as 2 to 4 grams per liter, while the international normalized ratio is commonly quoted as 0.8 to 1.2 in a person not taking a blood-thinning medicine. The platelet count belongs with these figures, because a normal clotting time in a patient with very few platelets is not reassuring. A clot that forms slowly may mean a deficiency of one factor, a blood-thinning medicine, liver disease, or consumption of clotting factors during severe illness, and the pattern across the tests separates them. Since each of these intervals is set by the reagent and the analyzer that performs it, take them to the current guideline and the local protocol before you act, and never compare a result from one hospital with a boundary printed in another.

Some values are reported by the laboratory but belong to the heart, and they are read with the tracing rather than alone. Troponin is released by injured heart muscle, and each laboratory sets its own boundary and its own way of deciding what counts as a rise, so a result is interpreted against the interval printed by the laboratory that produced it. A natriuretic peptide reflects the stretch of a failing heart, and its boundary is set locally and shifts with age and kidney function. On the electrocardiogram, or ECG, the duration of each part of the tracing is an approximate figure whose boundaries move with the rate, the age of the patient, and the machine that recorded it. A single normal marker never excludes a heart attack and a single abnormal marker never proves one, so confirm every one of these figures against the current guideline and the local protocol, since assays, units, and accepted boundaries are revised repeatedly.

Three influences move more reference ranges than any others, and they are age, pregnancy, and altitude. Age changes the pulse, the breathing rate, the pressure, the hemoglobin, the white cell count, and the kidney figures, and it changes them in different directions, so a value ordinary at 1 year may be abnormal at 40 and unremarkable again at 80. Pregnancy changes the blood count, the clotting, the kidney values, the thyroid, and the pressure, and it requires ranges stated by trimester. Altitude raises hemoglobin and red cells and lowers the oxygen figure in the blood gas, and the change takes days to weeks to settle, while illness itself moves many values through inflammation, dehydration, fever, and medicines. Because these influences are handled differently in different services, confirm any range you use against the current guideline and the local protocol, and prefer the interval your own laboratory prints for the population it serves.

Most of the numbers in this appendix are worth more as a trend than as a single reading, and the reason is biological variation. The same substance in the same healthy person moves during the day, changes with meals and sleep, and differs from one sample to the next by more than a clinician expects. A change smaller than that ordinary variation may be noise, while a small change in the same direction across three samples may be real. The patient's own previous results are therefore the best comparison available, and the direction of travel usually matters more than the distance from the printed boundary. The report itself should be read with its units, its method, and its reference interval, since a value quoted without those is a number without a meaning. Read the trend against the current guideline and the local protocol as well, because the interval and the units may have changed between the two occasions you are comparing.

A constructed illustration may show how these habits work, and it is invented for teaching rather than drawn from any real person. Imagine a woman in late pregnancy whose hemoglobin sits at the lower edge of the non-pregnant range, whose platelet count has drifted down a little, and whose creatinine has fallen, since none of these is disease and all follow from the physiology of pregnancy. Now imagine an older man, thin and frail, whose creatinine lies comfortably inside the printed interval while his kidney function has silently declined, and whose only clue is a climb from his own previous result. Neither patient exists, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment. Take each figure in such a case to the current guideline and the local protocol, because the interval that helped one hospital may mislead in another.

Where there is no laboratory, the ranges in this appendix do not lose their value, but they change their role. They become the background against which a clinician judges what can be measured by hand, among them the pulse, the breathing rate, the temperature, the pressure, the color of the skin, the passage of urine, and the trend of the patient's appearance over hours. A clinic that can do a urine dipstick and count a pulse still holds two of the most useful measurements in medicine. The decision that follows is rarely a diagnosis but a plan, and the plan asks whether the patient can be watched, whether treatment should begin on the clinical picture, and whether the journey to a hospital must start now. The reasoning survives the loss of the machine, so every figure you remember from this appendix must still be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol.

To summarize, a reference range belongs to a laboratory, a method, and a population, and it illustrates reasoning rather than settling a diagnosis. The values here are gathered by system, from the vital signs and the blood count through the chemistry, the liver and kidney figures, the inflammatory markers, the blood gas, and the urine, with the changes that age, pregnancy, and altitude produce noted as they arise. Each is offered only as the sort of number a report may contain, and each must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the interval is local to the laboratory and the edition in use varies from service to service. Most are more useful as a trend in one patient than as a single measurement compared with a printed boundary, and the patient's own previous results are the best comparison available. This appendix is educational material, it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and no patient described in it is real. A number is a question to be answered in the light of the patient, never an answer on its own.
