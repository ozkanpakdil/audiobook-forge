# Chapter 53 — Infectious Diseases

Infectious disease is the part of medicine where the diagnosis changes while the clinician is still thinking about it, because the organism is multiplying and the patient's defenses are answering at the same time. The reasoning that organizes this chapter is therefore not a list of organisms but a set of questions that each infection answers differently: how quickly the illness arrived, what the patient was exposed to before it began, what state the patient's defenses were in, and which part of the body the infection has chosen to occupy. An illness that reaches its full force within hours tells a different story from one that has crept up across a week, and a patient who was well last month tells a different story from one whose defenses are suppressed by disease or by treatment. Because the same organism can produce a mild illness in one person and a lethal one in another, the clinician reasons about the host as carefully as about the microbe. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient. The same logic is followed in a hospital where a laboratory can name an organism within hours and in a clinic where a stethoscope, a torch and a thermometer are the whole of the technology, and the difference between the two lies in the tools rather than in the reasoning.

Before any comfortable explanation is accepted, the lethal possibilities must be named, each together with the finding that would reveal it. A patient with fever who becomes drowsy or confused, who has a severe headache and a neck that resists being bent forward, and who develops a rash of small dark spots that do not fade when a clear glass is pressed against them may have meningococcal disease, and that rash is the finding that turns a vague febrile illness into an emergency. A patient who has been in a region where malaria is transmitted and who now has fever with drowsiness, a convulsion, deep and labored breathing, or very little urine may have severe malaria, and the finding that reveals it is the parasite seen in a stained film of blood or detected by a rapid test. A patient with fever and bleeding from the gums, the nose or the bowel, or blood under the skin, especially after travel to an area where viral hemorrhagic fever occurs, must be isolated and cared for with personal protective equipment, known as PPE, while expert advice is sought, because that patient may be infectious to everyone who comes near. A patient with a wound, however minor it seemed at the time, who then cannot open the mouth fully, whose face takes on a fixed smile, and whose muscles go into spasm at a sudden noise or a bright light has tetanus, and the spasm provoked by stimulation is the finding that names it. A patient, often a child, who at first appears to have an ordinary viral illness and who then becomes floppy, mottled, cold or difficult to rouse is showing the pattern of a viral illness that is in truth an early bacterial sepsis, and the change in the patient between two observations is the finding that matters most.

The first of the organizing questions is tempo, because how fast an illness has moved narrows the field before any test is ordered. The second question is exposure, and the clinician asks it in a fixed order so that nothing is missed: where the patient has lived and traveled in recent weeks and months, what insects bite in those places, what water is drunk and used for washing, what animals are kept or encountered, what food has been eaten away from home, and whether there has been a bite, a wound, a procedure, an injection, or a device placed inside the body. The third question is the state of the patient's defenses, because the same organism behaves differently in a person whose immunity is intact and in one whose immunity has been weakened by illness, by treatment, by age, or by the loss of a spleen. The fourth is the syndrome, which is the part of the body the infection has occupied, and it directs the examination from the first minute, so the clinician keeps the Oldcarts framework in mind while taking the history and then moves deliberately from the general appearance to the skin, the mouth, the chest, the abdomen and the hidden sites. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is often taught as the line above which fever is present, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be checked against the guideline in force where the clinician works and against the local protocol, because the accepted line differs between countries, has been redrawn between editions, and depends on where the measurement was taken.

Infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, is the diagnosis that changes the meaning of every other infection, and it is considered in any patient whose illnesses are unusual in kind, frequency or severity. The conditions that signal it are a weight loss the patient cannot explain, night sweats, a diarrhea that persists, a white coating on the tongue or the inside of the cheeks, shingles appearing at a young age, repeated infections of the lung or the sinuses, lymph nodes that stay enlarged, and certain tumors of the lymph system. None of those findings proves the diagnosis, and the finding that does prove it is a test performed on the blood or on a sample taken from the mouth, offered with consent and with a conversation about what the result will mean for the patient and for others. The clinician must remember that an early test can be negative while the virus is present, and the interval during which that can happen is described differently by different tests, so the period to be trusted must come from the current guideline and the laboratory's own protocol rather than from memory. A diagnosis of HIV is not the end of the reasoning but the beginning of a new set of questions, because the state of the patient's defenses, the organs already affected and the other infections present all change what must be sought next.

Sexually transmitted infections are approached without judgment, because shame is the reason patients delay and the reason histories remain incomplete. The clinician asks directly about genital sores or ulcers, discharge, pain on passing urine, bleeding between periods, pain during intercourse, and about the number and health of partners, and then asks about the same matters for the partner where that is possible. Some of these infections announce themselves with a single painless ulcer, which may be syphilis and which will heal without treatment while the organism spreads, later producing a rash on the palms and the soles, hair loss, and damage to the heart or the nervous system that appears years afterward. Others produce a discharge and a burning on passing urine, and these may be gonorrhea or infection with chlamydia, which can scar the tubes in a woman and lead later to infertility or to a pregnancy that implants outside the womb. Herpes produces painful blisters that break and crust and may return in episodes, and infection with the human papillomavirus may cause no symptoms at all until a wart or a change on the neck of the womb is found. The examination requires privacy, a good light and a chaperone, and the clinic with a torch can inspect the genitals, the anus and the mouth, although the cervix and the deeper organs need a speculum and, where possible, a laboratory. The most important decision is often not diagnostic but preventive, because an infection in a pregnant woman can pass to the baby and cause infection of the eye, pneumonia, or a serious generalized illness, and the finding that would make treatment during pregnancy the right answer is a positive test or a partner known to be infected.

Malaria is the tropical fever that kills most quickly, and it is placed first among them because a patient who looks comfortable at midday can be dying by night. The exposure history is the key that opens the diagnosis, since a patient who lives in or has traveled to a region where the parasite is transmitted, who has not used the preventive measures available or has used them incompletely, and who has been bitten by mosquitoes during the hours of darkness is a patient in whom malaria must be actively excluded rather than assumed away. The fever may come in waves with a shaking chill, a rise in temperature and heavy sweating as the episode passes, and the patient may be anemic, jaundiced, or have a spleen that can be felt below the ribs. The finding that reveals malaria is the parasite seen under a microscope in a stained film of blood, or its product detected by a rapid test, and neither the pattern nor the height of the fever can replace those tests, because other infections imitate them closely. The signs that mark the change from simple to severe disease are drowsiness or confusion, a convulsion, breathing that is deep and labored, a low passage of urine, dark urine, bleeding, and a falling pressure; a breathing rate above 30 breaths per minute in an adult belongs in that same group, and the level at which each sign becomes alarming is stated differently in different services and editions, so it should be taken from the current guideline and the local protocol rather than recalled from a book. Where a test cannot be performed, the travel history together with the clinical picture must carry the decision, and a patient who cannot be tested and cannot be watched is a patient who must travel to a place where both are possible.

Other tropical fevers are separated by the same exposure history and by the tempo of the illness rather than by geography alone. Dengue arrives suddenly with a severe pain behind the eyes, pain in the muscles and the bones, a rash and a falling platelet count, and the patient who seems to improve as the fever falls is often the one in whom fluid leaks from the circulation and the pressure drops, so the pressure, the pulse and the urine are watched more closely than the temperature. Typhoid climbs in steps across the first days with a headache, a poor appetite, abdominal discomfort and a pulse that is slower than the temperature would suggest, and the organism is grown from blood taken early in the illness, before treatment can remove it from the sample. Leptospirosis follows contact with water or soil contaminated by the urine of animals, and it produces severe pain in the calves, red eyes, headache and sometimes jaundice and injury to the kidney. The rickettsial infections are spread by ticks, fleas and mites, and they may leave a dark scab at the site of the bite with a rash that spreads from the trunk or the limbs, and they are easily mistaken for a viral illness unless the history of bites and of rural or camp exposure is asked.

Viral hemorrhagic fever is the diagnosis that changes how the patient is handled before it is confirmed, and the clinician must act on the possibility rather than on the answer. The pattern that should raise it is a fever with bleeding from the gums, the nose or the bowel, or blood in the skin, in a patient who has recently been in an area where the disease occurs, especially where there has been contact with another person who was ill in the same way. Because the disease spreads through contact with blood and body fluids, the patient is isolated at once, gloves, gown, mask and eye protection are worn, and the number of staff who enter the room is kept to the minimum needed. The clinician does not wait for the laboratory before taking those steps, because the risk is not only to the patient but to everyone who touches the patient and to those who handle the samples afterward. Advice is sought from the service responsible for such infections, and the samples are taken, labeled and transported according to the local protocol rather than by improvisation. The finding that would set the possibility aside is a clear alternative diagnosis that fully explains the bleeding, such as a known disorder of the clotting system or a complication of a medicine, and even then the isolation is lifted only when the responsible clinician is satisfied.

Tetanus is a diagnosis made entirely at the bedside, because no laboratory confirms it and no laboratory is needed. The organism enters through a wound, which may be so small that the patient does not mention it, and it produces a toxin that reaches the nervous system after an interval that varies from days to weeks. The earliest signs are a stiffness in the jaw that makes it hard to open the mouth, difficulty in swallowing, and a stiffness of the neck and the back that differs from the resistance of meningitis, because the patient is fully awake and the muscles are rigid rather than tender. The finding that names the disease is a spasm of the muscles provoked by a sudden noise, a bright light or an attempt to move the patient, and the spasm may be strong enough to arch the back or to stop the breathing, so the patient must be handled gently and treated in a place where the airway can be supported. Prevention is the part of this disease that matters most, and the decision about vaccination and about cleaning a wound belongs to the clinician who sees the injury, guided by the patient's history of immunization and by the current guideline and the local protocol rather than by a remembered schedule. The finding that would make tetanus the wrong diagnosis is a clear alternative cause of the muscle stiffness, such as a reaction to a medicine or an infection of the jaw or the neck.

Meningococcal disease deserves its own paragraph, because the distance between the first symptom and death can be measured in hours and because the patient may look deceptively well at the first examination. It begins as a fever with a headache, an aching body and a dislike of light, and the child may be irritable in a way the parent has not seen before, while a neck that resists being bent forward is the classic sign but may be absent in a young infant, in an older person and in anyone whose defenses are suppressed. The finding that should stop the clinician cold is a rash of small red or purple spots that do not disappear when a clear glass is pressed firmly against the skin, and that finding, together with cold hands, a rapid pulse and a falling pressure, means the patient must be treated immediately and moved to a place where the airway, the breathing and the circulation can be supported. A systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the point at which shock must be assumed, but the figure is offered here as an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the pressure that matters depends on the patient's usual reading, on age and on pregnancy. In a hospital the diagnosis is pursued with blood cultures drawn before treatment and with a sample of the fluid around the spine once the safety of that procedure has been considered, and the organism can sometimes be identified within hours. In a clinic with a torch and a thermometer the decision is made on the rash, the level of consciousness and the pressure, and treatment begins on the clinical picture while the journey to hospital is arranged, because waiting for a result the clinic cannot obtain is not a plan.

Most infections that walk into a clinic are viral and self-limiting, and the work of diagnosis is to recognize the few that are bacterial without treating the many as though they were. A viral illness of the upper airway usually begins with a sore throat, a runny nose, sneezing, a cough and a fever that settles over a few days, and the patient or the parent can often describe a similar illness in the household during the same weeks. A fever that has lasted more than 3 days without localizing signs is a reason to look again, although the interval that should prompt concern is not the same everywhere and must be verified against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the usual course of these illnesses is described differently in different populations and in successive editions. The findings that turn the balance toward a bacterial cause are a fever that persists beyond the expected course, a localized pain or swelling, a discharge of pus, difficulty breathing, a breathing rate that is high for the age, a stiff neck, or a level of alertness that has fallen, and each of these is an answer to the question of where the infection has settled. A sore throat with a high fever, swollen tonsils bearing white patches, tender nodes in the neck, and a cough that is absent is more likely to be bacterial than a sore throat with a cough and a runny nose. In the young child the integrated management of childhood illness approach offers a syndromic method, asking first about the general danger signs and then about the symptoms that name the common infections, and it is deliberately designed for the clinic where no laboratory exists. The clinician who uses such a framework must still say what it excludes and what it assumes, because a rule tested in one population can mislead in another.

The patient whose defenses are not ordinary is the patient in whom the usual signs are least reliable, and a short list of such patients should run through the clinician's mind with every fever. A newborn with a fever, or even with a low temperature and poor feeding, is an emergency, because the signs of serious infection are subtle and the illness can progress within hours. An older patient may have no fever at all and may present instead with a fall, a confusion, a refusal to eat or a loss of continence, and the finding that matters is a change from the patient's usual state rather than any single measurement. A patient without a spleen, a patient receiving treatment that suppresses the immune response, a patient with a cancer of the blood or the lymph system, a patient with a line or a device in a vein, and a patient with a replaced joint or heart valve are all at risk of infections that behave differently and may need treatment before the organism is named. In each of these patients the threshold for seeking help and for starting treatment is lower rather than higher, and the finding that would make treatment the wrong answer is a clear non-infectious explanation that accounts for the whole picture.

In a hospital the reasoning is the same, but the laboratory shortens each loop of it by naming the organism sooner. Blood cultures taken before the first treatment are the single most valuable samples in a patient with suspected infection of the bloodstream, and the timing of the draw matters more than the number of bottles, because the organism leaves the blood in waves and a sample taken at the right moment may be the only one that grows. A sample of urine, a sample of sputum, a swab from a wound or a throat, and a sample of fluid from a collection each answer a particular question, and the clinician should be able to say what a positive or a negative result would change before the sample is sent. Staining the sample and looking at it under the microscope can give a first answer quickly, and methods that detect the organism's own material, such as the polymerase chain reaction, can identify it faster still, but each method has a limit, and a negative result never cancels a clinical picture that is clear. The patient who is drowsy, breathing fast, cold at the extremities or passing very little urine belongs in the intensive care unit, known as ICU, and the decision to move that patient is made on the clinical findings rather than on the result of a test, while the decisions about supporting the airway, giving extra oxygen or giving fluids follow the measured level and the clinical picture rather than any figure carried in a book. The laboratory is an extension of the reasoning and not a replacement for it, and a clinician who treats every positive result as a disease and every negative result as reassurance will be wrong in both directions.

Where no laboratory exists, the decision is made on the syndrome and then reviewed as the patient declares himself, and this is a discipline rather than a compromise. The clinician asks the four organizing questions of tempo, exposure, defenses and syndrome, performs a complete examination of the skin, the mouth, the throat, the chest, the abdomen, the joints and the hidden sites, and then states plainly what is believed and what remains uncertain. The plan that follows takes one of three shapes: the patient can be watched with a defined time set for review, the patient should begin treatment now on the clinical findings, or the patient must travel to a hospital without delay. Whichever is chosen, the patient or the family is told exactly which signs should bring them back or send them onward, among them a fever that will not settle, a new drowsiness or confusion, difficulty breathing, a rash that does not fade when pressed, a stiff neck, a reduced passage of urine, or a deterioration the family can see for itself. Treatment begun on a clinical case is reviewed when the patient returns, because the diagnosis at the first visit is a hypothesis and the second visit is where it is tested, and a patient who is not improving as expected is a patient whose diagnosis should be reconsidered rather than treated harder. The clinician who records the reasoning, the uncertainty and the plan for review has done the most valuable thing that can be done without a laboratory.

The decision about treatment is part of diagnosis, and in infection it is often made before the organism is known. Treatment is likely to be the right answer when the patient looks seriously ill, when there are signs that an organ is failing, when the defenses are suppressed, when a life-threatening cause such as malaria or meningitis is plausible, or when the patient cannot be observed and the chance to treat may not come again. Treatment is likely to be the wrong answer when the picture is clearly a mild viral illness with no localizing sign and no danger sign, when a non-infectious cause explains everything, or when giving treatment before the samples are taken would destroy the evidence on which the diagnosis depends. The clinician should also weigh the cost of treating everyone, because unnecessary treatment exposes the patient to harm, hides the real diagnosis, and breeds resistance that will make the next patient harder to help. Prevention belongs in the same reasoning, since the decision to offer a vaccine, to clean and cover a wound, to provide a bed net, or to advise on safe water and food is a diagnostic act when it rests on the exposure history the clinician has just taken. The details of every preventive decision, including who should receive a vaccine and when, are set by the current guideline and the local protocol and are not carried in this chapter, because the schedules differ between countries and are revised between editions. What this chapter carries is the finding that would make a decision right, the finding that would make it wrong, and the honesty to say when the answer is not yet known.

To summarize, infectious disease is organized by four questions that every infection answers differently, which are how fast the illness moved, what the patient was exposed to, what state the patient's defenses were in, and which part of the body the infection has occupied. The lethal possibilities are named where the presentation appears and not deferred, among them meningococcal disease with its rash that does not fade, severe malaria with its drowsiness and labored breathing, a viral hemorrhagic fever with its fever and bleeding, tetanus with its spasm provoked by stimulation, and the ordinary-looking viral illness that is in truth an early bacterial sepsis. Infection with the human immunodeficiency virus is sought through the conditions that signal it; sexually transmitted infections are taken without judgment and with attention to the partner and to pregnancy; the tropical fevers are separated by exposure and tempo; and the common viral illnesses are distinguished from the bacterial ones that imitate them by the focal signs, the duration and the trajectory. Every figure in this chapter, whether a temperature, a pressure, a rate, a duration, an interval or a reference range, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. The same logic runs in the hospital where an organism can be named within hours and in the clinic where the decision is made on the syndrome and reviewed as the patient declares himself, and this chapter is educational material that does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A patient who is deteriorating is treated and helped first, and the organism is named afterward.
