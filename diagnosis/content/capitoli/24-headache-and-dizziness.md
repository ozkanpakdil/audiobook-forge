# Chapter 24 — Headache and Dizziness

Headache and dizziness most often mean nothing and occasionally mean everything, and the clinician must sort one from the other before the patient has finished speaking. Both are symptoms rather than diagnoses, shaped by age, setting, and speed of onset as much as by the quality of the pain. This chapter is organized by pattern and by triage rather than by a list of diseases, because a pattern tells the clinician what to do next while a label only names what has happened. It is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and what follows is a way of thinking to be practiced with a teacher rather than instructions to be applied alone.

The dangerous causes of headache must be named where the symptom is described, because each hides behind the ordinary and each is revealed by one finding. Subarachnoid hemorrhage produces a headache that reaches full intensity almost at once, often called the worst of a lifetime, sometimes with vomiting or a brief loss of consciousness. Meningitis reveals itself through fever, neck stiffness, photophobia, a rash that does not fade when pressed, and a consciousness that does not match how well the patient looks. Raised intracranial pressure betrays itself by pain worse on waking and lying flat, by vomiting without nausea, and by a swollen optic disc. Cerebral venous thrombosis is suggested by a headache that worsens steadily in a young woman who is pregnant or recently delivered. Temporal arteritis in an older patient warns through scalp tenderness, jaw pain on chewing, and any disturbance of vision. A stroke in the posterior circulation may present with headache together with vertigo, unsteadiness, double vision, or slurred speech rather than weakness of a limb.

The dangerous causes of dizziness deserve the same naming. A posterior circulation stroke can present with vertigo alone, especially when the patient cannot stand or walk, and a cerebellar hemorrhage can imitate a benign inner ear disturbance until the patient suddenly deteriorates. A cardiac cause is named by an irregular pulse, a murmur of narrowing in the aortic valve, or a faint during exertion rather than after it. Serious blood loss shows itself through a rapid pulse, a falling pressure, pallor, and a history of bleeding from the gut, while anemia, infection, and dehydration produce a similar complaint and are separated by simple measurements. The clinician who asks first what would kill this patient, and second what is most likely, will order the search correctly in both halves of the chapter.

Headache is read by pattern before it is read by diagnosis, and the patterns are defined mainly by time. The sudden headache arrives in an instant and demands exclusion of bleeding, while the progressive headache builds over days or weeks and points toward a mass, an infection, a venous thrombosis, or a rise in pressure within the skull. The postural headache changes with the position of the head, one kind worsening on standing and another on lying flat. The recurrent headache returns in the same shape again and again and usually means a primary headache disorder once a secondary cause has been considered, while the chronic daily headache has usually been present for months and forces a review of what the patient has been taking. The intervals separating one pattern from another are conventions rather than fixed borders, so confirm the definitions your service uses against the current guideline and the local protocol.

The sudden headache is the one that most often carries a lethal cause and must be treated as an emergency until explained. A subarachnoid hemorrhage usually produces its worst pain within seconds, and that pain may then settle, so a patient who feels better at the consultation is not thereby safe. A smaller warning bleed may occur days earlier and is easily dismissed as a strain or a migraine, so any severe and unusual recent headache deserves a direct question. The examination looks for neck stiffness, photophobia, a changed level of consciousness, and weakness of an eye movement, since a nerve supplying the eye can be compressed by blood at the base of the brain. A computed tomography, or CT, scan of the head is the first step where imaging exists, and its ability to detect fresh blood falls as time passes from the bleed. A scan in the first hours is far more likely to show blood than one after a day or more, but those intervals are illustrations rather than laws, so confirm the timing your service relies on against the current guideline and the local protocol, and if the scan is negative while the story stays suspicious, the decision about further testing belongs to a clinician with the full picture.

Meningitis and encephalitis are the infections that turn a headache into a race, and their signs are sought in every patient with fever and headache. The neck resists flexion, the light hurts the eyes, the patient may vomit, and consciousness may fall out of proportion to the fever. A rash that does not fade when pressed with clear glass or a finger should stop the consultation and start the response, because it suggests bleeding into the skin from a bloodstream infection. Kernig and Brudzinski described maneuvers that stretch the inflamed membranes around the brain and spinal cord, and though neither is reliable alone, a positive result adds weight to a picture that already concerns the clinician, and a falling consciousness, focal weakness, or seizure may mean that raised pressure must be considered before the fluid around the spinal cord is sampled. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is treated as a fever in many settings, but the figure that matters is the one your own service uses, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol.

A raised pressure inside the skull produces a headache with a character of its own, and recognizing it may prevent a catastrophe. The pain is often worst on waking, worse lying flat, and worse when the patient coughs, strains, or bends forward, and vomiting may occur without preceding nausea and may briefly relieve the headache. Vision may blur, the optic disc may swell, and one eye may turn outward because the nerve that moves it is stretched along the base of the brain. The safest way to look for the swollen disc is to darken the room, ask the patient to look at a distant point, and examine the back of the eye with whatever instrument is available; a torch held at the outer corner of the eye can sometimes show enough to raise or lower suspicion. A lumbar puncture in the presence of raised pressure can cause the brain to shift, so the revealing finding is the swollen disc together with the postural pattern of the pain, and either alone is a reason to seek help rather than wait.

Cerebral venous thrombosis is most often missed because its headache looks ordinary and its first scan can look normal. It is a clot in the veins draining blood from the brain, and it occurs more often in young women, after childbirth, in dehydration, in inherited clotting conditions, and in people using hormonal contraception. The headache commonly builds gradually and may worsen on lying flat or straining, and it may come with vomiting, seizures, weakness on one side, or a change in behavior or speech, while a swollen optic disc may be seen even when the clot is invisible on a plain scan. The clinician who waits for a definitive image before considering the possibility may lose time that matters, because the treatment decision depends on recognizing the pattern and arranging the right test. When a young person has a new and progressive headache with any of these features, the question should be asked aloud and the answer sought from imaging able to show the veins as well as the brain.

Temporal arteritis is the headache emergency of the older patient, and it threatens sight rather than life. It causes a new headache in a person usually older than 50 years, and the pain may sit over the temple, may be tender to touch, and may be joined by jaw pain on chewing and by pain in the muscles of the shoulders and hips. Any disturbance of vision, whether brief blurring, double vision, or sudden loss of sight in one eye, changes the situation at once. The clinician should feel the scalp arteries, look at the back of the eye, and arrange the blood tests that reflect inflammation, but when the story is strong the decision to begin treatment may have to be made before those results return. The age of 50 years is a common teaching rather than a sharp boundary, so confirm the current guideline and the local protocol for your population instead of treating the figure as a rule, and remember that the revealing finding is the tender, thickened artery with jaw pain and visual symptoms, while the finding that makes treatment wrong is a clear alternative cause with no inflammation anywhere.

Several other secondary headaches are worth keeping in mind during a busy clinic. A sudden painful red eye with a fixed, mid-sized pupil and blurred vision suggests a rise in pressure inside the eye that can damage sight quickly and demands immediate attention. Pain over the cheek or forehead that worsens when the head bends forward, with a blocked nose and fever, suggests sinus infection, while pain beginning in the neck and spreading to the back of the head, worse on neck movement, points to the joints and muscles of the cervical spine. A headache that appears every day and is relieved by a painkiller only to return as its effect wears off suggests the treatment itself has become the cause, and the answer is a planned withdrawal rather than another tablet. Carbon monoxide poisoning causes headache with nausea and confusion that improves on leaving a building and returns on going back inside, and it should be suspected when more than one person in a household is affected. Pregnancy brings its own causes, including a rise in pressure that endangers mother and child.

The primary headaches are far more common than the dangerous ones, and the task is to recognize them without letting familiarity dull the search. Tension-type headache is usually a band-like pressure on both sides, mild to moderate, without vomiting and without worsening on ordinary movement. Migraine is often one-sided, throbbing, moderate to severe, worse with movement and light, and accompanied by nausea, and it may be preceded by a visual or sensory disturbance that spreads slowly across the vision. Cluster headache is a severe pain around one eye with redness, tearing, a drooping eyelid, and a blocked or running nose on the same side, and it comes in bouts that may wake the patient from sleep. A previous identical pattern is reassuring but not conclusive, and the trap is to attribute a new and severe headache to a familiar label, so the safeguard is to ask what is different about this attack before accepting it.

Migraine deserves more attention because it is so often both the correct answer and the wrong one. Its aura is a reversible disturbance that develops gradually, and it may take the form of flashing lights, a spreading blind spot, tingling across the face and hand, or difficulty finding words, while the headache that follows is often one-sided, throbbing, and worse with movement. A patient who has had exactly this sequence many times and is now in it again is usually having another migraine, and the clinician can be reasonably confident once the dangerous patterns have been considered. The same patient with a headache different in onset, site, severity, or accompanying features is not having a routine attack, and that difference is the finding that matters. A migraine that is new after middle age, or that comes with fever, neck stiffness, a change in consciousness, or a new weakness, requires a different search altogether.

The patient's circumstances change the probability of each cause, and the clinician should let them do so. A new headache in a person with the human immunodeficiency virus, or HIV, or with another condition weakening the immune defenses raises the possibility of an infection or a tumor behaving differently from the usual picture. A patient with known cancer may have spread to the brain or its membranes, and a patient who has had a recent fall or a spinal procedure may have bleeding or a leak of spinal fluid. Pregnancy brings a rise in blood pressure and clotting that can threaten the brain, and the period after delivery carries a particular risk of venous thrombosis. Children may not describe a headache well, and their presentation is often dominated by vomiting, irritability, or a change in behavior, so a parent's report deserves as much weight as the child's words, and an older patient may have more than one cause at once.

Dizziness is a word covering several different experiences, and the first task is to find out which one the patient is describing. Vertigo is the false sense that the world or the patient is spinning or moving, and it points toward the vestibular system, whether in the inner ear or in the brain. Presyncope is the sense of being about to faint, with dimming vision, warmth, sweating, and nausea, and it points toward the circulation. Disequilibrium is unsteadiness on the feet without a false sense of movement, and it points toward the balance system as a whole, including the feet, the joints, the eyes, and the cerebellum. The vague light-headedness that fits none of these may arise from anemia, infection, dehydration, low blood sugar, anxiety, poor sleep, or a medication, and the clinician should ask the patient to choose among these descriptions rather than accepting the single word dizziness as an answer.

Once vertigo is described, the next question is whether the cause lies in the inner ear or in the brain, because the second is the dangerous one. Vertigo of inner ear origin is usually severe, rotational, and accompanied by nausea and sweating, and it is often worse on head movement, but the patient can usually stand and walk with support. Central vertigo is more often joined by an inability to stand or walk, by double vision, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, numbness or weakness on one side, or a headache or neck pain, and any of these shifts the balance toward the brain. The nystagmus, the rhythmic movement of the eyes, is examined by asking the patient to follow a finger and then look straight ahead, and its direction, persistence, and response to fixation all carry information. In inner ear disease the fast phase usually beats away from the affected ear and is reduced by fixation, while in brain disease the nystagmus may change direction with gaze, may be vertical, and may persist or worsen with fixation, and that difference is one of the most useful signs at the bedside.

The stroke in the posterior circulation is the diagnosis that must not be missed in a dizzy patient, and its features are assembled rather than found in any single sign. The patient may have vertigo and may also be unable to stand or walk even though the limbs are strong when tested in bed, and that inability is a serious finding. There may be double vision, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, facial numbness, loss of coordination in one arm or leg, or a sensory change on one side of the body and the opposite side of the face. A new headache or neck pain may accompany it, and the patient may be drowsy or behave unusually. The clinician can test the reflex that stabilizes the eyes when the head is turned quickly, can look for nystagmus, and can check whether the eyes are aligned by covering one at a time while the patient looks at a target, and a combination of abnormal results raises the suspicion of a central cause. The patient who cannot walk, or who deteriorates while being observed, needs urgent help rather than watchful waiting.

The common inner ear causes each have their own pattern. Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo is brief and intense, brought on by a particular change of position such as rolling over in bed or looking up, and it lasts only seconds. The maneuver described by Dix and Hallpike places the patient flat with the head turned and lowered to one side, and it reproduces the vertigo after a short delay with a rotational nystagmus that fades if the position is held; the same maneuver can reposition the crystals that cause it. Vestibular neuritis is a single severe attack of vertigo developing over hours, with nausea and unsteadiness but no hearing loss, and labyrinthitis is the same picture with hearing loss or ringing. Meniere disease causes episodes of vertigo with fluctuating hearing loss and a feeling of fullness in the ear, and vestibular migraine causes episodes in a patient who also has migraines. A new one-sided hearing loss deserves particular attention, and the durations separating these attacks are taught differently in different places, so confirm the definitions used in your own service against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Presyncope is the second great category, and it is a symptom of the circulation rather than the ear. The patient feels faint, vision dims or closes in, the skin becomes warm or clammy, and there may be nausea, and the episode often ends when the patient sits or lies down. A faint during exertion, or while lying flat, is more concerning than one after standing a long time in a warm room. The examination looks for an irregular or slow pulse, a murmur suggesting narrowing of the aortic valve, a difference in the pulses of the two arms, and any sign of bleeding from the gut. The blood pressure is measured lying and then standing, and a fall of 20 millimeters of mercury in the systolic pressure or 10 millimeters of mercury in the diastolic pressure supports a postural drop, though those figures are commonly quoted illustrations, so confirm the definition your service uses against the current guideline and the local protocol. An electrocardiogram, or ECG, is essential when available, because a disturbed rhythm may be the whole explanation and may be intermittent.

Disequilibrium is the third category, and it is examined by watching the patient walk rather than by asking about spinning. The clinician asks the patient to stand with feet together and eyes open, then with eyes closed, and a marked increase in swaying when the eyes close points toward a problem with position sense from the feet and legs. Walking heel to toe, turning, and walking while the examiner watches from the side reveal a great deal, and an unsteady gait worse in the dark or on uneven ground suggests a sensory rather than a cerebellar problem. The cerebellum produces a wide-based, staggering gait with poor limb coordination, and a stroke or tumor there can present this way. Weakness of the legs, joint pain, a neuropathy from diabetes or alcohol, poor vision, and the fear of falling after a previous fall all contribute, and the gait examination is also a test of safety because the patient who is unsteady is at risk of a fracture.

The vague light-headedness that fits none of the previous categories is a diagnostic problem in its own right, and its causes are frequently outside the nervous system. Anemia reduces the oxygen carried in the blood and produces tiredness, breathlessness on effort, pallor, and a racing pulse as well as light-headedness. Infection, fever, and dehydration reduce the effective volume of the circulation and produce the same complaint, and low blood sugar produces sweating, hunger, confusion, and tremor that improve when the patient eats. Anxiety and rapid breathing can produce light-headedness, tingling in the hands and around the mouth, and a sense of unreality, and a medication that lowers the blood pressure, a sedative, or a drug affecting the inner ear can each be the cause. Poor sleep, low fitness, and a long illness also produce a persistent light-headedness that is real even though no single organ can be blamed, and the patient deserves an explanation rather than a dismissal.

The examination of the dizzy patient is a complete examination of the nervous system, the ears, the eyes, and the circulation, performed in a fixed order so that nothing is omitted. The clinician looks at the ears for discharge, scarring, or a blister-like rash in the canal that might indicate a viral infection of the nerve, and tests hearing by whispering or rubbing the fingers near each ear in turn. The eyes are examined for redness, pupil size and reaction, range of movement, and nystagmus, and the back of the eye is examined for swelling of the disc. The heart is examined for a murmur and an irregular rhythm, and the pulses in the neck and limbs are felt, because a narrowed neck artery can produce both dizziness and a stroke. The blood pressure is measured in both arms and again after standing, the temperature is taken, and the gait, coordination, and neck are examined.

In a hospital with immediate imaging, the examination decides which image is needed and how quickly. A patient with a sudden severe headache is usually scanned first without contrast, and if the story remains suspicious after a normal scan, the blood vessels or the fluid around the spinal cord may need further study. A patient with vertigo and any feature suggesting the brain is involved is imaged with magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, because it shows the back of the brain and the brainstem better than a plain scan. A patient with suspected infection may need the fluid around the spinal cord examined, but only after raised pressure has been considered, because the test can be dangerous when the pressure is high, and a patient with suspected temporal arteritis may have the artery examined by ultrasound or by a small sample sent to the laboratory. Each choice is guided by the history and examination rather than by the machine, and the clinician should state what is expected before the image is taken, so that a surprise is recognized as one.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a thermometer, and a torch, the same reasoning is performed with fewer instruments, and the decision replaces the image. The torch is used to look at the pupils, the eye movements, the back of the eye in a darkened room, the ear canal, and the throat. The thermometer turns the patient's sense of being unwell into a number, and the watch allows the pulse to be counted rather than guessed, which is essential when a faint or dizziness may be caused by a disturbed rhythm. The stethoscope reveals a murmur of narrowing in the aortic valve, an irregular heartbeat, or a bruit over the neck artery, and the cuff, if one exists, allows the pressure to be measured lying and standing. The clinician tests gait, hearing, coordination, and neck, and asks directly about rash, stiff neck, recent fall, pregnancy, and bleeding. Where no imaging exists the decision is not a diagnosis but a plan, and the clinic should state honestly that it cannot exclude a small bleed, an early stroke, or a clot in the veins of the brain, so a normal examination in a worrying story is a reason to repeat it rather than a license to send the patient home.

Fundoscopy with a simple torch is a skill worth practicing, because it is the one test that can reveal raised pressure where no other instrument exists. The patient sits in the darkest room available, looks at a distant object such as a light or a mark on the far wall, and keeps the eyes still. The examiner stands slightly to the side, holds the torch at the outer corner of the eye and shines the beam through the pupil, then looks along the beam into the back of the eye from a short distance away, moving closer as the view allows. What the examiner looks for is the pale disc where the optic nerve leaves the eye, its margins, its color compared with the other side, and the dark vessels crossing it, and a disc that is swollen, blurred at the margins, or ringed by a pale halo is the finding that matters. The torch technique is imperfect and may miss a swelling that a formal examination would find, but a swollen disc found with a torch is serious, and a normal-looking disc found with a torch does not exclude raised pressure.

To summarize, headache and dizziness are symptoms whose meaning is carried by their pattern, their speed, and the company they keep, and the clinician's first duty is to name the lethal causes before settling on the likely ones. Subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningitis, raised intracranial pressure, cerebral venous thrombosis, a stroke in the posterior circulation, and temporal arteritis in the older patient are each revealed by a finding that can be sought in the history or at the bedside, and each changes the pace and place of care. Vertigo, presyncope, disequilibrium, and vague light-headedness are separated by the patient's own words, by examination of the eyes and gait, and by a few simple measurements. Every figure in this chapter, whether an interval, an age, a pressure, a temperature, a rate, or a proportion, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such values differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This material is educational and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen, and no patient described here is real, so when the story is worrying and the examination is reassuring, the answer is to repeat the examination and to seek help, not to relax.
