# Chapter 52 — Musculoskeletal and Rheumatology

The musculoskeletal and rheumatic diseases arrive as pain, swelling, or stiffness, and they are organized less by the name of a disease than by the pattern in which the joints and their surrounding tissues are involved. The first questions are therefore structural: how many joints are affected, whether the problem came over hours or over months, and whether it behaves as inflammation or as wear. Those three answers, read together with what the rest of the body shows, reduce a vast list to a few possibilities that can be tested at the bedside. A joint complaint is never only a joint complaint, because the diseases that inflame the synovium can also inflame the skin, the eye, the kidney, the lung, the gut, and the blood vessels. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

The causes that kill or cripple must be named before the comfortable ones, and each carries a finding that betrays it. A single hot, swollen joint is an infection until the fluid proves otherwise, and the finding is a joint the patient refuses to move, with pain at the slightest attempt. Infection of the spine, in the disc, the vertebral body, or the space around the cord, gives pain that wakes the patient at night and is not relieved by rest, often with fever and tenderness over the bone, and the emergency is revealed by new weakness, numbness in the saddle area, or difficulty passing urine or stool. A vasculitic emergency declares itself through the organ it attacks, whether a new headache with jaw pain and disturbed vision in an older patient, a rash of spots that do not fade when pressed, a foot that has suddenly dropped, blood in the urine, or breathlessness with bleeding into the lungs. A fracture dismissed as a strain is another disabling miss, because an older patient who cannot bear weight after a minor fall, or whose back pain followed a stumble, may have a break that the first radiograph does not show. Bone pain at rest and worse at night, in a patient with weight loss or a known cancer, belongs in the same list. These possibilities are named first so that the search begins with them rather than arriving at them last.

The number of joints involved is the first filter, because diseases of one joint are few and urgent while diseases of many joints are numerous and mostly chronic. Arthritis in a single joint has a short list: infection, a crystal, trauma, and a tumor, with infection and crystal first because both can destroy a joint quickly if missed. Arthritis in a few joints adds the infections spread through the blood, the crystal diseases, and the forms of spondyloarthritis. Arthritis in many joints opens the broad territory of the inflammatory diseases, among them rheumatoid arthritis and lupus, together with the viral illnesses that imitate them for a few weeks and then leave. The count is not a diagnosis, but it tells the clinician how much danger to assume before any test is ordered. A patient whose joint count is rising over successive examinations is a different patient from one whose single joint has been unchanged for a year.

The speed with which the problem arrived carries almost as much information as the count. A joint that becomes hot and swollen over hours is an emergency, because infection and crystals both behave that way and there is no time to be wrong. A joint that becomes painful over days or a couple of weeks suggests an inflammatory arthritis or an infection declaring itself slowly, while a problem present for months suggests either a chronic inflammatory disease or a degenerative one. The tempo must be confirmed with the patient rather than assumed, because patients often date the beginning to the moment the pain became unbearable. A useful question is what the patient could do a month ago that is now impossible, since function changes earlier and more honestly than pain. The boundary between acute and chronic is a convention rather than a fact, and the interval used in any guideline must be checked against the current edition and the local protocol, because services draw it differently.

Whether the process behaves as inflammation or as wear is the distinction that organizes the whole chapter, and it is made from the history as much as from the examination. Inflammatory arthritis produces stiffness that is worst after rest, especially in the early morning, and that eases as the joint is used, so the patient describes a slow start to the day and feels better by the afternoon. Degenerative disease behaves in the opposite way: the joint is stiff for a few minutes after rest, hurts more as activity accumulates, and is often easier after a night of sleep. Inflammatory disease brings swelling that feels boggy rather than bony, warmth, and a general sense of illness, while degenerative disease produces bony enlargement, grating, and no systemic features. Morning stiffness lasting longer than about an hour is the figure most often taught as favoring inflammation, but that interval is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted duration varies between services and editions.

Infection of a joint is the diagnosis that must be excluded in every patient with a single hot joint, and it is more easily missed than it should be. The classical picture is fever with a joint that is red, hot, and swollen, held still because movement is unbearable, but the fever may be absent, and in the very young, the very old, the patient with a prosthetic joint, the patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, and the patient who injects drugs the picture may be muted. The knee and the hip are affected most often, and an infection of the hip in a child or an older patient may present only as a refusal to walk or as pain referred to the thigh or the knee. An organism usually reaches a joint through the bloodstream, so the clinician looks for a source in the skin, the teeth, the urine, the gut, or a recent procedure, and takes blood cultures before treatment begins, because cultures drawn afterwards are far less useful. Infection with gonorrhea and other sexually transmitted infections can also settle in a joint, and that possibility changes the questions asked and the examinations performed. The decision that follows is made on the clinical picture and cannot wait for certainty, so a hot joint with a fever is treated as an infection until the fluid says otherwise.

Gout and septic arthritis can look identical at the bedside, and separating them is the central exercise of this chapter. Gout usually arrives at night in a patient who was well the day before, reaches its worst within hours, and is so tender that even the weight of a sheet is intolerable, and the first attack often involves the joint at the base of the big toe. The patient may report earlier attacks that settled on their own, heavy alcohol use, a diet rich in meat and seafood, medicines that raise the uric acid level, or a recent illness or operation that provoked the attack. Examination may show tophi, which are firm deposits under the skin near the joint or on the ear. The two conditions overlap in every direction: gout can produce fever and a raised inflammatory response, an infected joint can contain crystals that were already there, and both can occur together in the same patient. Calcium pyrophosphate crystals produce a very similar attack, often in an older patient and often in the knee or the wrist, and only the crystal seen in the fluid separates them. The level of uric acid in the blood is often normal during an acute attack of gout, so a normal value does not exclude it, and a raised value between attacks does not prove that the current joint is the cause. Because the treatments point in opposite directions, the bedside judgment is a probability and not a proof; only the fluid examined under a polarizing microscope gives the answer, and a hot joint with a fever is safer treated as an infection.

Among the many-jointed inflammatory diseases, rheumatoid arthritis is the one the listener should recognize from the hands alone. It is symmetric, it favors the small joints of the hands and feet, and it spares the joints nearest the fingertips while damaging the knuckles and the wrists, which is the opposite of the pattern in degenerative disease. The patient describes morning stiffness that lasts a long time, swelling that comes and goes, and a tiredness out of proportion to the joint pain, and over months the joints may drift into deformity. Examination looks for boggy swelling of the knuckles, tenderness along the joints of the feet, firm nodules under the skin near the elbow, and the signs of a more aggressive disease elsewhere. The antibodies measured in the blood support the diagnosis but do not make it, and the reference range and the meaning of a positive result must be confirmed against the current guideline and the laboratory's own protocol, because the assays differ between centers. The dangers in a patient who looks like this are an infection in one of the affected joints, an infection elsewhere imitating a flare, and a disease of the blood or the bone marrow producing joint pain as an early sign.

A different pattern of inflammatory disease targets the spine and the places where tendon meets bone, and it appears in younger patients as back pain that behaves in a way ordinary back pain does not. The pain is worse at rest and in the second half of the night, it improves with exercise and not with rest, and it usually begins gradually in a young adult. The examination looks for tenderness where tendons insert at the heel, for loss of the normal curve and movement of the lower spine, and for the extra-articular clues that travel with this group, among them a scaly skin condition, an eye that is red and painful, and inflammation of the bowel. A particular inherited tissue type is more common in these patients, but it is not a test for the disease, because many people carry it without ever becoming ill. Chronic back pain of this kind must also be distinguished from infection and from a tumor, because both can produce night pain and stiffness in the same region. Where the pattern is inflammatory and the patient is young, the decision is to pursue the diagnosis rather than to treat the pain as mechanical.

Back pain becomes a different problem when it is caused by infection or by a tumor, and the features that point that way must be sought deliberately. Infection of the spine is more likely in a patient with diabetes, a patient who injects drugs, a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, a patient on dialysis, and a patient who has had a recent bloodstream infection or a procedure on the spine. The pain is usually constant, worse at night, not relieved by rest, and often accompanied by tenderness when the clinician presses or taps over the affected vertebra, and the patient may look unwell without any obvious fever. Tuberculosis of the spine develops slowly, produces weight loss and night sweats, and can leave a knuckle-shaped deformity of the backbone when several vertebrae are destroyed. A cancer that has spread to the bone, and the cancer of the bone marrow that produces anemia and bone pain, behave in a similar way, and a patient with a known tumor who develops new back pain must be assumed to have disease in the spine until that is excluded. The finding that turns any of these into an emergency is new weakness in the legs, a band of numbness around the trunk, difficulty passing urine or stool, or a loss of bladder or bowel control, because these mean the cord is being compressed. That patient needs magnetic resonance imaging and an urgent decision about decompression, and the interval within which the pressure must be relieved is a local protocol matter to be confirmed in the service where the patient is cared for.

Osteoporosis produces no symptoms at all until it produces a fracture, which is why it is described as presenting with its consequence rather than before it. The bone loses mass and its internal architecture becomes fragile over years, and the patient notices nothing until a fall that would have been harmless breaks a wrist, a hip, or a vertebra. The history that should raise the suspicion includes a fracture after a fall from standing height or less, a loss of height, a stoop, a previous fragility fracture in the patient or a parent, early menopause, a low body weight, long treatment with steroids, smoking, heavy alcohol use, and a diet poor in calcium and vitamin D. Examination looks for the stoop of the upper spine, for tenderness over a compressed vertebra, and for the diseases and medicines that cause bone loss, and it also looks for the reasons the patient fell, because a fracture is usually a fall plus a fragile bone. Measurement of bone density by a special form of radiograph gives a figure compared with a reference population, and that figure and the reference range must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local laboratory protocol, because the equipment, the population, and the reporting convention differ between centers. A fracture can be invisible on the first radiograph, especially in the hip and in some small bones of the wrist and foot, so the injury dismissed as a strain is often this very one, and a patient who cannot bear weight after a fall needs a second image rather than reassurance. The rule that serves is that a patient who cannot use the limb, or whose pain is not settling as a strain should, needs imaging and review arranged rather than assumed.

The vasculitides are diseases in which the blood vessels themselves are inflamed, and they present through whatever organ the inflamed vessels supply rather than through the joints. A large-vessel disease of older patients produces a new headache, tenderness of the scalp, pain in the jaw when chewing, and disturbed vision, and the finding that must never be missed is a sudden loss of vision, which can become permanent within hours and may be preceded by brief episodes of blindness. A disease of the medium and small vessels produces a rash of spots that do not fade when a clear glass is pressed on them, ulcers of the skin, pain and weakness in a limb from inflamed nerves, blood in the urine, and bleeding into the lungs, and any one of these findings in a patient who also has joint pain changes the whole assessment. The kidney is involved more often than any other organ in this group, and the finding is blood and protein in the urine together with a rising waste product in the blood, so a urine test belongs in the assessment of every patient with a suspected rheumatic disease. The inflammatory markers in the blood are often raised, but a normal value does not exclude these diseases, and the definitive answer usually comes from a sample of inflamed tissue examined under the microscope. The decision to treat before that answer is confirmed is made on the danger of the presentation, and the finding that would make it wrong is a confirmed alternative diagnosis that fully explains the picture in a patient whose organs are not threatened.

Lupus is recognized through the way it involves many systems at once rather than through a single criterion, and a clinician who waits for one test to settle the question will miss it. The patient is usually a young woman, though the disease occurs in men and at every age, and the symptoms accumulate over months, among them joint pain and swelling, extreme tiredness, and episodes of fever without an obvious cause. The skin findings include a rash over the cheeks and the bridge of the nose that spares the folds beside it, a rash in the sunlight, sores in the mouth, and hair loss, and the joints are painful and swollen without the destruction seen in rheumatoid arthritis. The serious manifestations involve the kidneys, the nervous system, the blood, the lining of the lungs and heart, and the clotting system, and each is sought rather than waited for, because the disease can be quiet in the joints while it is damaging an organ. The antibodies used in the diagnosis support the impression, but the criteria that list them were built to classify patients for research rather than to diagnose an individual, so their contents must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local laboratory protocol. Because infection is common in these patients and can imitate a flare, and because treating a flare can worsen an infection, the clinician must look deliberately for an infection in every patient who appears to be deteriorating. The finding that changes the decision is a clear source of infection, and the finding that demands action is a new fall in the blood count, a rise in the waste products of the kidney, or a new neurological sign.

The examination of a joint follows the same order as the examination of any other part of the body, and it is performed on both sides for comparison. Inspection looks at the posture of the limb, swelling, redness, wasting of the muscle above and below the joint, and the way the patient stands and walks, because a joint that cannot take weight matters more than a joint that merely aches. Palpation uses the back of the hand to compare warmth, then the fingers to feel for fluid, boggy swelling, bony enlargement, nodules, and tenderness along the joint line, and the squeeze test across the joint detects inflammation of the whole joint rather than of one spot. Movement is tested actively and then passively, and the difference between the two is important: pain only at the end of the range suggests a mechanical block or a strained structure, while pain throughout the movement suggests inflammation of the joint itself. The knee is examined for fluid by pressing it from one side to the other and watching for a bulge, and the hip is examined by rotating the whole leg and measuring how far the limb moves in each direction. The single most useful sign in separating inflammation from wear is warmth felt over the joint compared with the other side, provided the examiner uses the back of the hand rather than the palm.

When fluid can be obtained from a joint, the examination moves from inference to evidence, and the way the sample is handled decides how much it is worth. The fluid should be drawn before treatment begins wherever that is possible, and it should be examined for its cells, for crystals under a polarizing microscope, and for organisms grown in culture, because each question is answered differently and none replaces the others. In a well-resourced hospital the laboratory can also measure the inflammatory markers in the blood, the antibodies associated with the rheumatic diseases, and the level of uric acid, and each result must be read against the reference range of the laboratory that produced it, and such ranges differ between centers and editions, so they must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. The clinician should decide what each test would change before sending it, because a test that cannot alter the decision wastes time and may mislead. A radiograph shows the structure of the bone and the degree of damage, an ultrasound performed at the bedside can show fluid and inflammation and can guide the needle, and magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography answers specific questions about bone, joint, and soft tissue. A test ordered without a question is a result waiting to be misread.

In a clinic whose equipment is a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is carried out with the hands and the eyes, and the standard of thought is not lower because the tools are few. The torch makes the skin, the eyes, the mouth, the scalp, and the nails visible, and those are exactly the places where the extra-articular clues of a rheumatic disease are found. The thermometer turns the impression of fever into a figure that can be recorded and compared over time, though a normal temperature does not exclude an infection of a joint or of the spine, and the finding of a warm joint remains available to any examiner with a hand. A watch allows the pulse to be counted, and counting it rather than estimating it separates the careful clinician from the hurried one, since a rising pulse may be the earliest evidence that a joint infection is spreading. Urine can be inspected and tested where a dipstick is available, and blood or protein in the urine changes the assessment of a patient with joint pain more than any joint sign. What the clinic cannot do is examine the fluid, so the decision becomes whether to treat on suspicion and refer, or whether the patient can be watched, and the reason must be recorded together with a plan for review.

The decision about treatment belongs to the diagnosis even when it is made before the diagnosis is certain, and the reasoning can be stated without naming a medicine. Treatment is the right answer when a joint may be infected and the patient is systemically unwell, when the spine is threatened by compression, when a vasculitis is attacking the eye, the kidney, or the lung, and when an inflammatory arthritis is damaging joints that will not recover. Treatment is the wrong answer when the picture is fully explained by a mechanical or degenerative cause, when infection has not been excluded in a joint that is hot, and when treatment would remove the evidence the diagnosis depends on before the samples are taken. The finding that makes treatment wrong in a hot joint is fluid showing crystals with no organism in a patient who is stable, and the finding that makes it right is fluid showing organisms or a patient who is deteriorating while the result is awaited. In a patient with a possible infection of the spine, the finding that makes immediate action right is a new neurological deficit, and the finding that allows a few hours of investigation is pain without any neurological sign in a stable patient. Naming the finding rather than the drug transfers between settings, because the reasoning does not depend on which medicines happen to be available. Every threshold used in these decisions is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the figures that define danger differ between countries, editions, and services.

A constructed illustration may make these habits concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine an older woman who arrives with a knee that became hot and swollen overnight, a temperature of 38 degrees Celsius, and no history of gout, and whose joint is aspirated before anything is given. That temperature is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the figure that counts as fever varies with the site of measurement and with the patient. Now imagine an older man who stumbled on a step two days earlier, whose back pain is attributed to a strain, and in whom a tap over one vertebra reproduces the pain exactly; that tenderness is the finding which turns a strain into a suspected infection or fracture. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a person without a full assessment. What the two share is the move that matters, which is that the clinician asked the question the label did not.

To summarize, the musculoskeletal and rheumatic diseases are read by the pattern in which they involve the body: how many joints are affected, how quickly the problem arrived, and whether it behaves as inflammation or as wear, with the skin, the eyes, the urine, and the nerves examined for the clues that explain the patient. The dangerous causes are named at the bedside and not deferred, among them septic arthritis, an infection of the spine, a vasculitic emergency, a fracture dismissed as a strain, and a cancer in the bone, and each carries a finding that reveals it. Gout and septic arthritis are separated by the history and the signs rather than by certainty, and the definitive answer requires the fluid, examined for cells, crystals, and organisms. Lupus is recognized through its multisystem manifestations rather than a single criterion, and osteoporosis presents with its consequence rather than before it, so the fracture is the diagnosis. The same reasoning is carried into a hospital where immunology and imaging are available and into a clinic where a hand on the joint, a torch, a thermometer, and a watch must do the work. Every score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, or duration in this chapter is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and a patient whose joint is hot, whose spine is threatened, or whose vision has changed is a patient to be helped and referred rather than reassured.
