# Chapter 21 — Fever

Fever is the complaint that fills more clinics than any other, and it is the one whose meaning is most easily assumed. A raised temperature is not a disease and not even a single sign, but the visible end of a response the body mounts against something, and the reasoning begins when the clinician asks what that something is likely to be. The same figure on the thermometer may accompany a mild illness that resolves without any decision, or malaria, meningitis, or an infection of the bloodstream that kills quickly, and nothing in the figure itself separates them. Because the stakes are unequal, fever is organized not by its height but by its pattern in time, and that pattern divides the problem into the fever that is acute, the fever that is prolonged, and the fever that returns. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient.

A short list of dangers deserves to be named before the ordinary reasoning begins, because each is revealed by a finding that can be sought in the first minutes. The febrile patient with a stiff neck, a severe headache, and a dislike of light may have meningitis, and the finding that betrays it is the resistance felt when the neck is flexed, sometimes with a rash that does not fade when pressed. The patient with a fever and a new murmur, especially one with a damaged or prosthetic valve or a history of injecting drugs, may have infection of the lining of the heart, and a murmur heard for the first time turns a vague fever into a specific hunt. The patient whose immune defenses are suppressed may carry a severe infection with almost no signs, so that the fever itself, or even a change in behavior, is the only evidence available. The traveler returning from a region where hemorrhagic fever occurs, who has a fever with bleeding or sudden collapse, must be handled as a hazard to the patient and to the staff until that possibility is set aside.

The mechanism of fever explains why the pattern carries information. When organisms enter the body, the immune response releases substances that travel to the brain and raise the temperature set point, so the patient behaves as though cold and generates heat until the new target is reached. This is why a rising fever brings shivering and a pile of blankets, and why the same patient sweats and throws them off when the set point falls again. It is also why fever differs from the passive overheating of heat stroke, in which the set point is unchanged and the body cannot lose enough heat; that distinction matters, because cooling such a patient is a different decision from treating a regulated fever. The height of the fever is therefore a poor guide on its own, while the way it rises and what accompanies each peak carry far more.

Measurement is the first place where the reasoning can go wrong, because a temperature has no meaning without the method used to obtain it. A reading from the mouth, the armpit, the ear canal, the rectum, or the skin differs from the others, and a clinician who compares a reading from one route with a threshold written for another will misjudge the patient. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius is often taught as the line above which fever is said to be present, but that figure is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the accepted threshold shifts with the site of measurement, the age of the patient, and the edition of the reference being used. The ordinary thermometer, shaken down and left in place long enough, remains among the most useful instruments in medicine, and in a clinic with nothing else it turns a vague impression of illness into a figure that can be repeated.

Repeated measurement is worth more than any single reading, and it is the cheapest investigation available anywhere. A fever falling over several observations tells a different story from one that is climbing, even when both readings lie the same distance above the accepted line. The timing of the peaks matters, because a fever that spikes at a regular interval suggests a cause that releases its signal in waves, while a fever that never settles suggests something continuous. A temperature that falls after a cooling measure has proved nothing about the cause, and a clinician who mistakes that fall for improvement may delay a decision that should have been made hours earlier. What the repeated reading really measures is the trajectory of the illness, and the trajectory often separates a patient who can be watched from one who must be treated now.

The classical patterns are taught as continuous, intermittent, and relapsing, and each is a clue rather than a diagnosis. A continuous fever stays above the line with little variation, an intermittent fever falls to normal between peaks, and a relapsing fever disappears for a stretch of days and then returns. A rigor, which is a shaking chill with chattering teeth, often marks the moment when organisms enter the bloodstream, and the patient can often give the hour of its arrival. A fever with a pulse slower than the temperature would suggest is a pattern traditionally linked to certain infections, among them typhoid, and it is noticed only when pulse and temperature are recorded together and compared. None of these patterns proves a cause, but each narrows the field and tells the clinician which question to ask next.

The source hunt begins with the history, and it begins before the patient is touched. The clinician asks how the fever started, whether it came suddenly or gradually, whether it is continuous or comes in episodes, and what accompanies each episode, using the Oldcarts framework to keep the account complete. The questions then turn to exposure: where the patient lives and has traveled, what animals are kept at home, what water is drunk, what food has been eaten away from home, whether there have been injections, dental work, surgery, or a device placed in the body, and whether there is any risk of a sexually transmitted infection. Occupation, season, similar illness in the household, and the medicines the patient already takes belong in the same conversation, because each changes the short list of likely causes. A clinician who asks these questions systematically finds the source in the history more often than in any test.

The examination of the febrile patient is a search for a focus, and it is performed in a fixed order so that nothing is skipped. The clinician begins with general appearance, hydration, the color of the skin, and the work of breathing, then examines the skin from scalp to sole for a rash, a puncture mark, an ulcer, or a bite. The throat, ears, nose, mouth, and teeth are inspected, because an abscess in a tooth or a hidden sinus infection can sustain a fever for a long time. The chest is examined for crackles and dullness, the heart for a murmur, the abdomen for tenderness, an enlarged organ, or a mass, and the joints for swelling and warmth. The sign that accompanies the fever, whether jaundice, bleeding, enlarged lymph nodes, or joint pain, tells the clinician where to look next. The examination is not complete until the back, the perineum, the pressure areas, and every line or tube in the body have been looked at directly, since these are the places where infection hides in a patient who cannot move or cannot speak.

Meningitis must be considered in every febrile patient with a headache, and the neck is where the search begins. The examiner asks the patient to touch the chin to the chest and watches for resistance, then looks for the other signs: a severe general headache, dislike of bright light, vomiting, and a level of consciousness that is not quite right. A rash that does not disappear when a clear glass is pressed against it is a finding of great importance, because it may mean bleeding into the skin from an infection in the bloodstream, and that patient needs treatment and senior help at once. The Glasgow coma scale is a way of recording the level of consciousness, and the reasoning it formalizes is the response to voice, to pain, and to command; its exact contents and the figure at which it should cause alarm must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the version in use varies between services and editions. In an older patient, a person with diabetes, or a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, the neck may be supple and the headache dismissed as nothing.

Infection of the lining of the heart is the second danger that hides inside a fever with no obvious source. It is most likely in a patient with a damaged or prosthetic valve, a pacemaker or another device, a history of injecting drugs, or a recent procedure on the teeth or the gut, and the fever may be low and persistent rather than dramatic. The finding that reveals it is a murmur that is new or that has changed, and an examiner who hears one in a febrile patient has found the question to answer. The other clues are small and easily missed: tiny bleeding spots under the nails or in the eye, tender red nodules on the fingers or toes, and an enlarged spleen. The decision that follows is whether to draw blood cultures before any treatment begins, because cultures taken afterwards are far less likely to name the organism. The finding that would make treatment the wrong answer is a clear alternative focus that fully explains the fever.

The patient whose immune defenses are suppressed is the one in whom fever reasoning changes most sharply, because the usual signs may simply not appear. A low white cell count, a transplant, a cancer treatment, a long course of steroids, advanced infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, or a poorly controlled cancer of the blood can all blunt the inflammatory response, so an infection that would inflame an ordinary person produces nothing to see. The same is true in a milder way of the frail older patient, in whom infection may present as confusion, a fall, or a refusal to eat with little or no rise in temperature. In this group the fever may be the only abnormal finding, and sometimes even the fever is absent. Here the threshold for seeking help and for starting treatment is lower rather than higher, and treating on the clinical picture before any result returns is often the right decision. The finding that would make that decision wrong is a clear non-infectious explanation, such as a reaction to a medicine or a new clot in a vein, and even then the patient must be watched closely.

Malaria must be considered first in any febrile patient who has been in a region where it is transmitted, and it is named here because it can kill within days. The fever is often described as coming in waves, with a shaking chill at the start and heavy sweating at the end of the episode, and the patient may be anemic, jaundiced, or have an enlarged spleen. In severe disease the patient may become drowsy, breathe rapidly, or pass very little urine. The finding that reveals it is the parasite seen under the microscope in a stained blood film, or a rapid diagnostic test that detects its product in the blood, and neither can be replaced by the shape of the fever alone. Where such a test exists it should be performed at once, and where it does not, the travel history and the clinical picture together must carry the decision, because a patient who looks well at the first visit can deteriorate quickly. The interval over which the disease declares itself varies with the species and with the patient, so weigh that timing against the current guideline and the local protocol, where the figure may be stated differently again.

Typhoid fever begins quietly, with a fever that climbs in steps over the first days, a headache, a poor appetite, and abdominal discomfort, and the pulse may be slower than the temperature would lead the clinician to expect. Constipation is common early and diarrhea later, the tongue is coated, and an enlarged spleen may be felt; the finding that confirms it is the organism grown from blood or bone marrow, and the blood culture is most rewarding early in the illness. Dengue fever arrives suddenly with severe pain behind the eyes, pain in the muscles and bones, a rash, and a falling platelet count, and the patient who seems to improve while the platelet count falls is the one who may progress to bleeding and shock. The danger in dengue is usually not the fever itself but the leakage of fluid from the circulation that follows it, so the clinician watches the pressure, the pulse, and the urine rather than the temperature alone.

A traveler returning with fever and bleeding raises the possibility of a viral hemorrhagic fever, and that possibility changes how the patient is handled before any diagnosis is made. Bleeding from the gums, the nose, the gut, or under the skin, together with fever, a falling pressure, and a recent journey to an affected area, is the pattern that should prompt isolation, protective clothing, and an urgent call for advice rather than a routine examination. The reasoning is not exotic; it is the same search for a focus, conducted with the awareness that the patient may be infectious to those who care for them. Other tropical causes belong in the same list, among them leptospirosis, which follows contact with water contaminated by animal urine, the rickettsial infections spread by ticks and mites, brucellosis from unpasteurized milk or from animals, and schistosomiasis from fresh water.

In a hospital the febrile patient is also assessed for sepsis, which is organ failure caused by the response to infection rather than the infection itself. The signs are a rapid pulse, a breathing rate that has climbed, a pressure that has fallen, confusion or drowsiness, cool or mottled skin, and a shrinking passage of urine. A systolic pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury is often quoted as the point at which shock must be assumed, but that figure is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the pressure that matters depends on the patient's usual value and on age and pregnancy. The most sensitive sign is often the one least carefully recorded, which is the direction of the breathing rate and the pulse over successive observations. A patient who fits this picture needs treatment and senior help now, and the fever itself is the least important part of the assessment.

Tuberculosis produces a fever that is prolonged, usually low, and often worse in the evening, accompanied by night sweats, weight loss, and a cough that has lasted for a long time. The chest radiograph may show changes at the top of the lung, but it can be normal in the early stage, in the patient with HIV infection, and in disease that lies outside the lung. The finding that reveals it is the organism identified in sputum, in fluid, or in a sample of tissue, and where that is not possible the clinical picture, the radiograph, and the response to treatment may have to carry the decision together. Tuberculosis should be considered in every patient with a fever that will not settle, whatever the first suspicion was, because it imitates many other illnesses and is easily missed. The treatment decision belongs to the program that manages the disease, and the finding that would make treatment the wrong answer is a different confirmed cause that fully explains the picture.

An abscess can sustain a fever for a long time without declaring itself, and it is found by examining the places that are easy to forget. A collection of pus in the abdomen, beside the kidney, deep in the pelvis, in the liver, in the spine, or around a tooth may produce nothing more than fever, a poor appetite, and a vague sense of illness. An examiner who does not look at the teeth, press on the spine, examine the back and the perineum, and perform a rectal or pelvic examination when it is indicated may never find the source. In a patient who has had surgery or a device placed recently, every wound and every line must be inspected directly, and the skin around them watched for redness and discharge.

Prolonged fever is a different problem from acute fever, and it is defined by an interval rather than by a cause. A fever that persists for more than about 3 weeks, by which time ordinary self-limiting infections have resolved, belongs in a category of its own, and that interval must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the boundary is drawn differently in different services and editions. In the well-resourced setting the causes are grouped into infection, cancer, disease of the connective tissue, and a remainder that includes medicines, hormone disorders, and fever that is produced or invented by the patient. The history must be taken again rather than continued, because the details that matter now are the ones that seemed unimportant at the first visit. Repeated examination is worth more than one complete examination, since a sign absent today may be unmistakable at the next review.

Among the prolonged causes, a cancer of the blood or the lymph nodes is among the most important, and it announces itself through enlarged lymph nodes, an enlarged spleen, night sweats, weight loss, and an itching the patient may not think to mention. A tumor of the kidney or the liver can produce fever as its first sign, and the finding is an enlarged or irregular organ. The connective tissue diseases produce fever with joint pain and swelling, a rash, mouth ulcers, hair loss, or a change in the color of the fingers in the cold, and they are usually accompanied by other symptoms the patient has learned to accept as normal. A medicine is a cause easily overlooked, because the fever may begin well after the drug was started and may come with no rash at all; the decision to withdraw the suspected medicine as a diagnostic test belongs to the clinician who knows the patient, and the finding that supports it is a fever that settles once the medicine is stopped.

Recurrent fever is the third shape, and it asks a different question: what makes the fever return. The clinician looks for a pattern in the calendar of the episodes, for a common trigger such as cold, injury, or a particular food, and for anything that changes between attacks. Some causes release organisms into the blood in cycles, others depend on a periodic failure of the immune system, and still others are inherited conditions in which inflammation flares without any infection at all. In a patient whose fever returns at intervals, the examination between attacks may be completely normal, and the most useful act is to document each episode carefully so that the pattern becomes visible over time. The clinician should also consider that the fever may not be what it appears to be, and should record a temperature measured by a reliable observer rather than accept a reported reading.

In a hospital the hunt uses the laboratory and the scanner, but the reasoning that directs them is unchanged. Blood cultures drawn before treatment begins are the single most useful test in a patient with a suspected infection of the bloodstream, and the timing of the draw matters more than the number of bottles. A sample of urine, a sample of sputum, and a sample of fluid from a collection each answer a particular question, and the clinician should decide what a positive result would mean before the sample is sent. Imaging by ultrasound, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging can find a collection of pus, an enlarged organ, or an infection in a place that cannot be reached by hand, and the clinician who has formed a hypothesis already knows which image to ask for. A lumbar puncture is the decisive step in the patient with signs of meningitis, and it is performed once the questions of safety have been answered. A test is worth ordering only when its result would change what is done next, and a clinician who cannot say what either result would mean should not send it.

Where there is no laboratory, the same hunt is conducted with a thermometer, a torch, and clinical judgment, and it is not a lesser form of reasoning. The history is taken with more care rather than less, because it is the one instrument that never runs out, and the examination is repeated at intervals the patient's condition allows. The torch makes the throat, the ears, the skin, the eyes, and the perineum visible, and the thermometer turns the impression of fever into something that can be recorded and compared. A watch allows the pulse and the breathing to be counted rather than guessed. The decision that follows is not usually a diagnosis but a plan: whether the patient can be watched, whether treatment should begin on the clinical picture, and whether the journey to a hospital must start now. A normal examination in a patient who worries the clinician is a reason to look again rather than a reason to relax, and a clinic that cannot test must rely on the honesty of its own uncertainty.

The decision about treatment is part of diagnosis, and in fever it is often made before the cause is known. Treatment is the right answer when the patient looks seriously ill, when the signs of organ failure are present, when the immune defenses are suppressed, or when a treatable cause such as malaria or meningitis is strongly suspected and waiting would cost more than acting. Treatment is the wrong answer when a clear non-infectious cause explains the fever, when the patient is stable enough to allow the diagnosis to be completed first without risk, and when treating before the samples are taken would destroy the very evidence the diagnosis depends on. Naming the finding that would justify or forbid treatment is more useful than naming a medicine, because the reasoning transfers between settings even when the available medicines do not.

A constructed illustration may make these habits concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a traveler returning from a region where malaria is transmitted, who has a fever that comes in waves with a rigor, an enlarged spleen, and a headache, and whose blood is examined at once because the travel history was asked in the first minutes. Now imagine an older patient at home who has stopped eating and has become confused, whose temperature is barely raised, and in whom the search for a focus finds a red, warm area over the hip. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment. What the two share is the move that matters: the clinician asked the question the first number did not, and the answer changed the plan.

Fever is the complaint that most often brings a patient back, and a clinician who sends a febrile patient home must say clearly what should bring that patient back sooner. The patient or the family should be told which signs matter: a fever that will not settle, a new confusion, a stiff neck, a rash that does not fade when pressed, difficulty breathing, a reduced passage of urine, or a worsening the patient can see. A follow-up visit arranged in advance is worth more than a promise to return if things worsen, because many patients cannot judge that for themselves. The record of a febrile illness should show the thinking and not only the numbers, including the route and time of each temperature, the pulse and breathing beside it, what the patient said about travel and exposure, and what was considered and set aside.

To summarize, fever is a response rather than a disease, and the reasoning divides it into the acute, the prolonged, and the recurrent, each asking a different question of the history and the examination. The dangers are named at the bedside and not deferred: the febrile patient with a stiff neck, the patient with a new murmur, the patient whose immune defenses are suppressed and whose signs are silent, and the traveler with fever and bleeding. The hunt for a source runs through the history of travel, exposure, food, water, animals, and medicines, and then through an examination that looks deliberately at the skin, the mouth, the heart, the abdomen, the spine, and the hidden sites. Repeated measurement and repeated examination are worth more than any single reading, and the trajectory of the illness is usually what decides. Every figure in this chapter, whether a temperature, a pressure, an interval, or a score, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. This is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A febrile patient who looks unwell is treated and helped first, and the exact cause is settled afterwards.
