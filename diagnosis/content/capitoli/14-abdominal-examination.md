# Chapter 14 — Abdominal Examination

The abdomen is the part of the body where the ordinary and the lethal wear the same face, so it must be examined in a fixed sequence rather than with a hopeful hand. Indigestion and a strained muscle are common, and so are a ruptured aortic aneurysm, a perforated viscus, a strangulated hernia, and an ectopic pregnancy, and each of them can begin as a vague ache. A systematic examination makes the dangerous findings hard to miss and the common ones easy to set aside once they have been sought. This is educational material and cannot replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. What follows is reasoning to be practised under supervision, not a set of instructions to be applied alone.

Four catastrophes deserve naming at the very beginning, each with the finding that would betray it. A ruptured aortic aneurysm appears as a pulsatile, expansile upper abdominal mass, often with pain that has traveled to the back or flank, a rapid pulse, and a falling pressure. A perforated viscus produces a rigid, board-like abdomen that hurts on any movement, with bowel sounds that have gone quiet. A strangulated hernia is an irreducible, tender swelling at one of the natural orifices, and the bowel inside it is already losing its blood supply. An ectopic pregnancy presents with lower abdominal pain, a missed or unusual period, and the signs of peritoneal irritation or internal bleeding in a woman of childbearing age. The fifth danger is the abdomen that is tender but not yet rigid, because early peritonitis and mesenteric ischemia can hide behind an examination that looks almost unremarkable.

The reasoning is identical in a hospital full of machines and in a clinic whose whole equipment is a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, but the consequence of a finding differs enormously. Where a laboratory, a bedside ultrasound, computed tomography, and a surgeon are within reach, the examination decides which of those to summon and how fast. Where none of them exists, it decides whether the patient can be watched safely or must begin a journey that may take hours. The clinician who examines well with his hands will examine better when imaging exists, because he already knows which question the image must answer.

Before the hands touch the abdomen, several things must be settled, and they are part of the examination rather than a courtesy around it. The patient lies flat with the head on a single pillow, the arms at the sides rather than folded behind the head, and the knees slightly drawn up to relax the wall. The abdomen is exposed from the lower ribs to the groin, because examining through clothing hides exactly the scars, hernias, and swellings that matter. Hands and stethoscope are warmed, the space is private, and the patient is told what will happen and asked for permission. For the rectal and pelvic parts, consent is sought separately, a chaperone is offered, and a refusal is respected without pressure and recorded without judgement.

The classical order is inspection, then auscultation, then percussion, and finally palpation, and the order is not arbitrary. Listening precedes touching because palpation and percussion stir the bowel and change the sounds the stethoscope is meant to record. Palpation comes last with the painful region saved for the end, because a wall already tightened by pain hides everything beneath it. Within palpation the order runs from light to deep and from the least tender region towards the most tender. The examiner watches the face throughout, because a grimace is a finding in its own right and often more honest than the patient's account.

Inspection begins as soon as the abdomen is uncovered and continues while the patient describes the pain. The examiner notes whether the shape is flat, hollowed, or distended, and whether the distension is uniform or local. Scars are named by position and direction, since a scar is surgical history the patient may have forgotten to mention. Dilated veins, bruising around the umbilicus or in the flanks, yellowing of the skin, and small spider-like vessels on the upper trunk all point to what may be happening inside. The examiner also looks for visible peristalsis, and for any bulge at the umbilicus, the groins, or the line of an old operation.

Distension is produced by a small number of things, and the examiner works through them silently. Gas, free fluid, fat in the wall, a growing fetus, a large mass, and a full bladder can each create the same rounded shape, and the rest of the examination separates them. A visible upper abdominal pulsation matters greatly, because a normal aorta transmits its impulse forward while an aneurysm expands in every direction. The examiner therefore views the pulsation from the side, where an expansile mass seems to push the wall outward in two directions at once. A mass that merely lifts with each beat usually lies in front of the aorta, while a mass that widens with each beat is the aorta itself.

Auscultation is performed with the diaphragm laid lightly on the skin, because pressing hard creates artefacts of its own. Normal bowel sounds are irregular soft clicks and gurgles, and their complete absence means something only after the examiner has listened in several regions long enough to be sure. High-pitched rushing sounds arriving in waves suggest bowel contracting hard against an obstruction, and a tinkling quality carries the same implication. A quiet abdomen may instead mean a bowel paralysed after surgery, an inflamed peritoneum, or a disturbed electrolyte, so the sounds are always read with the rest of the picture. The examiner also listens over the epigastrium for a bruit, over the liver and spleen for a friction rub, and at the stomach for a splashing sound that suggests an organ that cannot empty. A bruit may be heard over an entirely normal aorta, but a bruit with a pulsatile expansile mass and new back pain is a different and far more urgent matter.

A common teaching is to listen for a full minute in each region before declaring the abdomen silent, but that interval is a convention rather than a law. Confirm the duration your own teachers and current guideline recommend against the local protocol, because the recommended listening time varies between settings, examiners, and editions. What matters more than the exact interval is the discipline of listening in several places and recording that the examination was quiet rather than normal. A truly silent abdomen in a patient with pain and distension is serious, and it can never be inferred from a hurried listen through clothing.

Percussion is the bridge between listening and feeling, and it answers questions palpation alone cannot. Tapping produces a tympanic note over gas and a dull note over fluid, solid organ, or stool, and the pattern of notes maps what lies beneath. The examiner percusses lightly at first, then follows the change of note that marks the edge of the liver, the spleen, a mass, or a full bladder. Percussion is used deliberately over a painful region as well, because percussion tenderness is a reliable sign of peritoneal irritation and can be elicited when deep palpation is too distressing. The technique is gentle, the movement comes from the wrist, and the examiner watches the face for the flinch that shows the spot has been found.

Free fluid is detected by looking for shifting dullness, in which the dull note over the flank moves as the patient is rolled towards one side. The test relies on fluid settling with gravity while gas-filled bowel floats, and it becomes detectable only once the collection is large enough to change the note reliably. A figure of roughly 1 liter is often taught as the volume needed, but treat that as an illustration rather than a measurement you can trust, and confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the amount differs with the patient's build and the examiner's technique. When the collection is very large a fluid thrill may be felt by flicking one flank while the flat of the other hand rests opposite, with a hand in the midline to prevent a wave of fat being mistaken for fluid. Where these signs are equivocal, the everted umbilicus and the other signs of chronic liver disease carry as much weight as the note itself.

One percussion finding points towards perforation and deserves special attention. The normal liver is dull, and free gas under the diaphragm can press against it and reduce or abolish that dullness. The finding is famous but unreliable in both directions, because a length of normal gut can lie in front of the liver and imitate the change. Where no imaging exists, a rigid abdomen, quiet bowel sounds, and a lost area of liver dullness together are enough to force the decision rather than reassure the examiner.

Palpation is performed with a warm, flat hand, beginning lightly and examining the least painful region before the worst. The examiner asks the patient to breathe gently through the mouth and to say what he feels, because tenderness is data and its location is more useful than a general impression of tightness. Light palpation surveys the whole abdomen for a mass, an area of resistance, or an unexpected fullness. Deep palpation follows in the same order, and for the liver, spleen, and kidneys a bimanual technique presses one hand towards the other in time with breathing. Every finding is described by what the examiner actually did and felt, because a note that says the abdomen was soft and non-tender means little without saying where the hands went.

Guarding is contraction of the abdominal wall, and its meaning depends on whether it is voluntary or involuntary. Voluntary guarding is the patient deliberately bracing against expected pain, and it relaxes when he is distracted or asked to breathe slowly. Involuntary guarding is generated by the spinal reflex that fires when the peritoneum is inflamed, and it does not disappear when the patient is reassured. Rigidity is the extreme of involuntary guarding, in which the wall becomes board-like and barely moves with respiration. The examiner therefore never judges on the strength of guarding alone, but asks whether the tightening can be abolished by the patient's own effort and whether it is confined to one region.

Rebound tenderness is the pain felt when the examining hand is removed after pressure, and it reflects sudden movement of an inflamed peritoneum rather than the pressure itself. It is elicited deliberately and gently, because the maneuver hurts and a rough test produces the same complaint in a patient who does not have peritonitis. Peritonism is the whole pattern: involuntary guarding, tenderness on percussion, pain on movement or coughing, quiet bowel sounds, and often a rising pulse with a falling pressure. The examiner thinks in terms of that pattern rather than one positive sign, because each sign is imperfect while the pattern is far harder to produce falsely. A patient with peritonism needs the surgical decision now, whatever the underlying cause turns out to be.

There is a particular patient who punishes the clinician, and he is the one whose abdomen is tender but not yet rigid. In the first hours of a perforated ulcer, in the early phase of a strangulated hernia, in mesenteric ischemia, and in a leaking aneurysm, the classical signs may be absent or seem mild. The pain of mesenteric ischemia is classically severe and out of proportion to a soft abdomen, and that mismatch should alarm the examiner rather than reassure him. The same holds for the older patient, the patient whose immune response is suppressed by illness or treatment, and the patient with poorly controlled diabetes. In these people the decision rests on the trajectory and the repeated examination rather than the first set of findings, and the threshold for asking for help is lower rather than higher.

Organomegaly is assessed by the character of the organ rather than by the number of fingerbreadths below the rib, and this is one of the most useful habits in the examination. A liver edge may be felt in many healthy people, especially when a high diaphragm or a distended lung has pushed it down, so its presence alone proves little. What matters is the span from the upper border percussed in the chest to the lower edge, the consistency of that edge, whether the surface is smooth or irregular, whether the organ is tender, and whether it moves with respiration. A span of roughly 12 to 15 centimeters is often quoted as normal in an adult, but treat that range as an example of the reasoning and confirm the figure against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the span changes with body size, technique, and the reference the examiner was taught from. A hard, nodular, non-tender liver means something different from a smooth, tender, soft one, even when both edges lie at the same level. The examiner therefore describes what he felt rather than how far he felt it.

The spleen is not normally palpable, and when felt it is usually the tip of an organ that has enlarged enough to travel below the left rib margin. It is identified by the way it moves downwards with inspiration towards the right lower abdomen, by a notch on its inner border, by dullness to percussion, and by the fact that the examiner cannot reach above it. The kidney behaves differently, because it moves with respiration, can be held between the two hands, is often reachable from the flank, and the examiner can usually get above it. Both kidneys are examined bimanually with one hand lifting from behind, and a kidney that is enlarged, tender, or irregular suggests obstruction, infection, or a mass. A very large spleen that crosses the midline narrows the differential towards a small set of causes.

The bladder is examined as a suprapubic dullness that extends upward when full and disappears after the patient passes urine, so a bladder still palpable after voiding suggests retention. Any mass is described by its site, its size in the examiner's own units, its shape, surface, consistency, mobility, whether it moves with respiration, and whether it is tender. A mass that pulsates, one that is hard and irregular, and one that is fixed and painless each carry their own short list of explanations. A swelling that disappears when the abdominal wall is tensed probably lies in the wall rather than within the abdomen, and describing a mass by how it behaves is more useful to the next clinician than naming the organ it might belong to.

The aorta is palpated deeply in the upper abdomen, with the examiner's hand placed along the vessel rather than across it, and the width of the pulsation is noted. In a thin patient the normal aorta may be felt to pulsate, and that alone is not an aneurysm. The finding that matters is a pulsatile mass that is expansile, that widens in two directions as the hands are placed on either side, and that is wider than about 5 centimeters across. That figure is an illustration and not a rule, so confirm the current guideline and the local protocol for your setting, because the width that triggers a decision differs between countries and editions and is influenced by the patient's size. Aortic palpation must be gentle, and the examiner should not chase the finding at the expense of everything else. A bruit heard over such a mass, or an absent femoral pulse on one side, makes the situation more urgent still, because an aneurysm in the abdomen and a blockage in the legs often travel together.

Hernias are sought at every orifice, and the search is not complete until all of them have been examined. The umbilicus, both groins, the femoral canal below the groin crease, the site of every previous operation, and the rare sites along the abdominal wall each deserve a deliberate look and feel. The patient is examined flat and standing, and asked to cough while the examiner's finger rests at the inguinal ring, because a hernia invisible at rest may appear with a rise in abdominal pressure. An umbilical hernia in an adult with a distended abdomen may be the outward sign of fluid within, and should lead back to the liver and spleen rather than being dismissed as cosmetic. A hernia with colicky pain, vomiting, and distension is an emergency until proven otherwise, and it must be looked for before the abdomen is dismissed as a medical problem.

A strangulated hernia has trapped its contents, and the bowel inside loses its blood supply while the examination is still taking place. The signs are an irreducible tender swelling, skin that may be red or warm over it, and the features of obstruction: colicky pain, vomiting, distension, and later the passage of nothing at all. The absence of a cough impulse over the swelling is ominous, because it suggests the neck of the sac is tightly closed. The patient may not volunteer the swelling, and an obese or large abdomen may conceal it from everyone. When strangulation is suspected the decision is surgical and immediate, and waiting for a clearer picture is unlikely to improve the outcome.

Bowel obstruction produces a pattern worth assembling before any image is seen. The patient has colicky pain in waves, vomiting, distension, and a failure to pass stool or gas, and the bowel sounds may be high-pitched and rushing at first and quiet later. A previous operation raises the probability of adhesions, a palpable mass raises the possibility of a tumor, and a hernia at any orifice raises the possibility of strangulation. Fever, tenderness, or a rising pulse in a patient with obstruction suggests the bowel is already compromised and the problem has become a surgical emergency. The record should say clearly which features are present and which are absent, because the next clinician will decide differently depending on the answer.

The rectal examination is quick, informative, and frequently omitted for reasons that have more to do with the examiner than the patient. It is performed with the patient on his left side and the knees drawn up after the reason is explained and consent obtained, or in any other position he finds acceptable. The examiner inspects the skin around the anus for cracks, openings, skin tags, prolapse, and external swellings, then passes a lubricated gloved finger gently through the anal canal. The finger assesses the tone of the sphincter, the contents of the rectum, the presence of blood, and the surface of the walls, and it reaches further than most beginners expect. In the male the prostate is felt through the front wall and described by size, consistency, central groove, and any nodule, while in the female a bimanual examination can be completed with the other hand on the abdomen. It must not be omitted when there is bleeding from the gut, when a pelvic or rectal mass is suspected, when weight loss or anemia is unexplained, or when a collection of pus is suspected after surgery, and it must not be performed without consent, privacy, and a chaperone.

The pelvic examination answers questions the rectal examination cannot, and it deserves the same frankness in the description. With consent and a chaperone the examiner inspects the external genitalia, passes a speculum to see the cervix and vaginal walls, and then performs a bimanual examination with one or two fingers in the vagina and the other hand on the lower abdomen. The size, position, and mobility of the uterus are noted, the structures beside it are felt for a mass or tenderness, and the cervix is moved gently to see whether that reproduces the pain, which is a sign of pelvic infection. Blood in the vagina, a discharging cervix, an ulcer, or a mass all change the differential immediately. The examination is limited to what the clinical question requires, and the patient's comfort and dignity are part of the technique rather than a concession to it.

Ectopic pregnancy is the diagnosis that makes the pelvic examination urgent, and it is named here because the cost of missing it is measured in minutes. The patient is a woman of childbearing age with lower abdominal pain, often on one side, a missed or unusual period, and sometimes slight vaginal bleeding. The examination may reveal tenderness on one side of the pelvis, a tender mass beside the uterus, and pain when the cervix is moved, while a ruptured ectopic adds the whole picture of peritoneal irritation with a rising pulse and a falling pressure. A pregnancy test changes the reasoning more than any other single step, and where no test exists the menstrual history and the signs of early pregnancy must carry the weight. The examiner who considers the possibility in every woman of childbearing age with abdominal pain will find it, and the one who assumes a gastrointestinal cause may not.

A patient who is shocked and whose abdomen is the source requires a different pace from one who is comfortable. A rapid pulse, a pressure below 90 millimeters of mercury, cool clammy skin, and a pulsatile abdominal mass together describe a leaking aneurysm, and they should move the clinician to summon help rather than to finish a leisurely examination. That pressure is an illustration of the reasoning, so confirm the figure your service treats as shock against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the value is changed by age, pregnancy, longstanding high blood pressure, and the definition being used. A falling urine output, which for an adult may be described as less than half a milliliter for each kilogram of body weight in an hour, is another sign that the circulation is failing, and that figure must likewise be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol because it is adjusted for age and illness. The abdomen may be soft in such a patient, and a soft abdomen is not a reason to wait.

When imaging is available the abdominal examination becomes the question the image is asked to answer. A bedside ultrasound can show free fluid, a distended gallbladder, a dilated aorta, a pregnancy inside the uterus or outside it, and an obstructed kidney, and it can be performed while the patient is still being examined. Computed tomography, or CT, gives a more complete picture of perforation, obstruction, inflammation of the pancreas, and masses, while magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, answers particular questions in the liver and the biliary tree. The examiner decides what he expects the image to show before it is performed, so an unexpected result is recognized as a surprise rather than accepted without thought. Imaging extends the examination but does not replace the reasoning that chose it, and a normal scan in a clearly unwell patient is a reason to think again rather than a reason to relax.

Where imaging will not come, the examination must be performed more thoroughly and repeated more often, because nothing else will correct a first impression. A torch allows the skin around the anus and the hernial orifices to be examined properly, and a thermometer turns a vague sense of illness into a number that can be followed. A stethoscope brings the bowel sounds, bruits, and rubs, and a watch allows the pulse and respiratory rate to be counted rather than estimated. The urine can be inspected for color and tested if a dipstick exists, and weight, intake, and output can be recorded by hand. The decision that follows is not a diagnosis but a plan: whether the patient can be watched safely, whether treatment can begin on the clinical picture, and whether the journey must start now. The examination also has limits that should be stated honestly, since it cannot exclude an early obstruction, a small perforation, or a leaking aneurysm with confidence, and a normal examination in a worrying story is a reason to repeat it rather than a licence to discharge.

A constructed illustration may help to fix the sequence. Imagine an older man with some days of vague mid-abdominal discomfort and a new ache in the back, no fever, a soft abdomen, and the only striking findings of a rapid pulse and a pressure lower than his usual. The examination is not complete until the aorta has been palpated deliberately, and if it is expansile and unusually wide the working diagnosis changes from indigestion to a leaking aneurysm and the plan changes with it. Now imagine a young woman with lower abdominal pain and a fainting episode, in whom the examiner asks about the last period before deciding the pain is colic, and receives the answer that redirects everything. Neither patient is real and neither should be read as a portrait, for they are constructed only to show how one further question can reverse the order of a differential.

To summarize, the abdominal examination proceeds from inspection to auscultation to percussion and finally to palpation, with the painful region examined last and the hernial orifices and the rectum sought deliberately rather than hopefully. It reads guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness, and peritonism as a pattern of peritoneal irritation rather than as isolated signs, and it judges an organ by its character rather than by the number of fingerbreadths below the rib. The dangers are named at the bedside and not deferred, among them a ruptured aortic aneurysm, a perforated viscus, a strangulated hernia, an ectopic pregnancy, and the abdomen that is tender without yet being rigid, each with the finding that would reveal it and each demanding a decision. Every number in this chapter, whether a width, a span, a pressure, a rate, a temperature, a volume, or an interval, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, between editions, and between laboratories. This recording is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service. A tender abdomen that does not yet look peritonitic is a patient to be re-examined and discussed, never a patient to be reassured and sent away.
