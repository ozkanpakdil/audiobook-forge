# Chapter 20 — Mental State Examination

The mental state examination is the structured observation of a mind, done with the same discipline as the examination of a chest. It asks how the patient appears, speaks, feels, thinks, perceives, remembers, understands, and judges. Its purpose is not to name a psychiatric illness but to produce a description precise enough for another clinician to picture the patient. That description then joins the physical findings as evidence, to be weighed rather than obeyed.

The examination must be separated from the diagnosis. A mental state examination produces a description, while a diagnosis is a judgment assembled from that description, the history, time, and what physical illness has been excluded. The description can be complete while the diagnosis remains uncertain, and that is not a failure. It is why the examination is repeated rather than performed once.

The most important warning here concerns the body. Many physical illnesses announce themselves through behavior, speech, mood, or confusion, and the lethal causes that do so include a low blood glucose, a low level of oxygen, infection in the blood, bleeding or swelling inside the head, inflammation of the brain or its coverings, and withdrawal from alcohol or sedatives. Each can look exactly like a primary psychiatric disorder to a clinician who has decided the problem is mental, and each kills if missed. The revealing finding is not psychological but a fluctuation in attention, a fever, a stiff neck, an abnormal pupil, weakness on one side, or a measurement nobody took. An acute examination therefore begins with safety, the blood glucose, the temperature, and the physical cause still unconsidered.

Setting shapes what can be observed and should be chosen rather than inherited. In a well-resourced hospital the interview happens in a quiet room with the door positioned so both people can leave, and a colleague knows it is taking place. In a busy clinic it may happen on a corridor chair, and the examiner accepts a rougher description. What matters is that the patient receives as much privacy, calm, and unhurried attention as the place allows. A patient frightened by his surroundings will look agitated, and an examiner who forgets the surroundings will call that agitation a symptom.

Appearance and behavior are read before a question is asked. The examiner notices clothing, grooming, weight, posture, tremor, sweating, scars, and injury, and whether the patient is caring for himself as usual. This is also the moment for the physical signs that hide behind a psychiatric label, such as the thinness of chronic illness, the yellow of jaundice, the smell of alcohol or acetone, the small pupils of an opiate, an enlarged thyroid, or the tremor of withdrawal. Behavior includes eye contact, the distance kept, whether the patient sits still or paces, and whether he responds. A patient who will not stay still is giving information rather than being difficult.

Agitation is a symptom rather than a diagnosis and deserves its own place. It may be fear, pain, a low level of oxygen, a full bladder, constipation, hunger, noise, fever, a drug effect, or withdrawal, and it may also be the restlessness of a mind that has lost its grip. The dangerous reading treats it as purely psychological and leaves the physical need unmet, so a patient in pain or retention of urine becomes violent while the cause goes untreated. The finding that redirects the reasoning is often mundane, whether a distended bladder, a dry mouth with a racing pulse, a wound, or when the patient last passed urine or drank. With an agitated person, ask first what the body needs, and only then what the mind is doing.

Speech carries the pace and structure of thought. The examiner listens to rate, volume, rhythm, melody, and amount, and notices whether speech is pressured, slowed, mute, or hard to interrupt. A patient who speaks in a rush, follows every tangent, and cannot be stopped differs from one who answers after a long pause. It is also necessary to separate a disorder of language from a disorder of thought, because damage to the language areas makes a patient sound confused while his thinking remains intact. Wrong words, substituted sounds, and sentences that lose their grammar point towards the brain rather than the mind.

Mood is what the patient says he feels, and affect is what the examiner sees. The two may agree, but they may also diverge in a way that is itself the finding. A patient who describes deep despair while smiling is showing a mismatch that must be recorded rather than resolved. The examiner observes the range of expression, whether it reacts to what is said, whether it is flat or labile, and whether the feeling fits the thought. A narrowed, unreactive mood persisting across days differs from a mood that lifts when the conversation turns.

Mood should also be asked about directly, because it is not reliably volunteered. Short questions about low mood, loss of interest, sleep, appetite, energy, and guilt uncover a depression the patient assumed was not medical. The same questioning should cover anxiety and panic, since a racing heart and shortness of breath may describe fear rather than cardiac disease. An answer to a direct question is safer than an inference from the face.

Thought form describes how thought is organized rather than what it is about. In ordinary thought one idea follows another in a way a listener can follow, even when the subject changes. In flight of ideas the connections are real but too many and too fast, and the speech jumps because everything reminds the speaker of something else. In loosening of associations the connections disappear, so sentences no longer hold together and the path cannot be reconstructed. Circumstantiality is loss of the goal with the grammar kept, and perseveration is repetition of an answer after the question has changed. Describing form often separates mania, psychosis, and organic brain disease before content is known.

Thought content is what the patient believes, and the examiner records it faithfully rather than arguing with it. A delusion is a fixed belief held with conviction, not shared by the patient's culture, and not yielding to ordinary evidence or reason. Delusions may be persecutory, grandiose, somatic, religious, or nihilistic, and the content matters less than the certainty and the way it organizes behavior. An overvalued idea is understandable in origin but has come to dominate the patient's life out of proportion to its truth. Obsessions are intrusive thoughts the patient recognizes as his own and finds distressing, which is the opposite of a delusion in the way that matters for treatment. Ask what the patient intends to do about a belief, because a delusion held quietly differs from one that has produced a plan.

The most dangerous content is content that issues an instruction. A patient may hear a voice that comments on his actions, argues with other voices, or tells him to do something, and a commanding voice is the finding that most reliably predicts harm. The examiner must ask directly and in plain words, and must ask whether the patient believes he should obey and whether he has already tried. A commanding hallucination with an intention to act or a plan turns a diagnostic exercise into an emergency. This is where the clinician stops gathering and starts protecting, and where help from colleagues, family, and the receiving service is summoned rather than postponed. A patient who reports such a voice should never be left alone while the decision is made.

Perception describes what the senses deliver without an external object. A hallucination is a perception without a stimulus, and an illusion is a real stimulus misread, such as a coat on a chair that looks like a person in the half-light. Hallucinations may be auditory, visual, tactile, olfactory, or gustatory, and the kind is a clue to the illness. Auditory hallucinations in clear consciousness suggest a primary psychotic illness, while visual hallucinations in a confused, drowsy patient suggest a physical cause, a drug, or withdrawal. Tactile hallucinations of insects under the skin point towards stimulant drugs, alcohol withdrawal, or another physical illness rather than towards schizophrenia. Brief, unpleasant olfactory hallucinations can arise from a seizure in the temporal lobe and deserve the same seriousness as weakness on one side.

Cognition is the part of the examination that most often changes a diagnosis and the part most often skipped. The examiner assesses consciousness, orientation to time, place, and person, attention, memory, language, and the ability to plan. Attention is tested by counting backwards, reciting the months in reverse, or naming the days of the week backwards, and the examiner watches for the wandering that marks it impaired. Memory is tested with 3 words recalled later in the interview, and with questions about recent events, recent meals, and the reason for the visit. A patient who remembers the distant past but not the last hour is showing a pattern that points towards a physical or progressive cause rather than an emotional one.

The level of consciousness should be described in words and, where the patient is not fully awake, measured with a recognized scale. The Glasgow coma scale is the one most widely used, summarizing the eye, verbal, and motor responses so another clinician can follow the trend. The points at which a score should prompt urgent escalation differ between services and editions, so the number that matters in your setting must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol rather than assumed from memory. What transfers between settings is the direction of travel, because a falling score is more alarming than a low but stable one. The scale also cannot be read without the patient's baseline, since a longstanding disability may produce a poor score at rest.

Delirium is the syndrome this examination exists to catch, and it must be named early. It is a disturbance of attention and awareness developing over a short period, fluctuating through the day, with a change in cognition not explained by a pre-existing dementia. It is common, dangerous, and missed constantly, because the patient may be quiet rather than agitated, because the family is told he is simply confused, or because the examiner has decided the problem is psychiatric. Causes to seek include infection, a low level of oxygen, a low blood glucose, an electrolyte disturbance, a drug or its withdrawal, retention of urine, constipation, pain, stroke, a bleed in the head, and deficiency of thiamine or cobalamin. What reveals delirium is not a long interview but a repeated one, because the fluctuation is the sign.

Delirium and dementia are separated by time and by attention. Dementia is a chronic, progressive decline in an alert patient, while delirium is an acute disturbance in a patient whose attention wanders and whose arousal swings. They commonly coexist, because a patient with dementia is more vulnerable to delirium, and one does not exclude the other. The examiner who labels an acute confusion as dementia may send home a patient with an untreated infection or a fractured hip. The finding to seek is always the change from the patient's own baseline, which is why a relative's account is worth more than any single test.

Insight and judgment are best observed rather than interrogated. Insight is the patient's understanding that something is wrong, that it has a name, and that it needs treatment, and it ranges from full understanding to an unshakable certainty that everyone else is mistaken. Judgment is the capacity to make decisions that protect the patient and those around him, assessed by what he would do in a practical difficulty and by what he has actually done. A patient who has stopped eating because of a belief, or spent the rent on a delusional project, has demonstrated his judgment more clearly than any hypothetical question. Insight and judgment determine whether he can accept help and be cared for at home.

Risk is a conclusion drawn from everything above rather than a separate examination. The examiner asks directly about thoughts of death, of self-harm, of a plan, and of available means, and about any previous attempt, because the patient who is asked is far more likely to tell. Suicidal intent is the dangerous finding that must be named early, and it is revealed not by a dramatic appearance but by the answer to a direct question, by a plan, by means at hand, by recent loss, and by past attempt. Thoughts of harming others should be asked about too, because the safety of the patient and the household are considered together. A patient who expresses intent requires immediate supervision and the involvement of the clinical team, and the details belong to local protocol rather than to this book.

The physical examination continues throughout and should not wait. The examiner takes the pulse, the blood pressure, the temperature, and the respiratory rate, and looks at the pupils, the neck, the skin, the tongue, and the hands. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius or more in a confused patient demands a search for infection, though what counts as fever varies with the site measured, the time of day, and the patient's age, so confirm the threshold against the current guideline and the local protocol. A pulse of 110 beats per minute may be fear, fever, pain, dehydration, or withdrawal, and the alarming figure differs in a trained athlete and in a patient taking a drug that slows the heart, so check the current guideline and the local protocol. A small pupil, a drooping eyelid, facial asymmetry, weakness of an arm, or new confusion about the day all narrow the search away from the mind.

The neurological examination is the bridge between the two worlds. A patient with a change in mental state should be examined for neck stiffness, rash, weakness on one side, an abnormal plantar response, incoordination, and tremor, because meningitis, encephalitis, stroke, and intracranial bleeding all present this way. A headache with fever and a stiff neck is a medical emergency, and the finding that reveals it is a neck that will not bend rather than any psychological observation. A patient confused after a fall must be assumed to have a head injury until it is excluded. The confusion may also be a seizure, a state after a seizure, or a drug effect, and each possibility changes what is done next.

In the well-resourced hospital, testing answers specific questions. Blood is taken for glucose, electrolytes, calcium, thyroid function, a blood count, and markers of infection, and results are read as evidence rather than verdicts. Computed tomography, or CT, of the head is used when bleeding, a mass, or a fracture is suspected, and magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, when the question needs finer detail or different tissue contrast. An electrocardiogram, or ECG, is recorded because a rhythm disturbance can reduce the blood supply to the brain and produce confusion. A lumbar puncture is performed when infection of the coverings of the brain is suspected, and an electroencephalogram is used when a seizure disorder is considered. The intensive care unit, or ICU, is involved when the patient cannot be kept safe on an ordinary ward. None of this removes the need for the description, since a normal scan in a deteriorating patient is a reason to repeat the reasoning rather than to relax.

In the clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same questions are asked with different instruments. The torch examines the pupils and their reaction to light, looks into the throat, and shows whether the neck can bend. The thermometer identifies a fever the history alone might have missed, and the stethoscope may reveal a murmur pointing to infection of a heart valve or crackles pointing to pneumonia. Blood glucose can be tested with a handheld meter where one exists, and otherwise the history of diabetes and the signs of low sugar such as sweating, tremor, and hunger must be relied upon. The most powerful instrument available is a relative who can say what the patient was like a week ago. When a physical cause cannot be excluded with what is at hand, the decision is about referral and not about a psychiatric label.

The physical illnesses that present through the mental state examination form a long list that must be carried in mind. Thyroid disease can produce agitation, depression, or a slowing that looks like dementia, and adrenal disease can produce anxiety, weakness, and confusion. A low level of oxygen, a low glucose, a disturbance of sodium or calcium, liver or kidney failure, and deficiency of thiamine or cobalamin all alter behavior and thinking. Infection anywhere, and especially infection of the brain or its coverings, the human immunodeficiency virus, and syphilis of the nervous system can present first as a change in the mind. Autoimmune disease affecting the brain, bleeding beneath the skull, tumor, and the effects of alcohol or prescribed drugs are further causes. Each is found by looking for it, and none is found by looking only at behavior.

Drugs and substances deserve particular attention because they are so often the cause and so often concealed. A prescribed medicine may cause confusion, or a combination of medicines may, and the list must be reviewed rather than assumed. Withdrawal from alcohol, sedatives, or opiates produces restlessness, sweating, and tremor, and alcohol withdrawal in particular may bring seizures and hallucinations. Stimulant drugs produce agitation, paranoia, and tactile hallucinations, while cannabis and hallucinogens distort perception in ways patients find hard to describe. Ask about these openly and without judgment, because a patient who feels accused will stop talking. The finding that changes the plan is often the simplest, whether a list of medicines, a smell on the breath, or a pupil that does not behave as it should.

Culture, language, and belief shape what is normal and what is pathological, and difference must not be converted into disease. A belief shared by the patient's community, an unfamiliar way of expressing distress, and a religious experience meaningful to the patient are not delusions merely because the examiner does not share them. A patient interviewed in a second language may appear to have a disorder of thought when the difficulty is translation and fatigue. Where an interpreter is needed, the interpreter should be trained, introduced, and asked to translate rather than summarize, and a family member should not carry the burden of a sensitive interview if that can be avoided. The examiner records what was observed and notes the circumstances, so the next clinician can judge how much weight the observation deserves.

The examination should be repeated, because one observation is a snapshot and illness is a film. A patient calm and oriented in the morning may be drowsy and disoriented in the evening, and the difference between the two carries more information than either alone. The examiner records the time of the observation, what the patient was doing, what had been given, and what had changed since the last look. This is how a fluctuating delirium is caught and how quiet deterioration is noticed before a crisis. Writing the same description in the same order at each review makes the comparison possible.

Documentation uses the same discipline as any other note. The Soap notes framework, recording subjective information, objective findings, assessment, and plan in that order, keeps the observation separate from the interpretation and makes the reasoning visible to the next reader. The description should state what was seen and heard in plain language, and it should state what was not found as well as what was, because the absence of a commanding hallucination or of suicidal intent is a finding of real value. The source of the information should also be recorded, since a relative's account is a different kind of evidence from the patient's own. A note containing the reasoning can be reviewed, while one containing only a label cannot.

The compressed examination at the bedside is a skill every clinician needs. When the patient will not stay still, when the setting is public, or when time is short, the examination does not disappear but is reduced to the observations that cannot wait. In 2 minutes or so the examiner can note the level of alertness and its fluctuation, whether attention can be held, whether speech is coherent, whether the patient is oriented to place and person, whether there is fever, a stiff neck, a small pupil, weakness on one side, or a wound, and whether he expresses any intent to harm himself. That interval is a practical compromise rather than a rule, and what can be achieved within it depends on the patient and the setting, so confirm the expectations of your own service against the current guideline and the local protocol. The governing principle is simple, in that safety questions come first and elegant descriptions wait until the patient is safe.

A constructed illustration can make the opposite point. A young woman is reviewed after an overdose and says she is fine and the tablets were a mistake. The examiner who accepts the statement has performed an incomplete examination, because the finding that matters is not her summary but her plan, her means, her previous attempts, and whether she still wishes she had died. The safe habit is to ask the uncomfortable question directly and to record the answer. The examination is finished only when the risk has been described, not when the patient has stopped talking about it.

The order of the examination can be varied, but its coverage cannot be reduced. Appearance, behavior, speech, mood and affect, thought form, thought content, perception, cognition, insight, judgment, and risk are observed in every patient, even if briefly and some in passing. A missing domain is a blind spot, and blind spots are where dangerous diagnoses hide. The examiner who checks the same list every time notices the one item that does not fit, and it is usually the item that changes the plan. A short examination covering every domain is better than a long one that forgets perception or risk.

The findings should be assembled into a description another clinician can act upon. Rather than isolated labels, the record should say how the patient appeared and how that changed, what he believed and how firmly, what he perceived and whether it commanded him, what he could remember and attend to, and whether he understood that he was ill. From that description the examiner forms a short differential including both psychiatric and physical possibilities, ranked by danger as well as likelihood. The dangerous end is addressed first, and the rest with the patient's safety in view. The purpose changes with the setting, because an emergency examination decides safety, a clinic examination detects an unnamed depression and decides which tests precede a psychological explanation, and a review follows the course of an illness and its treatment.

The patient who cannot speak, or will not, is still open to examination. A mute patient may be depressed, catatonic, frightened, unable to use language, or unwilling, and these are distinguished by observation and physical testing. A patient who does not respond to a command may not have heard it, understood it, been able to move, or may be refusing, and each possibility is tested differently. Eye tracking, the response to a painful stimulus, resistance to passive movement, and the ability to follow a simple instruction all give information. A failure to answer should not be read as a failure to perceive until the physical reasons have been examined. Catatonia belongs here, because it is dramatic, treatable, and easily mistaken for a hopeless state, presenting as held postures, resistance to movement, repetition of a movement or word, or mute immobility, and carrying a risk from the immobility itself that demands urgent attention.

Finally, this chapter is educational material and nothing more. It is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, or the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient. Every score, threshold, range, and duration mentioned here is an illustration of reasoning, and each must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol before it influences the care of a real person. Where a patient is at risk of harm, the description stops and protection begins, and the details belong to the rules of your own service. No case described here is a real patient, because each is a constructed illustration made to show a piece of reasoning.

To summarize, the mental state examination is a structured description of how a person appears, speaks, feels, thinks, perceives, remembers, understands, and judges, and it is evidence rather than a diagnosis. Appearance and behavior, speech, mood and affect, thought form and content, perception, cognition, insight, judgment, and risk are covered in every patient, however briefly, and the description is repeated because fluctuation is itself a finding. Suicidal intent, a commanding hallucination, agitation from an unmet physical need, and a delirium mistaken for a psychiatric disorder are the dangerous findings that must be named and sought early, each with the observation that would reveal it. A long list of physical illnesses presents through the mind, so the body is examined with the same care as the conversation, in the hospital with its laboratory and imaging and in the clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer. Every score, cut-off, and duration in this chapter is an illustration, and each must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. When safety is in question, the description yields to protection.
