# Chapter 46 — Respiratory Disease

Respiratory disease is organized by the pattern each illness produces rather than by a list of names. The clinician listens first for the tempo, because an illness that has developed over an hour asks a different question from one that has developed over a year, and then for the dominant disturbance, whether that lies in the airways, in the air sacs, in the pleural space, or in the vessels that carry blood through the lung. A cough, a fever, a wheeze, a chest pain, and a breathlessness that is worse on effort are the raw material, and the way they combine points more reliably to a mechanism than any single symptom can. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice, it is not a protocol, and it is not a substitute for training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.

The causes that kill must be named before the comfortable ones, each with the finding that would reveal it. A patient with asthma who becomes too breathless to speak and whose chest falls silent has a silent chest, a sign of failing air movement rather than of improvement. A patient with sudden breathlessness, pain on one side, absent breath sounds, and a falling pressure may have a tension pneumothorax, recognized and acted on at the bedside before any image is taken. A large pleural effusion gives stony dullness over half the chest, reduced breath sounds, and a windpipe pushed away from the affected side, and the danger is respiratory failure as the good lung is compressed. A clot in the lung may leave the chest sounding entirely normal, and that mismatch between severe breathlessness and a quiet examination should raise the possibility rather than dismiss it. A patient with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease who is drowsy during an exacerbation may be retaining carbon dioxide, and a patient with pneumonia who is cold, hypotensive, and passing little urine has moved into septic shock.

Breathlessness appears whenever the work of breathing rises or the oxygen in the blood falls, and disease of the chest disturbs one of four structures. The airways can narrow, as in asthma and in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, so that air leaves the chest slowly and each breath costs more effort. The air sacs can fill with fluid, pus, or blood, as in pneumonia and in pulmonary edema, so that a region of lung is supplied with blood but not with air. The pleural space can fill with air, fluid, or pus, so that the lung is compressed and cannot expand, and the pulmonary vessels can be blocked, as in pulmonary embolism, so that part of the circulation is wasted. The clinician who places the story into one of these four disturbances already knows which signs to seek, and the same reasoning must serve in two different rooms, because a clinician who reasons well without imaging will use it better when it exists.

The assessment begins with the patient's appearance and the simple observations that cost nothing. The respiratory rate is the most sensitive vital sign in chest disease and the one most often estimated rather than counted, so it should be counted and recorded. A rate of 30 breaths per minute in an adult is often quoted as a sign of serious illness, and that number is an illustration of the reasoning which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the threshold that should prompt action varies between services and editions. The ability to speak in full sentences and to walk without stopping are practical measures of reserve, and a patient who can no longer complete a sentence is more concerning than any single number. The work of breathing shows in the neck muscles, in the drawing in of the spaces between the ribs, in sweating, and in a posture that fixes the shoulders.

The history is taken in a disciplined way, and the first thing to establish is the tempo. Breathlessness that arrives over minutes suggests a clot, a pneumothorax, or an attack of asthma, while breathlessness that has grown over weeks suggests pneumonia that is not resolving, a large effusion, anemia, or a failing heart. A cough present for days is a different problem from one present for months, and the clinician should ask when it began and what has changed. A dry cough suggests an interstitial process, an early viral illness, or a reaction to a medicine, while a productive cough suggests infection. Any blood in the sputum must be asked for, because patients often do not volunteer it, and hemoptysis can come from an infection, a tumor, a clot, a damaged vessel, or tuberculosis. Fever, shivering, night sweats, weight loss, and a change in voice belong in the same account.

The context in which the illness appears changes what it probably means. Smoking, present and past, is the strongest single piece of history in chronic airway disease and in lung cancer, and the number of years and the amount should be recorded. Occupation matters, because exposure to dust, asbestos, silica, and fumes produces disease years after the exposure has ended. The clinician asks about travel, contact with someone who has tuberculosis, crowded living, and contact with birds, animals, or molds. A history of allergy, eczema, or hay fever raises the probability of asthma, while a previous clot, a recent operation, immobility, pregnancy, a cancer, or treatment with estrogen raises the probability of pulmonary embolism.

The examination is performed in a fixed order so that no region is skipped, and it begins before the stethoscope is used. The clinician inspects the shape and movement of the chest, the use of the accessory muscles, and the position the patient has chosen, then examines the hands for clubbing, the lips for central cyanosis, and the ankles for swelling. Percussion from side to side and from top to bottom distinguishes the resonant note from the dull note of fluid or consolidation and the hyper-resonant note of trapped air. Auscultation follows, listening for breath sounds that are reduced, bronchial, or absent, for crackles, wheeze, and a pleural rub, and comparing one side with the other. Auscultation is powerful and also limited, because a normal chest does not exclude a clot in the lung, an early infection, or a small effusion, and a quiet chest may mean either that disease is absent or that so little air is moving that nothing can be heard. The clinician should therefore look for signs outside the chest, among them clubbing, cyanosis, a raised jugular venous pressure, a tender swollen leg, and enlarged nodes above the collarbone, and should repeat the examination in a patient who is deteriorating.

Pneumonia is infection of the lung tissue, and it produces a recognizable pattern when the history and examination are put together. The patient is usually feverish, with a cough, sputum, breathlessness, and a pain in the side that catches on breathing. The examination may show dullness over the affected region, crackles, bronchial breath sounds, and an increased transmission of the spoken voice, and these together point to solid lung rather than air. The finding that separates pneumonia from other causes of febrile breathlessness is a focal abnormality that corresponds to the symptoms, because a pneumothorax gives hyper-resonance and absent breath sounds, a large effusion gives stony dullness with reduced breath sounds, and a clot typically gives a normal chest. The older patient may have none of the classical signs and may present with confusion or a fall. A pneumonia that does not resolve as expected deserves a second look, because a tumor obstructing a bronchus can produce an infection that clears partially and then returns in the same place.

The severity of pneumonia is judged by how the patient is behaving rather than by the radiograph alone. Confusion, a climbing breathing rate, a falling pressure, and advanced age all raise the risk, and a formal severity score such as the Curb sixty-five score organizes exactly those features into a decision about where the patient should be treated. Its contents and the figure at which it should change the plan must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the components and thresholds differ between editions and services. The score supports the examination and never replaces it, since a patient who looks unwell is treated as unwell whatever the arithmetic says. Treatment is the right answer when the picture is compatible and the patient is unwell, and the wrong answer when a clear non-infectious cause explains the findings, while samples that name the organism are most useful taken before treatment begins.

Asthma is a disease of variable airway narrowing, and its signature is that it changes. The patient describes attacks of breathlessness, wheeze, and cough that come and go, often worse at night and provoked by cold air, exercise, dust, or infection. Between attacks the examination may be completely normal, which is why the history carries more weight than the examination. Severity is assessed by the ability to speak and by the work of breathing, and the peak flow measurement is a supporting number rather than the arbiter. A peak flow that has fallen to less than half of the patient's usual best is often used to mark a severe attack, and that proportion is an illustration of the reasoning which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the fraction that should trigger alarm differs between age groups, services, and editions. The most dangerous finding is the disappearance of the wheeze, because a silent chest means that so little air is moving that no sound is made.

Drowsiness, exhaustion, and a slowing of the breathing rate are signs that the patient is losing the fight and need help at once, and a patient who can speak only in single words is in a severe attack. A normal peak flow in a struggling patient does not make a severe attack mild. Treatment is the right answer when the patient cannot speak comfortably, when the work of breathing is visible, or when repeated relief has not lasted, and it is the wrong answer to rely on a single measure of airflow while the patient is tiring. The finding that would make a clinician reconsider the diagnosis is a focal chest abnormality, a fever with a productive cough, or a sudden onset with one-sided pain, each pointing away from asthma and toward infection, a pneumothorax, or a clot. The interval over which improvement should be expected is a local matter, and the expected timing must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it differs with the treatment given and the setting.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease is a persistent narrowing of the airways that develops over years, usually in a person who has smoked. The patient describes a cough with sputum for years, breathlessness that has crept up on effort, and a chest that may be barrel-shaped with a prolonged expiration. An exacerbation is recognized by a change from the patient's usual state, most often breathlessness that is worse, more sputum, sputum that has changed in color, and a sense of being unwell. Fever may be absent and the chest may sound unchanged, so a clinician who relies on auscultation alone will miss the deterioration. The patient who is tiring must be recognized early, because drowsiness, confusion, a headache, and a bounding pulse may mean that carbon dioxide is accumulating. A patient who was fighting to breathe and now seems settled may be deteriorating rather than improving. Treatment is right when the patient is failing to clear carbon dioxide or cannot maintain the work of breathing, and the finding that would give pause is a clear alternative cause.

Tuberculosis is an infection that develops slowly, and its leading symptom is a cough that persists. A cough lasting more than 2 or 3 weeks, particularly with night sweats, fever, weight loss, and a loss of appetite, should raise the possibility, and that interval is an illustration of the reasoning which must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the duration that should trigger investigation is stated differently in different countries and editions. Blood in the sputum is a warning sign at any time and may be the only symptom the patient mentions. The disease may affect the pleura, the lymph nodes, the spine, the kidney, or the lining of the brain, so a respiratory complaint may be absent altogether. The clinician should ask about contact with a person who has tuberculosis, about crowded living, about previous treatment, and about infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV.

The examination in tuberculosis is often normal, and that is one of its most important limitations. The classical findings of wasting, fever, enlarged neck nodes, and changes at the top of one lung are often absent, and a chest radiograph may be normal in early disease, in a patient whose immune defenses are suppressed, and in disease outside the lung. Because examination and image are both unreliable, the diagnosis rests on identifying the organism, and sputum is the sample that usually provides it. Where the patient cannot produce sputum, the absence of a sample is not the end of the investigation, and other ways of obtaining material must be considered. Tuberculosis is transmitted through the air, so a patient with a prolonged cough who is unwell should be separated from others, and the clinician should use the protective clothing and mask that the local protocol requires. The combination of HIV infection and tuberculosis is common and dangerous, because each worsens the other.

Inflammation of the pleura without fluid produces a sharp pain that catches on breathing and on coughing, and the finding is a pleural rub, a coarse grating sound heard on both inspiration and expiration. When fluid accumulates in the pleural space, a small effusion may produce nothing at all, while a larger one gives stony dullness to percussion, reduced breath sounds, and a reduced transmission of the spoken voice over the fluid. As the effusion grows, the lung is compressed and the windpipe may be pushed toward the opposite side, and the patient becomes breathless on less and less effort. The danger of a large effusion is respiratory failure, and the danger of an infected effusion is sepsis, so a large or infected collection needs drainage and help without delay. The character of the fluid matters, because clear fluid, cloudy fluid, blood, and pus each point to a different cause, and the sample taken by a needle is what distinguishes them. A patient whose fever persists after an effusion is drained may have an empyema that has not been fully evacuated.

A pneumothorax is air in the pleural space, and its presentation depends on how much air has entered and how quickly. A spontaneous pneumothorax in a tall young person or in a patient with chronic lung disease produces a sudden pain on one side and breathlessness, with absent breath sounds and a hyper-resonant note over the affected side. A tension pneumothorax is the lethal form, in which air enters with each breath and cannot escape, so that the pressure rises, the lung collapses, the windpipe is pushed to the opposite side, the neck veins become distended, and the pressure falls. That diagnosis is made at the bedside and must be acted on before any image is obtained, because waiting for a radiograph in that situation can cost the patient's life. The clinician should suspect it in any patient who becomes suddenly breathless and shocked after an injury, after a procedure on the chest, or without any apparent cause. The finding that argues against it is breath sounds that are present and equal on both sides.

Pulmonary embolism is a clot that has traveled to the lung, and it is the diagnosis most often missed because the chest sounds normal. The patient may present with sudden breathlessness, a sharp pain on breathing, a cough, blood in the sputum, a racing pulse, or a fainting episode, any of which may occur alone. The risk factors should be sought deliberately, among them a previous clot, a recent operation, immobility, a long journey, a swollen tender leg, a cancer, pregnancy, and treatment with estrogen. The clinician should examine the legs for a difference in size and for tenderness along the deep veins, because that finding supports the diagnosis when the chest is unhelpful. A clinical prediction rule such as the Wells score organizes these features into a probability, and its exact contents and thresholds must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the rule has been revised. The mismatch between severe breathlessness and a calm chest is the central clue, and it should make the clinician ask about risk factors rather than move on.

A massive embolism is the form that kills within minutes, and it announces itself through the heart rather than the lung. The patient may collapse, become pale and sweaty, have a very rapid pulse, and show a raised jugular venous pressure, while the chest remains quiet and the blood pressure falls. The patient may be confused or lose consciousness because the circulation is failing rather than because the lungs have stopped working, and help must be summoned at once without waiting for a confirmatory test. The finding that supports the diagnosis is a sudden collapse with a normal chest and a strain on the right side of the heart, while the finding that points elsewhere is a chest full of crackles with a raised jugular venous pressure in a patient known to have a failing heart. A normal chest examination in a patient who has collapsed is a reason for urgency, not reassurance.

Assembling the differential means sorting the findings by the mechanism they suggest and ranking the possibilities by both likelihood and danger. Breathlessness with a focal chest sign, fever, and sputum places infection high; a silent chest with a history of attacks places asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease high; stony dullness places fluid in the pleural space high; a normal chest with a risk factor places a clot high; and sudden breathlessness with absent breath sounds places a pneumothorax high. The clinician should then add the causes that do not live in the lung, several of which imitate respiratory disease closely enough to mislead. Heart failure gives breathlessness that is worse lying flat, a raised jugular venous pressure, crackles at both bases, and ankle swelling, while anemia gives breathlessness on effort with a normal chest and a low concentration of hemoglobin in the blood. A metabolic disturbance that makes the blood acidic produces deep, sighing breathing without primary lung disease, and a large abdomen, a weak diaphragm, a muscle disease, or a poisoning can each reduce breathing without any abnormality inside the lung.

In a hospital the examination becomes the set of questions that tests are asked to answer. A chest radiograph shows consolidation, a large effusion, a pneumothorax, and the size of the heart, and it should be requested with a specific question rather than as a routine. A blood count, a measure of inflammation, and a measurement of oxygen and carbon dioxide answer different questions about infection, severity, and ventilation, while a sample of sputum can identify the organism in pneumonia and in tuberculosis. An ultrasound of the chest can detect a small effusion that a radiograph misses, computed tomography gives a detailed view of the lung tissue and its vessels, and spirometry separates the obstructive pattern from the restrictive one when the patient is well enough. The value of a test lies in the decision it changes, so a clinician who cannot say what either result would mean should not order it. A normal result in a patient with a high probability of disease is more often a reason to doubt the test than a reason to relax.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is conducted with fewer instruments and no less care. The respiratory rate is counted with a watch, the temperature is measured and recorded with its route, and the oxygen saturation is estimated from the patient's color and effort where no instrument exists. The stethoscope allows the clinician to compare the two sides of the chest, to hear crackles, wheeze, a rub, and a difference in breath sounds, and to detect dullness a hand cannot feel. The torch makes the throat, mouth, nose, and ear visible, and the sputum can be examined for color and volume or sent to a laboratory if the result would change the plan. What the clinic lacks in imaging it can partly replace with systematic examination, repeated observation, and a clear note of what was found. The central decision is whether the patient can be watched here or must travel, and a patient who is drowsy, whose breathing is visibly labored, whose chest is silent, or whose breathlessness appeared suddenly must go.

Treatment is part of diagnosis, and in respiratory disease the decision is often made before the cause is certain. In pleural disease the decision is whether the collection must be drained, and the finding that supports drainage is a large collection compressing the lung or an infected one causing fever and sepsis. In pneumothorax the decision is whether the air must be released at once, and the finding that supports immediate action is the tension pattern with a falling pressure and a shifted windpipe, while a small collection in a stable patient may be watched. In pulmonary embolism the decision is whether to treat for a clot, and the finding that supports it is a compatible picture with a high probability and no alternative explanation, while a clear alternative cause turns the decision the other way. In tuberculosis the treatment decision belongs to the program that manages the disease, and the finding that supports it is a compatible illness with the organism identified or strongly suspected. The specific medicines, their amounts, and the timing of their use belong to the current guideline and the local protocol, and this book names only the decisions they serve.

A patient with respiratory disease who is sent home must be given clear instructions about when to return. The signs that matter include a breathlessness that worsens, a chest that becomes painful, blood in the sputum, a fever that will not settle, a new drowsiness or confusion, a blue tinge to the lips, and a reduced ability to speak or eat. A follow-up visit arranged in advance is worth more than a promise to return if things worsen, because many patients cannot judge that for themselves. The record should show the reasoning and not only the numbers, including the respiratory rate and how it has changed, the work of breathing, the findings on each side of the chest, the appearance of the sputum, the risk factors for a clot, and what was considered and set aside. Infants and young children may deteriorate quickly, and older adults may present with confusion rather than a cough, so in these groups the trajectory over hours matters more than the first examination.

A constructed illustration may fix these habits, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a young patient with known asthma who arrives unable to speak in full sentences, sitting forward and sweating, with a respiratory rate of 30 breaths per minute and an almost silent chest. That rate is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because the figure that should trigger alarm varies with age and with the service. The clinician recognizes the silent chest as a sign of failing air movement rather than of improvement, and the plan changes within minutes. Now imagine an older patient who has been in bed after an operation and becomes suddenly breathless with a pulse of 120 beats per minute, a painful calf, and a chest that sounds entirely normal, in whom the mismatch between severe breathlessness and a calm chest prompts the search for a clot. The exact pulse at which a clinician should act is not fixed, and that figure too must be checked against the current guidance and the local protocol, because it changes with age, fitness, and the definition being used. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment.

To summarize, respiratory disease is read from the tempo of the complaint and from the mechanism it disturbs, whether that is the airway, the air sac, the pleural space, or the vessels within the lung, and the examination follows a fixed order so that no region is missed. The dangers are named where each disease appears: the silent chest of a tiring patient with asthma, the large effusion that compresses the good lung, the tension pneumothorax recognized at the bedside, the drowsy patient who is retaining carbon dioxide, and the patient with a clot in the lung whose chest sounds entirely normal. Pneumonia is separated from other causes of febrile breathlessness by a focal abnormality that matches the symptoms. Tuberculosis is considered in every cough that persists, because the examination and the radiograph are both unreliable and the organism must be found. Pleural disease and pulmonary embolism each carry a specific finding that turns a vague breathlessness into a decision, and the same reasoning serves in a hospital and in a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer. Every figure in this chapter, whether a rate, a pressure, a temperature, a proportion, a score, or an interval, is an illustration of reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories.
