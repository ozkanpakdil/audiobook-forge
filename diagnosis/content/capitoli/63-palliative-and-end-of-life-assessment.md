# Chapter 63 — Palliative and End-of-Life Assessment

Palliative and end-of-life assessment begins when the question that organizes care changes. The clinician is no longer asking only what disease is present, but what is happening now, what is likely next, and what would make the remaining time better. Some patients have a cancer that has spread, some have a heart, a kidney, or a lung that is failing beyond repair, and some are very old and frail with many things wrong at once. What they share is that the burden of investigation and of treatment must now be weighed against the comfort it buys. This is educational material, and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

Causes that can kill or disable within hours should be named before any comfortable assumption is made, because here they are common and each has a finding that can still be sought. A patient with a known cancer who develops a band of pain around the trunk, weakness of the legs, or difficulty passing urine may have compression of the spinal cord, and the finding is a level below which sensation and power are altered. A patient who becomes drowsy, confused, thirsty, and constipated may have a raised calcium level in the blood, and correcting it can restore alertness. A patient who becomes pale, sweaty, and faint, or who vomits blood, may be bleeding, and the finding is a falling pressure with a rising pulse. A patient who is difficult to rouse after a medicine for pain has been increased may be suffering the sedative effect of that medicine, and the finding is a falling breathing rate with small pupils. A temperature of 38 degrees Celsius in a previously stable patient should be explained rather than blamed on dying, though the figure that defines fever varies with the route of measurement, so confirm it against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Comfort and diagnosis are not opposing duties, and a clinician who treats them as opposites will fail the patient in one direction or the other. Many symptoms at this stage are reversible or partly reversible, and the search for their cause is the same disciplined search taught elsewhere, now directed at what can be relieved. A confusion caused by constipation, by retention of urine, by a medicine, by an infection, or by a dry mouth differs from the confusion of the final hours, and examination tells them apart. A test that would not change comfort may be set aside, while a test that would reveal a treatable cause of suffering is worth doing even in a patient who is clearly dying.

The history uses the same framework as any other, but the questions of most weight are different. The clinician asks what the patient understands, what the patient has been told, and what the patient believes is happening, because those beliefs shape every decision. The patient is asked what matters most now, whether freedom from pain, a particular conversation, being at home, or being spared a particular indignity. Symptoms are recorded with the Oldcarts framework, noting what makes each better or worse and what the family has already tried. The family's understanding, fears, and capacity to give care belong in the same conversation.

Prognosis is a judgment revised as the patient changes, used to decide how much should be done rather than to close a door. One useful discipline is to ask whether the clinician would be surprised if this patient died within a defined period, a question that formalizes an impression experienced clinicians already form. Any interval attached to it is an illustration and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because published periods differ between services, populations, and editions. Function is the most useful single guide to how much strength remains, and scales that record walking, self-care, and time spent in bed formalize the observation that a decline in these abilities mirrors decline in the whole body.

Pain is the symptom families fear most, and its assessment begins with the recognition that it is usually more than a physical sensation. The clinician asks about site, quality, what brings it on, what relieves it, and what it prevents the patient from doing, and then asks what the pain means to the patient. A new pain, a pain that has changed in character, or a pain in a place where the disease is not known to be should prompt a search for a cause rather than an increase in medicine, because a fracture, a clot, an infection, or a developing compression of the spinal cord can present this way. The finding that makes a medicine for pain right is a pain whose pattern fits the known disease and which the patient reports as eased once treatment begins; the finding that makes it wrong is an unexplained new or worsening pain, or a deepening drowsiness while the pain is chased.

The character of the pain points to the kind of cause and therefore to the kind of relief. A constant, aching pain that is worse with movement usually comes from injury to tissue, while a burning, shooting, or electric pain, sometimes with numbness, usually comes from injury to a nerve, and it often responds poorly to the medicines used for the first. Not every pain needs a medicine, because a full bladder, a constipated bowel, a pressure sore, a tight dressing, a cold room, and an uncomfortable position can all be removed. Some patients cannot report pain at all, and assessment then rests on behavior, including a frown or grimace, a clenched jaw, guarding, restlessness, withdrawal, a cry, or a change in the usual pattern of sleep. A change in behavior should be treated as possible pain until another cause, such as a full bladder, a loaded bowel, a fracture, or an infection, has been sought.

Breathlessness is frightening in a way that pain is not, because it is felt as the threat of suffocation, and its assessment follows the same logic as anywhere else. The clinician looks for causes that can still be relieved, among them fluid around the lung, a pneumonia, a clot in the lung, a failing heart, a narrowed airway, a severe anemia, and weakness of the muscles of breathing. Each announces itself differently: fluid dulls the lung base and reduces breath sounds, infection brings fever and crackles, a clot may bring sudden onset with a normal chest examination, anemia shows pallor and a rapid pulse, and a narrowed airway brings a wheeze or a stridor. The clinician who asks what has changed, and who examines the chest, the neck, the legs, and the color of the patient, finds a treatable cause more often than expected.

The relief of breathlessness is a decision about what will help, made with the patient awake and the family informed. Where fluid around the lung is found, the decision is whether removing it would improve breathing enough to justify the procedure, and the answer depends on how quickly it reaccumulated and how the patient tolerated the last attempt. Where an infection is found in a patient who still wants to live for a defined goal, treatment may be right, while in a patient clearly in the last days comfort measures without antibiotics may be kinder. Oxygen is a treatment like any other, justified when the level of oxygen in the blood is low or when the patient reports relief, not merely because the patient is breathless, and the amount given belongs to the local protocol rather than to a book. A breathing rate above 30 breaths per minute is worth recording and following, but the line at which it alarms shifts with age, illness, and medicines, so confirm any such figure against the current guideline and the local protocol.

Nausea and vomiting are distressing and often reversible, and the search for their cause is one of the most rewarding tasks at this stage. The clinician considers a bowel that is not moving, a stomach that is not emptying, raised pressure inside the head, a raised calcium level, an infection, a disease of the kidney, and the medicines the patient already takes. Timing carries information: vomiting soon after eating suggests a problem of the stomach or its outlet, while vomiting unrelated to meals and worse in the morning may suggest raised pressure inside the head. The finding that makes treatment right is a cause that can be named and corrected; the finding that makes it wrong is a symptom blamed on the illness while a simple cause such as constipation or a medicine is present.

Constipation causes the most preventable suffering in this group, because it is common, easily missed, and produces confusion, pain, nausea, and restlessness that are then blamed on the disease. The abdomen is inspected, felt, and listened to, and a rectal examination may reveal hard stool, an empty rectum with a loaded bowel above it, or a mass. The dangerous possibility is an obstructed or perforated bowel, whose findings are a distended abdomen, vomiting, absent or high-pitched bowel sounds, and severe pain, and that patient needs urgent help rather than another laxative. A patient with a known cancer and new constipation with back pain must also be considered for compression of the spinal cord.

Confusion is the most important diagnosis to make in this population, because it is common, frightening to the patient and the family, and often reversible. The patient may be restless and hallucinating, or quiet, withdrawn, and drowsy, and the quiet form is most often missed because it looks like peaceful acceptance. The clinician asks whether the change came suddenly, whether it fluctuates, whether attention wanders, and whether it is worse in the evening, and then looks for the cause. The causes to seek are an infection, a full bladder, a loaded bowel, a medicine, alcohol withdrawal, a low level of oxygen, a low level of sugar, a raised calcium level, a disease of the kidney or the liver, a new stroke, and untreated pain.

The management of confusion begins with correcting what can be corrected and shaping an environment a confused person can understand. The room is kept quiet and lit, familiar people are present, the patient is told where he or she is, hearing aids and glasses are used, and the day is given a shape with light and activity. A medicine that quiets agitation may sometimes be needed, and the decision belongs to the clinician who knows the patient, because the medicine that calms one patient may deepen the confusion of another. The finding that would make such a medicine wrong is a cause not yet sought, a patient who can be settled otherwise, or a sedation that threatens swallowing and breathing.

The family is often the second patient, and its distress deserves assessment in its own right. The clinician asks who is doing the caring, how much sleep that person is getting, what the family believes is happening, what they fear most, and whether they are arguing about what should be done. Exhaustion, guilt, anger, and the feeling of being unprepared are findings that change the plan, not signs of weakness. A family that cannot lift the patient, cannot give the medicines, or cannot reach help at night is a family whose patient will suffer, whatever the clinical decisions, and where the caregiver is also old or ill the plan must assume that support is needed.

The recognition that a patient is dying is made from a pattern of findings rather than a single sign, and it should be described honestly and without euphemism. In the last days and hours the patient becomes drowsy and then difficult to rouse, takes less food and then less fluid, and may be unable to swallow safely. The hands and feet become cool and may take on a mottled, bluish color, the urine becomes scanty and dark, and the mouth becomes dry. Breathing becomes irregular with pauses and may sound moist or rattly because the patient can no longer clear the throat, which distresses the family but usually not the patient. The pulse becomes rapid and weak, and the patient may become restless or speak of seeing people who have died, which is common and not necessarily a sign of fear.

These signs are not a clock, and the clinician should resist giving an exact time. A patient may live for hours or for days after the breathing changes, and a patient who seems to be dying may rally before declining again, and both are ordinary. Any interval a guideline attaches to these findings is an illustration and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because published durations vary with the cause of death, the setting, and the population studied. What matters more than the prediction is the preparation: whether the family knows what to expect, whether symptoms are controlled, and whether the plan for the final hours is written and understood.

The hardest judgment is to separate a patient who is dying from one who has a treatable cause of looking as though he or she is dying, and the distinction is made by examination rather than mood. A drowsy patient with a rapid pulse and a fever may have an infection that would respond to treatment, and the decision depends on the patient's goals and the likely burden. A drowsy patient with a low level of sugar or a high calcium level may recover alertness if the cause is corrected, and that is worth seeking even near the end. The finding that makes the diagnosis of dying right is a pattern progressive over days, with the signs of the last days present together and no reversible cause found; the finding that makes it wrong is any treatable cause not yet sought.

Some events at this stage are lethal within minutes and must be anticipated rather than discovered, and the team should plan for them with the patient and the family. A patient with a cancer of the head, the neck, or the chest who develops a swollen face and neck, distended veins over the chest, and breathlessness when lying flat may have obstruction of the large vein returning blood to the heart. A patient with a severe headache, vomiting, and a falling level of consciousness may have raised pressure inside the head, and the finding is a new drowsiness with a change in the pupils or in the use of an arm or a leg. A patient who bleeds heavily from the mouth, the nose, or the bowel may be having a final hemorrhage, and the plan for that possibility, including dark towels, a quiet room, and a medicine chosen in advance, is made while the patient can still discuss it.

Not every patient who needs this assessment has a cancer, and the reasoning is adjusted for other trajectories. A patient with a failing heart, a failing kidney, a failing liver, or failing lungs may decline in steps, each severe episode followed by a partial recovery that leaves the patient weaker than before. A patient with advanced dementia may decline over years while the family carries the burden, and the assessment must consider what the patient can still enjoy, what the patient can still recognize, and what the family can sustain. A very old and frail patient may die without a single dominant diagnosis, and the sum of many small failures is then the diagnosis.

In a well-resourced hospital the assessment is carried out with a palliative team available, and what that team adds is time, expertise, and continuity a rotating ward team cannot provide. The triggers for calling them are a patient whose symptoms are not controlled, a patient whose goals of care are unclear or disputed, a family in crisis, and a patient who is dying without a written plan. The palliative team works alongside the treating team rather than replacing it, and the reason to involve it early is that its problems are easier to solve before a crisis than during one. The intensive care unit, or ICU, is not the opposite of palliative care, and the two meet in the patient who needs support for breathing or for the circulation while the goals of care are clarified. Where no such team exists, the same work is done by the clinician who is there, and the telephone is a legitimate instrument of the assessment.

Investigations at this stage are chosen for what they would change, the same test used everywhere in this book. A computed tomography, or CT, scan, or a magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, study, may be the only way to confirm compression of the spinal cord, and then the imaging is urgent because the ability to walk may depend on how soon treatment begins. A chest radiograph may reveal fluid that can be drained, a pneumonia that can be treated, or a fractured rib that explains a pain. A blood count and a measure of the calcium and the salts may reveal an anemia or a raised calcium level that explains drowsiness.

At home, where the family is the nursing staff, the assessment changes shape, and the clinician's most important instrument becomes teaching. The family must be told what to watch for, what to do when they see it, and whom to call, in words they can repeat back. The plan should be written down, because memory fails under strain, and it should name the signs of the last days, the signs that mean a call is needed now, and the person to call at any hour. Where the home has no electricity, no running water, and no transport, the plan must account for that, because a decision that assumes a car and a telephone is not a plan.

Much of the care at home is delivered by telephone, and the quality of that call depends on what was prepared before it. The clinician who knows the patient, the diagnosis, the current symptoms, and the family's capacity can make a safe decision without seeing the patient, and the call should end with a clear instruction about what to do and when to call again. A small supply of medicines kept at home for a crisis, chosen in advance for expected symptoms, is one way to prevent a night of suffering, and the choice and the amount belong to the local protocol and the prescriber rather than to this book. The finding that makes such a plan right is a family that understands when to use it and when to call first; the finding that makes it wrong is a family left alone with medicines and no instruction.

The conversation with the patient and the family is itself a diagnostic act, because it reveals what they understand and what they most need. The clinician asks what the patient has been told, what the patient wants to know, and how much detail the patient wants, since not everyone wants the same information. Plain words should be used for the diagnosis and the prognosis, because a euphemism the family does not share leaves everyone more frightened than before. In some families and cultures the request that the diagnosis be kept from the patient is made with the intent to protect, and the clinician should respond by asking what the patient already understands rather than by refusing outright. The ethical duty is to the patient, and the family is served by being included rather than by being obeyed.

Spiritual and cultural needs are part of the assessment and not an optional addition. The clinician should ask what gives the patient strength, whether the patient wishes to see a religious leader, whether there are rituals or foods or times of prayer that must be respected, and whether the patient fears anything after death. A patient afraid of being abandoned, of being in pain, or of being a burden may suffer more than the physical disease alone would produce, and that suffering will not appear on any examination. The beliefs of the family about death and about who should be present belong in the plan, because a plan that ignores them will fail at the moment it matters.

The assessment of the patient's ability to decide is a clinical skill, not a formality. A patient who can understand the information, retain it long enough to weigh it, and express a choice has the capacity to make that choice, and a patient who cannot needs a decision made in his or her best interest. Capacity is specific to the decision, so a patient may be able to decide about food and not about a major operation, and it may change from hour to hour in a confusion. Where a surrogate decides, that person should be asked what the patient would have wanted, and the answer is often a clear one.

Decisions about treatment are made by weighing the benefit the patient will feel against the burden imposed, and both sides are clinical findings. Fluids into a vein may relieve a dry mouth and a failing circulation, or may worsen the swelling and the breathlessness of a failing heart, and the deciding finding is whether the patient is thirsty and whether the extra fluid is accumulating. An antibiotic may cure a pneumonia and give more time, or add nothing but discomfort when the infection is part of the dying process, and the deciding finding is the patient's own goal and the severity of the illness. Food and fluid by mouth should be offered as long as the patient wants them and can swallow safely, and should not be forced, because forcing adds choking and distress without benefit. Nutrition by tube or into a vein is a treatment with its own burdens, and the finding that makes it right is a patient who may recover enough to enjoy something, while the finding that makes it wrong is a patient who is dying and for whom it prolongs the dying.

Much of the comfort of a dying patient comes from care that is not a medicine at all, and the clinician should assess it as carefully as any symptom. The mouth becomes dry and painful when the patient stops drinking, and cleaning it, moistening it, and treating a fungal infection when present can change the whole experience of the day. The skin over the heels, the hips, and the base of the spine should be inspected at every visit, and a red or broken area treated and the position changed, because a pressure sore can cause more suffering than the disease itself. The Soap notes framework works here as anywhere, provided the assessment section states the reasoning and not only the diagnosis, and the record should name the patient's goals, the family's understanding, and the decisions made and why.

A patient kept at home needs a clear safety plan as much as any other patient in this book. The family should be told which signs mean help is needed at once: a new confusion, a pain that cannot be settled, a difficulty in breathing not eased by the usual measures, a complete inability to pass urine, vomiting that will not stop, a new weakness in the legs, or bleeding that will not stop. The family's needs do not end with the death of the patient, and the clinician should ask how the family is sleeping, whether anyone is alone, and whether there are other dependents. A family present at the death, that understood what was happening, and that was given permission to grieve usually fares better than one kept away.

A constructed illustration may make the reasoning concrete, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than a portrait of any real person. Imagine an older woman with a cancer that has spread, who becomes drowsy and confused over two days while her family assumes the end has come; the clinician who examines her finds a distended bladder, a loaded bowel, and a dry mouth, and the confusion lifts once those are relieved. Now imagine a man in the last week of a long illness who becomes restless and breathless at night, with breaths that become irregular and pause; the clinician recognizes the pattern of the last days, explains it to the family, and stays with them rather than ordering a test that would change nothing. Neither patient is real, and neither story should be applied to a real person without a full assessment. What the two share is the discipline of looking at the patient, naming the cause if there is one, and deciding what will actually help.

To summarize, palliative and end-of-life assessment changes the question from what can be cured to what is happening now and what will happen next, and it does so without abandoning diagnosis. The lethal and reversible causes are named early and sought deliberately, among them compression of the spinal cord, a raised calcium level in the blood, a hemorrhage, an infection, and the sedative effect of a medicine for pain, each with the finding that reveals it. Pain, breathlessness, nausea, constipation, confusion, and the distress of the family are assessed by their cause, because a large share of them can be relieved, and the search is the same disciplined search taught in every other chapter. In a hospital with a palliative team the work is shared and the problems are caught earlier, while at home the family is the nursing staff and the clinician's instrument is teaching that is written down and repeated. Every score, cut-off, reference range, pressure, temperature, rate, or duration named in this chapter is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such figures differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. Comfort and diagnosis are not opposing duties, and the clinician who looks for the treatable cause of suffering is doing the most useful work in the room. This chapter is educational material and does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service.
