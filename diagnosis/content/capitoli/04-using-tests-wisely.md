# Chapter 4 — Using Tests Wisely

A test earns its place in the clinical encounter by changing a probability, and a changed probability has value only when it changes a decision. The commonest error is to order a test in order to confirm what the history and the examination have already made certain, which adds cost and delay without adding information. The second commonest error is to order a test without first asking what result would alter the plan, so that an unexpected answer arrives with no prepared response. Before anything is requested, the useful question is what finding would make me do something different, and if no finding would, the test should not be ordered. This is the reasoning that organizes the whole of this discussion, because every property of a test and every harm of testing follows from it.

The first property to understand is sensitivity, which describes the behavior of a test in people who truly have the disease. A test with high sensitivity is positive in most of the people who are ill, so it is good at finding disease and a negative result from it is reassuring. A hypothetical test might be positive in 90 per cent of people with a particular infection, and that figure is an illustration that you must check against the current guideline and the local protocol, since the real value shifts with the population, the technique and the edition of the reference you happen to be holding. Sensitivity is therefore the property that a clinician values when the danger of missing the diagnosis is high, because a sensitive test rarely lets a case slip past. It says nothing yet about the people who do not have the disease, and that is where the second property begins.

Specificity describes the behavior of the test in people who do not have the disease, and it is the property that governs how often a positive result is a mistake. A highly specific test is negative in almost everyone who is well, so a positive result from it is strong evidence that the disease is really there. If the same hypothetical test were negative in 95 per cent of healthy people, the figure is again only an illustration, and it must be confirmed against the current guidance and the practice of your own laboratory and ward before you treat it as a fact about your patient. Specificity protects the patient from the consequences of a wrong positive result, from unnecessary anxiety to unnecessary procedures. A test can be sensitive and not specific, specific and not sensitive, or good at both.

Sensitivity and specificity are usually stable properties of the test itself, at least in principle, while the predictive values belong to the patient in front of you. The positive predictive value asks what proportion of people who test positive actually have the disease, and the negative predictive value asks the same question of those who test negative. These two values depend heavily on how common the disease is in the group being tested, which is why the same test result can mean very different things in different settings. Prevalence is the word for that underlying frequency, and it is the quiet variable that most often decides whether a positive result is a genuine finding or a statistical accident.

Consider a constructed illustration of a test with a sensitivity of 90 per cent and a specificity of 95 per cent, values whose true form must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol because they differ between settings and editions. Suppose the test is applied in a community where the disease affects 1 in 1000 people, an illustrative proportion that must likewise be checked against the current guidance and local practice. Among a thousand people there will be about one person with the disease, and the test will probably find that person. Yet the small proportion of healthy people who test positive, when multiplied by the large number who are healthy, produces a surprising crowd of false positive results. The positive predictive value in that setting is therefore low, and most of the people the test flags will turn out not to have the disease. This is the arithmetic that explains why screening a whole population for a rare condition produces so much anxiety and so many follow-up tests.

Now move the same test into a clinic where the patients have been selected by their symptoms and the disease affects 1 in 5 of them, a proportion that is an illustration only and that must be checked against your own setting and the current guidance. The sensitivity and the specificity have not changed, because they are properties of the test and not of the room, but the predictive values are transformed. A positive result now means that the disease is genuinely present in a large majority of those who receive it, and the test becomes a useful instrument rather than a source of noise. Nothing about the chemistry or the physics of the test has altered; only the prior probability has. This single contrast is worth more than any number of measurements, because it shows that a test cannot be judged apart from the patient and the population in which it is used.

Likelihood ratios are the bridge between the properties of the test and the probability in the patient, because they tell you how much a given result multiplies the odds of disease. A likelihood ratio close to one, a value whose exact form you should confirm in the current guideline and local protocol, leaves the odds almost where they were, so the test has told you very little and its result should not move you. A ratio of perhaps 2 to 5 raises the odds usefully without settling the question, and these illustrative values must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol if you intend to use them in arithmetic rather than as a way of thinking. A large ratio, in the region of 10 or more, can take a modest suspicion and turn it into a strong one, which is what clinicians mean when they say that a test result was decisive, though the exact figure belongs to the current guideline and your local protocol and should be confirmed there. Ratios below one work in the opposite direction, reducing the odds and supporting the exclusion of disease. The practical point is that ratios can be carried in the head and applied to the patient in front of you, whereas sensitivity and specificity alone cannot.

Every diagnostic decision has two thresholds rather than one, and they are not the same line. The first is the point at which the probability of disease has risen enough that testing becomes worthwhile, because a positive result would change management and a negative result would be meaningful. The second is the point at which the probability has risen so far that treating without waiting for a result becomes the right thing to do, because the harm of delay now exceeds the harm of treating someone who turns out not to need it. Between those two lines lies the zone of genuine uncertainty, where a test is most valuable and where its result, whatever it says, will guide the next step. Below the first line you should not test, and above the second you should act, with the test left to confirm what you have already decided.

The position of those lines depends on the disease and on the consequences of being wrong, which is why a dangerous and a trivial condition cannot share a rule. For a disease that kills quickly if it is missed, such as bacterial meningitis or a leaking aortic aneurysm, the threshold for treatment is low and the clinician may act on suspicion alone. For a self-limiting illness that will resolve whatever is done, the same probability is not worth a test or a treatment, because the harm of the intervention is now the greater risk. The same test result therefore leads to different actions in the two situations, which is a point that scores and algorithms cannot decide for you. This is also why a rule derived in one population, such as a busy emergency department, may behave badly in another, such as a quiet outpatient clinic, because the mix of disease and the cost of error are not the same.

Testing is never free of harm, and the harm is easiest to see when a test is applied to people who are unlikely to have the disease. A false positive result sends the patient down a path of repeat tests, imaging, procedures, and referral, each with its own risk and its own waiting. It also produces anxiety that does not disappear when the follow-up result is normal, because the person has now been told that something was found. In a low-prevalence population the false positives can outnumber the true positives by a wide margin, as the earlier arithmetic suggested, so the test's main output may be worry rather than diagnosis. The discipline is to ask, before ordering, whether the chance of a false positive and its consequences are acceptable for this particular person.

Beyond the false positive lies the incidental finding, the unexpected shadow on an image or the borderline value on a laboratory report that was never going to harm anyone. Such findings are common precisely because modern tests look at more than the question being asked, and each one creates a new decision that did not exist before. The cascade that follows is familiar: a small abnormality is pursued with a larger test, which finds something else, which requires a procedure, and the patient ends by carrying a diagnosis and a risk that the original complaint never justified. Overdiagnosis is the name for the detection of disease that would never have declared itself in the patient's lifetime, and it is a harm of testing rather than a benefit of early detection. This is why an unexpected result should prompt the question of whether it explains anything the patient actually has, rather than an immediate escalation.

Each unexpected result also consumes something scarce, whether it is the patient's time, the clinician's attention, or the budget of a clinic that has only a few tests to spend. In a setting with limited resources the harm is sharper still, because a test performed on a patient who cannot act on the result may take the place of a test that someone else genuinely needed. A negative result can also mislead when the prior probability was high, since a sensitive test is not a perfect one and a low-prevalence interpretation of a high-prevalence patient is a common source of error. The honest position is that tests reduce uncertainty without removing it, and a result must always be read together with the history and the examination that preceded it. When a result contradicts a strong clinical impression, the first response is to question the result rather than the patient.

Spectrum bias is the reason a test so often performs better in the textbook description than in the clinic. The people in whom a test is first studied tend to be the obvious cases and the clearly healthy controls, so the test is measured where the difference between them is largest. In real practice the same test meets mild, early, atypical, and overlapping illness, together with people who have several conditions at once, and in that mixed company its sensitivity and specificity fall. The published figures, whatever they are, come from a particular population, and their transfer to your patient requires the assumption that your patient resembles that population, an assumption that is often false. This is one reason to be cautious with any figure quoted without its setting.

The reference standard problem is deeper, because measuring the sensitivity and specificity of a test requires a way of knowing who truly has the disease, and that way is usually imperfect. If the reference standard is itself a test with its own errors, then some people are misclassified, and the new test is credited or blamed for mistakes that belong to the standard. In some conditions there is no acceptable standard at all, and the best available answer is a consensus of experts, which is an opinion rather than a measurement. A test validated against a flawed standard inherits the flaw, and the number that emerges should be held loosely. When you read a study of a test, ask what the standard was and how it was applied, because that question decides how much the result is worth.

Test characteristics are also not fixed, because a test is a procedure performed by people in places, and both of those vary. A chest radiograph read by an experienced radiologist is not the same test as the same film read at the end of a night shift by someone who rarely sees one. An ultrasound examination depends on the hand holding the probe, on the machine, on the time taken, and on the question asked, so its accuracy belongs partly to the operator rather than to the instrument. A laboratory assay depends on the sample, the transport, the reagent, and the calibration. A test that is excellent in one hospital can be mediocre in another without anyone changing the instructions. This is why the local protocol matters as much as the textbook, and why an imported accuracy figure should be treated as a hope rather than a promise.

Serial testing means performing tests one after another, and it is used to increase specificity, because a second positive result is harder to obtain by chance than a first. The arithmetic of repetition is easy to underestimate, since a test with a modest chance of a false positive at a single pass gives a low-prevalence patient an accumulating chance of a false positive with each additional test. Parallel testing means ordering several tests at once and accepting a positive result from any of them, which increases sensitivity at the price of more false positives. A repeated test that yields the same result may confirm it, but a repeated test that disagrees with the first is a signal that the test, the timing, or the patient's condition has changed. The rule to carry away is that each additional test must be justified by the decision it could change, not by the discomfort of uncertainty.

There are times when the correct decision is not to test at all, and the first of these is the situation where no result would change what is done. If the patient is already going to be admitted, treated, or referred on the basis of the clinical findings, then a confirmatory test may add cost and delay while altering nothing. If the test is being ordered for reassurance alone, it should be clear whose reassurance is being sought and whether the test can honestly provide it. The question that prevents most unnecessary testing is the same question as before: what would I do differently if this result came back positive, and what would I do differently if it came back negative.

The second situation in which testing is the wrong move is the patient who is too unwell to wait for the answer. A person in shock, with a threatened airway, or with a rapidly spreading infection may need the decision now, and the time spent obtaining a result is time spent not treating. This is not a rejection of tests but a recognition of their place in the order of events, because the unstable patient is stabilized first and the test follows when it can no longer harm. The threshold for treating without waiting falls as the danger of the untreated condition rises, which is the same reasoning that set the two thresholds earlier. If the treatment is safe and the disease is lethal, the balance tips toward acting.

The discipline that ties all of this together is the habit of stating the probability before the test and the decision that each result would trigger. A pre-test probability held in words, even loosely, turns a test from a ritual into an experiment with an expected answer. If the probability is already very low, a positive result is more likely to be an error or a coincidental finding than a diagnosis, and the test should not be ordered without a plan for that outcome. If the probability is already very high, a negative result should not overturn the clinical impression without a serious second look. The clinician who writes down the question and the possible answers before ordering has already avoided most of the harm described here.

Tests also have to be explained, because a result that the patient cannot interpret is only half a result. A word such as positive and negative sounds absolute, while the reality behind it is a probability that has moved, and the gap between those two is where fear grows. Telling the patient what the test can and cannot show, and what the next step will be if the result is unexpected, is part of using the test wisely rather than an extra courtesy. The clinician should also be honest that some results will require further tests, and that this does not necessarily mean that something serious has been found. Uncertainty managed in conversation is lighter than uncertainty discovered later.

In a clinic without a laboratory, the discipline of testing still applies, because watching the patient over time is itself a form of testing. The change in a fever, the response of a pulse, the appearance of a new sign, and the evolution of pain all carry information, and they are the observations available when no result can be obtained. What matters is that the observation is planned and interpreted in the same way a laboratory result would be, rather than treated as a vague impression. Serial examination, in this sense, is a test with a sensitivity and a specificity of its own, and it is subject to the same biases and the same limits. A clinician who has no tests is not without evidence, only without machinery.

A normal result deserves as much suspicion as an abnormal one, because a false negative can be more dangerous than a false positive. The test may have been performed too early, when the organism or the marker was not yet present, or too late, when it had already gone. It may have been taken from the wrong site, transported badly, or interpreted against the wrong reference range. A reassuring result in a patient who looks unwell should therefore be treated as a discrepancy to be explained rather than a question that has been answered. The safest response is to repeat the reasoning that led to the test, not merely the test itself.

Scores and decision rules are useful because they force the clinician to collect the same information every time and to compare it with a defined threshold. They are also dangerous when they are used as substitutes for judgment, because a score is a summary of probabilities in a population and not a verdict about a person. A rule derived in one age group, one setting, or one severity of illness may lose much of its value when applied elsewhere. The score's own numbers change with the population and with the edition of the guideline, so any figure attached to it must be verified against the current source and the local protocol before it is used to make a decision. The proper use of a rule is to sharpen a probability that the clinician has already estimated, not to replace that estimate.

Every test also has a cost that is measured in more than money, because access to testing is uneven and a diagnosis that depends on it can become a diagnosis available only to some. When a test is unavailable, the clinician must reason with the history and the examination, and that reasoning is not inferior merely because it lacks a number. When a test is available, the clinician must resist the temptation to use it because it can be used. The equity question is not separate from the diagnostic question, since a test ordered without a plan can take a place that another patient needed. Using tests wisely means using them where they change outcomes, and not using them where they only move uncertainty around.

A final constructed illustration may help to hold the pieces together, and it is invented for that purpose rather than drawn from any patient. Imagine a middle-aged person who has had a fever for 3 days, a cough, and a fast pulse of 110 beats per minute, and note that both of those figures are illustrative and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, since such values differ between countries, laboratories, and wards. The clinician's pre-test probability of a chest infection is moderate, neither negligible nor certain, and a chest radiograph would move it in one direction or the other. If the result were positive, the decision to treat would follow, but if it were negative the clinician would still have to decide whether the clinical picture justified treatment, because the test cannot exclude every possibility. The right order is to decide the question first, then the test, then the response to each possible answer, rather than to order the test and wait to see what it says.

Any test is an intervention, and like any intervention it must clear a bar of expected benefit over expected harm. The benefit is the change in management that follows from a more accurate probability, multiplied by the value of that change to the patient. The harm includes the discomfort of the procedure, the anxiety of the wait, the complications of the follow-up, and the possibility that the pursuit of one answer will reveal something that was better left alone. A test that is justified in a research protocol may be unjustified in a clinic, and a test that is unjustified in a screening program may be exactly right in a symptomatic patient. The judgment belongs to the clinician who knows what will be done with the result.

All of this rests on a pre-test probability that someone has to estimate, and that estimate is itself a clinical skill. It begins with the history and the examination, then adjusts for the setting, the age of the patient, and the local pattern of disease. A clinician who has not thought about the prior probability will read every result as though it were equally meaningful in every patient, which is the error that makes a screening test seem useless in one room and excellent in another. The estimate does not have to be precise, only explicit enough to be revised. Writing it down, even as a sentence, exposes the assumptions that a number would otherwise hide.

Not every test is a diagnostic test, and the properties that make a test good at one job can make it unsuitable for another. A test used to diagnose must separate people who have the disease from those who do not, which is where sensitivity and specificity matter most. A test used to monitor a known condition must instead detect change over time in the same person, so its precision and its within-patient variability matter more than its ability to classify a stranger. A test used to screen a population must be safe, cheap, acceptable, and followed by a confirmatory path, because most positive results in that setting will be false. Using a monitoring test as a diagnostic test, or a diagnostic test as a screening test, is a category error that no amount of technical accuracy can repair.

It is often useful to ask how many tests must be performed to find one case that matters, because that question exposes both the yield and the cost. When the disease is rare and the test is imperfect, the number can be large, and the clinic spends most of its effort on people who do not have the condition. When the disease is common and the test is good, the number falls and the effort is repaid. This is not an argument against testing the rare disease in a patient who has the signs of it, because the relevant probability then belongs to that patient and not to the population. It is an argument against testing everyone for everything, which is a different activity with a different arithmetic. The decision to screen a population deserves a higher standard of evidence than the decision to investigate a symptomatic person, because the first exposes many healthy people to the harms of testing while the second exposes only the person who came for help.

To summarize, a test is a tool for changing a probability and therefore a decision, and it should never be ordered to confirm what is already known or to fill a silence. Its sensitivity and specificity describe what it does in people with and without the disease, while its predictive values depend on how common the disease is in the patient in front of you. Likelihood ratios carry that information into practice, the two thresholds tell you when to test and when to act, and the harms of false positives, incidental findings, overdiagnosis, and cascades are real harms that fall on real people. Every number used in this discussion is an illustration, and each must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol before it is used, because the values differ between countries, editions, laboratories, and wards. Ask before you order what finding would make you do something different, and if no finding would, do not order the test. And remember that this is educational reasoning and not medical advice, so no test, score, or rule can replace the judgment of the clinician who is with the patient, and none of it should be applied without supervision, the local protocol, and the current guidance in hand.
