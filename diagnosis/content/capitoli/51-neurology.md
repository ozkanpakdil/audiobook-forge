# Chapter 51 — Neurology

Neurology is the part of medicine in which the history and the examination decide more than any image, because the nervous system can be localized by reasoning alone and the hand and the eye often name the site first. Two questions are asked of every patient in this chapter: where in the nervous system is the trouble, and how fast is it moving. The first is answered by anatomy, by deciding whether the problem lies in the brain, the brainstem, the spinal cord, the nerve root, the peripheral nerve, the muscle, or the junction between nerve and muscle, since each level gives its own pattern of weakness, reflex change, and sensory loss. The second is answered by time, because the same weakness that has crept over a year means something entirely different from weakness that appeared in an hour. This chapter is educational material; it is not medical advice and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of the service in which the patient is seen.

The dangerous presentations are named before the ordinary ones, because each carries a finding that can be sought at the bedside and each punishes delay. A stroke that is still within the window in which it might be reversed comes first, and the finding that reveals it is the sudden onset of a focal deficit, such as a face that droops on one side, an arm that drifts when both are held out, or speech that has become slurred or absent. A subarachnoid hemorrhage is so often misread as an ordinary headache that it deserves special suspicion, and the finding is a headache that reached full severity in an instant, sometimes followed by a stiff neck and a dislike of light. Compression of the spinal cord gives a band of pain around the trunk, weakness of both legs, numbness in the saddle region, and difficulty passing urine. A weakness that progresses over hours or days is the fourth, and the pattern that matters climbs from the feet upward while the reflexes disappear, since the danger is failure of the muscles of breathing.

The same reasoning must hold in two very different rooms, though the consequences of the answers differ. In a hospital with computed tomography, or CT, magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, a laboratory, and a specialist within call, the history and examination decide which image to obtain and how urgently. In a clinic whose equipment is a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same history and examination decide whether the patient can be observed here or whether the journey must begin now. A clinician who can localize a lesion by hand and eye uses the scanner better when it exists, because the request already contains the question. What travels between the two settings is the method, not the machinery.

The examination is a process of localization rather than a checklist, and each maneuver is chosen because it separates one level of the nervous system from another. Power is graded by comparing the two sides and by testing the limb against resistance and against gravity, and the distribution of the weakness, whether it is confined to one limb, one side, both legs, or the face and arm together, is more informative than its severity. Reflexes are sought because a brisk reflex points above the level of the reflex arc while a lost reflex points to the arc itself, and weakness with a lost reflex and wasting describes a lower motor neuron problem. Sensation is tested for light touch, pinprick, vibration, and position, and the pattern of loss, whether in a glove, in a stocking, in a band, or below a level, tells the clinician where to look. Coordination, gait, and the ability to stand with the feet together and the eyes closed complete a first survey, and a deficit that was maximal from the first moment points to a vascular event while one that spread over minutes points elsewhere.

Stroke is the sudden loss of function caused by an interruption of the blood supply to part of the brain, whether by a blocked vessel or by a hemorrhage, and its signature is a focal deficit of abrupt onset. The differentiation that matters most is from a seizure, a migraine, and a low blood glucose, because each can imitate a stroke and each is managed differently. A seizure may leave a temporary weakness behind, but the weakness follows the event rather than beginning it, and the account usually includes shaking, a loss of awareness, or a bitten tongue. A migraine may produce weakness or numbness that spreads slowly over many minutes and then resolves, and that march of symptoms separates it from the abrupt arrival of a stroke. A low blood glucose can produce confusion, sweating, a focal deficit, and even coma, and the finding that reveals it is a glucose measurement that is low; the value that defines hypoglycemia must be read against the current guideline and the local protocol, since it differs between laboratories and between patient groups.

A stroke still within the window in which treatment might reverse it is a medical emergency, and everything else pauses while that possibility is settled. The window is measured in hours from the moment the patient was last known to be well, and a figure such as 4 and a half hours is commonly quoted, but that boundary must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it has changed with successive trials and differs between services. The clinician must also establish whether the stroke is caused by a blocked vessel or by bleeding, because the treatments are opposites, and the tool that separates them at speed is the CT scan. A hemorrhage may declare itself by a sudden severe headache, vomiting, or a falling level of consciousness, while a blocked vessel often spares consciousness at the outset. The level of consciousness is recorded with the Glasgow coma scale, whose contents and alarming figure must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, since its versions differ.

A subarachnoid hemorrhage is the diagnosis most often lost inside a headache, and it is named early here for that reason. It occurs when a vessel bleeds into the space around the brain, and its classic presentation is a headache of instantaneous onset, the worst the patient has ever felt, sometimes with vomiting, a stiff neck, a brief loss of consciousness, or a dislike of light. It is easily mistaken for migraine or tension, especially when it eases and the patient feels better, and that improvement is a trap because the bleeding may recur. The finding that reveals it in the first hours is a CT scan, and where the scan is negative and the suspicion remains high, the next step is examination of the cerebrospinal fluid obtained by lumbar puncture. A sudden, severe headache unlike any previous headache should not be sent home as a common headache, and the clinician who is unsure should seek advice rather than reassurance.

A transient ischemic attack is a focal neurological deficit that resolves completely, and it matters because it is a warning rather than a harmless event. The symptoms are the same as those of a stroke, including weakness on one side, difficulty speaking, or loss of vision in one eye, but they pass, often within minutes and usually within 24 hours; that outer boundary is a convention rather than a biological truth and must be checked against the current guideline and the local protocol, because it has been redefined and differs between services. The absence of a deficit at examination proves nothing about the risk that follows, and the patient who has had one deserves the same urgent assessment as the patient who still has symptoms. The clinician should record exactly what the patient experienced and how long it lasted, because the account often reveals a narrowing vessel. The decision that follows is how quickly to investigate, and it is made on the pattern of the episodes rather than on the reassurance of a normal examination.

A seizure is a sudden, excessive discharge of the brain's electrical activity, and the diagnosis rests on the history rather than on the examination, because the patient is usually recovered by the time the clinician arrives. A witness is therefore more valuable than any instrument, and the questions should establish what happened before the event, how it began, whether it involved one side or both, how long it lasted, whether the tongue was bitten, and how the patient behaved afterwards. The cause must be sought actively, because a first seizure may be provoked by a low blood glucose, an infection of the brain or its coverings, a disturbance of sodium or calcium, alcohol withdrawal, a head injury, a stroke, or a tumor. In a woman in the later stages of pregnancy or shortly after delivery, a seizure may signal a hypertensive emergency of pregnancy, and that possibility must be raised at once. The finding that most often changes the immediate course is a measurement of the blood glucose, because it is quick, widely available, and reversible.

Epilepsy is the tendency to have recurrent seizures that are not provoked by a temporary cause, and it is classified by where the seizure begins and whether awareness is preserved. A focal seizure starts in one part of the brain and may remain confined there, producing a twitching limb, a strange sensation, a smell that is not there, or a period of altered awareness that a witness describes as the patient going blank, and it may then spread to become a generalized convulsion. A generalized seizure begins across the whole brain at once, and the commonest form produces stiffening followed by rhythmic jerking and a period of confusion afterwards. The seizure type matters because it hints at the site and the cause, and because it guides the choice of investigation and the advice given to the patient. The clinician should ask how often seizures occur, what seems to bring them on, whether sleep is being lost, and whether alcohol or missed treatment lies behind a change.

A seizure that does not stop, or that repeats without recovery of consciousness between attacks, is a life-threatening emergency, and so is a seizure that compromises breathing. The patient is protected from injury, turned on the side, given oxygen where it exists, and observed continuously while help is summoned, and nothing is placed in the mouth. The clinician looks for the cause while treating the emergency, because a reversible trigger such as a low blood glucose or an infection may be the whole explanation, and in a pregnant patient a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy must be considered alongside epilepsy. Here the decision is named rather than the drug: treatment is the right answer when seizures continue and the airway cannot be protected, and it is the wrong answer when a single provoked seizure has already stopped and its trigger is being corrected.

Migraine is the commonest imitator of serious neurological disease, and recognizing it is a safety skill rather than a convenience. It produces a headache that is often throbbing, often on one side, often with nausea, vomiting, and a dislike of light or sound, and it may be preceded by an aura of flashing lights, zigzag lines, or a spreading numbness. The aura frightens both patient and clinician, because a tingling that marches from the fingers to the face over several minutes resembles a stroke, and that slow spread distinguishes it, since a vascular deficit arrives at once. A migraine with aura can also be accompanied by weakness, and that combination must be assessed as a possible stroke until the picture is clear, particularly in an older patient or one with risk factors. The diagnosis is made from the pattern over time, not from a single attack, so the clinician asks how many similar episodes there have been and what made them better.

Dementia is a decline in more than one area of mental function that is severe enough to interfere with ordinary life, and it is diagnosed by its pattern of loss and its course. The pattern tells the clinician where the degeneration is concentrated: a failing memory for recent events with preserved social manner suggests disease of the hippocampus and the cortex, difficulty with language points elsewhere, and a change in personality suggests damage to the front of the brain. The course matters as much as the pattern, and a figure such as 6 months of progressive decline describes the tempo rather than a rule, so it must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, where the intervals used differ. The causes that must not be missed are the reversible ones, among them a low thyroid hormone level, a deficiency of vitamin B twelve, a chronic infection, a tumor pressing on the brain, a blood collection over the brain, and the effect of medicines. Imaging by CT or MRI is chosen to answer those questions rather than to confirm a suspicion of dementia for its own sake.

Delirium is the great confuser of dementia, and the two must be separated because one is often reversible and the other is not. Delirium develops over hours or days, and its hallmark is a fluctuation of attention and awareness, so a patient who is drowsy and inattentive in the morning may be lucid in the afternoon, and an alert patient may be unable to sustain a conversation or count backwards. Dementia develops over months or years, and attention is usually preserved until late, although a person with dementia is far more vulnerable to delirium when illness, pain, constipation, dehydration, or a new medicine arrives. The two can coexist, and a patient with known dementia who suddenly becomes worse should be assumed to have a delirium until proven otherwise, because the treatable cause is usually the new change. The search examines the temperature, the pulse, the pressure, the breathing, the glucose, the urine, the bowel, and every medicine, and a patient whose confusion comes with a new focal deficit, a headache, a fever with neck stiffness, or a recent fall needs urgent help.

Parkinson disease is recognized by a combination of signs rather than by any one of them, and the reasoning is the same whether or not the patient already carries the label. The cardinal features are a tremor most obvious when the limb is at rest and easing when it is used, a stiffness felt as resistance through the whole range of movement, a slowness in starting and sustaining movement, and a loss of balance that appears later. The face loses its expressiveness, the voice becomes quiet, the handwriting shrinks, the gait becomes shuffling with small steps, and the arms swing less on one side, and these signs are often visible before the patient complains. The tremor must be distinguished from an essential tremor, which is more obvious when the arms are held out and often runs in families, and from a tremor produced by a medicine or an overactive thyroid. The finding that would make the diagnosis wrong is a different explanation for the stiffness and slowing, such as a medicine that blocks dopamine, a stroke in the same pathways, or a thyroid disorder.

Neuropathy is disease of the peripheral nerves, and its distribution is the first thing to establish because the distribution names the likely cause. A polyneuropathy affects many nerves, usually beginning in the feet and climbing toward the knees in a length-dependent pattern that produces numbness, burning, and a loss of vibration and position sense, and the ankle reflexes are lost early. A mononeuropathy affects a single nerve, and a common example is compression of the median nerve at the wrist, which causes tingling in the thumb and the first fingers at night. A mononeuritis multiplex affects several separate nerves at once or in sequence, and a multiple mononeuropathy of this kind suggests disease of the blood vessels, poorly controlled diabetes, or an infection. The pattern of sensory loss, whether in a glove, in a stocking, in a patch, or in a band, and the pattern of weakness, whether it climbs, is symmetrical, or follows one nerve, are the findings that direct the search.

The conditions behind a neuropathy are read from the distribution, the time course, and the rest of the patient's story. Diabetes is the commonest cause of a symmetrical, length-dependent polyneuropathy in many parts of the world, and it often begins before the patient knows that the blood glucose is abnormal. Alcohol use, a deficiency of vitamin B twelve or of other vitamins, a low thyroid hormone level, kidney failure, and a cancer not yet known can each produce the same picture, so the clinician asks about diet, alcohol, medicines, and weight loss. In regions where it occurs, leprosy produces a neuropathy with patches of altered sensation and thickened nerves, and infection with the human immunodeficiency virus, known as HIV, may cause a neuropathy directly or through the medicines used for it. A neuropathy that appears suddenly and spreads quickly, or that comes with weakness of the face or difficulty swallowing, is an emergency rather than a chronic problem.

A rapidly progressive weakness is one of the most important presentations in neurology, because the danger is failure of breathing rather than failure of the limbs. When it begins in the feet and climbs over days, with diminishing reflexes and little or no sensory loss, it suggests an acute inflammatory polyneuropathy, sometimes called Guillain-Barré syndrome, in which the immune system attacks the nerves. The breathing must be watched closely, because the weakness can reach the muscles of respiration, and the ability to speak in full sentences, the strength of the cough, and the movement of the chest are repeated observations rather than single measurements. A figure such as 3 days of progression is a reason for urgent assessment rather than a fixed rule, and any interval used to define rapid progression must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol. Other causes include a disorder of the junction between nerve and muscle, in which weakness worsens with use, along with poisons, infections, metabolic disturbances, and disease of the spinal cord.

Compression of the spinal cord is a true emergency, because the cord tolerates pressure badly and lost function may become permanent within hours. The usual cause is a cancer that has spread to the spine, but an infection, an abscess, a prolapsed disc, and an injury can do the same, and the patient is often already known to have one of these conditions. The findings that reveal it are back pain worse at night or on coughing, a band of pain or tightness around the trunk, weakness of both legs, numbness in the saddle area between the legs, and difficulty starting the passage of urine or loss of control of the bladder and bowel. The examination should include tone, power, reflexes, and sensation in both legs, and a search for the level below which sensation is altered, since that level points to the site of the compression. Every patient with a known cancer and new back pain or new weakness of the legs must be treated as though the cord is threatened until the question is answered, and the answer usually requires urgent MRI.

Where the resources exist, the neurological emergency is investigated along a fixed path. The CT scan without contrast is the first image in most acute situations because it is fast and separates bleeding from a blocked vessel, and it also shows a fracture, a collection of blood, and a shift of the brain. The MRI gives more detail of the brainstem, the spinal cord, and the nerves, and it is the investigation of choice when the cord is threatened, though it takes longer and demands a patient who can lie still. The lumbar puncture is used when infection of the coverings of the brain is suspected, or when a subarachnoid hemorrhage remains possible after a negative scan, and it is performed only after the safety questions have been answered. An electroencephalogram records the electrical activity of the brain and helps to classify seizures, though it cannot exclude a seizure that did not occur during the recording.

In a clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, the same reasoning is carried as far as the instruments allow and no further. The stethoscope is used for the heart and the lungs, the torch examines the pupil, the movement of the eyes, the throat, and the skin, and a pupil that is larger than the other and does not react to light, or unequal pupils in a patient whose consciousness is falling, points to pressure inside the head and demands urgency. The thermometer turns the impression of fever into a figure that can be compared with the next reading, and the pulse and the breathing are counted with a watch rather than estimated. The clinician examines the blood pressure, the glucose where a meter exists, and the power, reflexes, and sensation of the limbs, and repeats those observations at intervals the patient's condition allows. The decision that follows is not a diagnosis but a plan: whether to observe, to begin treatment on the clinical picture, or to send the patient now.

The decision about treatment belongs to diagnosis, and in neurology it is often made before the cause is certain. Treatment is the right answer when a stroke is within the window in which the clot might be dissolved or removed and the scan has excluded bleeding, when infection of the coverings of the brain is strongly suspected and delay would cost function, when the cord is compressed and the pressure must be relieved, or when a seizure continues and the airway is at risk. It is the wrong answer when a bleed has not been excluded in a patient who might receive a treatment for a clot, when a single provoked seizure has already stopped and its cause is being corrected, and when the picture fits a condition for which the available treatment would do harm. Naming the finding that would justify or forbid the decision is more useful than naming a drug, because the reasoning travels between settings even when the medicines do not.

A constructed illustration may fix these habits, and it is offered as a teaching device rather than as a portrait of any real person. Imagine a man of about 60 whose wife noticed his speech had become slurred and his right arm weak while they were eating, and the weakness was maximal from the first moment, so the clinician treats it as a possible stroke and arranges imaging at once. Imagine a woman of about 45 who describes a headache that came on like a thunderclap while she lifted a box, and who feels better an hour later and asks to go home, and the clinician who remembers the instantaneous onset keeps her for assessment despite the improvement. Imagine an older man with a known cancer who has new back pain, weakness of both legs, and difficulty passing urine, in whom the saddle numbness is sought deliberately and the spine is imaged urgently. None of these people is real, and each shows how one question changes the order of the plan.

What a patient is told on leaving is part of the diagnosis, and in neurology the advice must be specific because the danger is often a second event. The patient with a transient attack or a minor stroke should be told what to do if the symptoms return, how quickly to seek help, and where to go, and that advice should name the signs in ordinary words, such as a face that droops, an arm that will not lift, or speech that has changed. The patient with a new seizure should be told about the risks of driving, swimming, and working at height, and about the importance of sleep and of avoiding identified triggers. The patient with a headache unlike any previous headache should be told which features would bring her back, and the patient with progressive weakness should have a plan for review rather than a promise to return if things worsen. The record should show what was found, what was considered, what was excluded, and what was arranged.

To summarize, neurology is organized by two questions asked of every patient, where the lesion lies and how quickly it is moving, and the answers are assembled from the history, the examination, and the course over time. The dangerous presentations are named at the bedside and each carries its own revealing finding: a stroke of abrupt onset that may still be within the window for reversal, a subarachnoid hemorrhage disguised as a headache of instantaneous onset, a cord compressed by cancer or infection with a band of pain and a numb saddle, and a weakness that climbs over hours or days while the reflexes disappear. The common imitators, the seizure, the migraine, the low blood glucose, and the delirium, are separated by their timing and their associated features, while epilepsy is read by seizure type, dementia by the pattern of loss, the neuropathies by their distribution, and Parkinson disease by the combination of tremor, rigidity, slowness, and loss of balance. Every figure in this chapter, whether a pressure, a temperature, a rate, a duration, a score, or a laboratory value, is an illustration of the reasoning and must be confirmed against the current guideline and the local protocol, because such numbers differ between countries, editions, and laboratories. The same reasoning serves the hospital and the clinic with a stethoscope, a torch, and a thermometer, where the task is to recognize the patient who must be referred now. This chapter is educational material and it does not replace training, supervision, clinical judgment, or the protocol of your own service, and a patient whose story is alarming deserves to be examined again and discussed rather than reassured and sent away.
